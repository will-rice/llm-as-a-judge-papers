###### Abstract

When an LLM judge filters instruction-tuning data, its scoring format
(binary verdict, 1–5 rating, 0–100 score, or comparison) is rarely
justified or ablated. Holding the candidate pool, judge prompt, decoding
and selection budget fixed, we score 15,406 UltraFeedback candidates
under five formats with three judges, curate, fine-tune 66 models, and
evaluate them with four judges. A 0–100 scale is worth only 7–12 equally
used levels, two to three times a 1–5 scale rather than twenty, and a
binary verdict leaves the top score tied in 77–78% of prompts, so most
selections are made at random. These properties are set by the format
almost independently of the judge. Kendall’s $`\tau_{b}`$ hides them: it
ranks binary best while $`\tau_{a}`$ and top-1 agreement rank it last,
because it discounts the ties a selector must break at random. Both
comparative formats inherit a slot bias whose sign differs across
judges. Downstream, curated data beat random selection under every
evaluation judge, robust to length and markdown adjustment, but no
ordering among formats is detectable. We propose four quantities that
judge-based curation pipelines should report.

## 1 Introduction

A practitioner assembling an instruction-tuning corpus without an
annotation budget typically asks an LLM judge to score every candidate,
keeps the top fraction, and fine-tunes on what survives (Zheng et al.,
2023; Chen et al., 2024; Sachdeva et al., 2026). How to ask for the
judge’s opinion (ACCEPT/REJECT, a 1–5 rating, a 0–100 score, a
comparison) is usually decided once, on grounds of convenience, and
rarely ablated with the selector held fixed. Yet a curation pipeline
consumes scores as an _ordering_ and breaks ties at random, so tie
structure is a first-class property of a format: a binary verdict leaves
every accepted candidate indistinguishable, and the choice at the
frontier is made by chance.

We vary only the format across five designs, score a fixed pool with
three judges, and follow one judge’s scores through curation and LoRA
fine-tuning to 66 models. Our main contributions are as follows: (i) a
fully crossed study of three judges $`\times`$ five formats, showing
that the format, not the judge, determines how many ties the scores
contain, a result that holds when any one judge is left out and when
each format’s number of available levels is taken into account (§3.1);
(ii) evidence that $`\tau_{b}`$ inverts the ordering a selection audit
needs (§3.3); (iii) evidence that both comparative formats inherit a
judge-specific slot bias, measured three ways (§3.4); and
(iv) downstream results under four evaluation judges (§4).

#### Related work.

Elicitation studies measure formats by agreement with human or reference
ratings (Li et al., 2026; Novikova et al., 2018; Zhuang et al., 2024) or
compare pointwise and pairwise protocols for bias (Tripathi et al.,
2025), without following scores into training. Data-selection work does
follow them, but with one elicitation protocol (Chen et al., 2024; Cui
et al., 2023; Pang et al., 2025; Wettig et al., 2024). Deutsch et al.
(2023) establish that ties distort Kendall-style meta-evaluation; we
show the consequence for selection. Configuration beyond the judge model
is a known variance source (Zhang, 2026; Don-Yehiya et al., 2026; Norman
et al., 2026; Soumik, 2026). We add the controlled format ablation,
carried through to trained models.

## 2 Method

#### Pool and reference.

We draw 4,000 UltraFeedback prompts (Cui et al., 2023), yielding 15,406
multi-model responses after a 20–6,000 character filter
($`\bar{k}=3.85`$ per prompt); 300 further prompts are held out.
UltraFeedback’s GPT-4 scores are used as the _reference_. They never
enter a prompt as numbers, the selection decision, or the training data,
but they choose the anchor in the anchored format and the evaluation
reference, so we report agreement with the GPT-4 reference and avoid the
term accuracy.

#### Formats.

The pointwise formats share one preamble (helpfulness, correctness,
completeness, clarity) and differ only in the final instruction; the
comparative formats use a parallel pairwise prompt with five verdicts
mapped to $`v\in\{+2,\dots,-2\}`$ (Appendix A). Binary (ACCEPT/REJECT
$`\to\{0,1\}`$), Likert-5 ($`(v-1)/4`$) and Numeric-100 ($`v/100`$) are
pointwise. Anchored compares the candidate (always Response A) with a
fixed per-prompt anchor, the upper-middle candidate by GPT-4 score
(Don-Yehiya et al., 2026). Tournament compares every pair of candidates
within a prompt once. Each verdict $`v`$ gives Response A a win
probability $`\sigma(v)=1/(1+e^{-v})`$ (0.88 for a strong win, 0.5 for a
tie), and a candidate’s score is its average win probability over its
matches, a soft win rate. Decoding is greedy, with a 16-token verdict
budget and reasoning disabled. Selection uses within-format ranks, which
preserve ties, so each format’s tie structure passes unchanged into
selection.

#### Judges, curation and training.

Three curation judges score the identical pool: deepseek-v4-flash
(DeepSeek-AI et al., 2026), qwen3.5-9b (Yang et al., 2025), and
nemotron-3-nano-30b-a3b; parse rates are $`\geq 0.998`$ in all fifteen
cells. Curation uses DeepSeek scores only. At retention
$`r\in\{10,25,50\}\%`$ we keep the highest-scored response per prompt
(ties broken at random), then the top $`r`$ of these globally. Random
keeps one uniform response per prompt and a uniform fraction $`r`$ of
prompts; Length applies the same policy to response length; Full uses
all candidates. Each subset fine-tunes Qwen3-4B (Yang et al., 2025) with
LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) and three seeds: seven conditions at three rates
plus Full, a total of 66 models.

#### Evaluation.

Each model answers the 300 held-out prompts; each response is compared
with the highest-GPT-4-rated completion in both orders, scoring 1 when
both orders agree on a win, 0 on a loss, and 0.5 otherwise. Four
evaluation judges (deepseek-v4-pro, gemini-3.5-flash-lite, grok-4.20,
Olmo-3-7B) are analyzed separately. They resolve different fractions of
comparisons into a win or loss, and every unresolved comparison scores
0.5, so a judge that resolves fewer pulls all win rates toward 0.5;
levels are therefore comparable within a judge but not across judges.
Per judge and rate we run a Friedman test (Friedman, 1937), paired
seed-clustered bootstrap contrasts (10,000 resamples), and Holm
correction (Holm, 1979), and report how many judges concur.

## 3 Scoring-Stage Results

with one observation per cell, the residual absorbs the interaction.

On structural diagnostics, format explains 97–99% and the judge under
0.5%. This is partly by construction: a $`K`$-level alphabet has tie
probability at least $`1/K`$, and binary sits almost on its floor of
0.50. The informative question is how efficiently each judge uses its
alphabet. Normalized by nominal capacity, $`D_{2}`$ is still 96% format
(log-normalized, 85%): every judge uses binary and Likert-5 at 66–100%
of capacity and numeric-100 at 7–10% (§3.2). The residual is not empty:
tie probability above the $`1/K`$ floor is 54% format and 41% residual,
so how a judge fills an alphabet is judge-and-format specific.

#### Reference agreement depends on the weakest judge.

The judge accounts for 22–50% of reference agreement in the full grid,
but leave-one-judge-out shows this is carried by Nemotron: with DeepSeek
and Qwen alone, format explains 99.0–99.7% and the judge 0.1–0.4%. Given
a format, DeepSeek and Qwen give nearly identical agreement, whereas
Nemotron lowers agreement under every format. The result does not depend
on the two comparative formats, whose implementations carry slot bias
(§3.4): over the three pointwise formats alone, DeepSeek and Qwen give a
format share of 98.7–99.5% for $`\tau_{a}`$, Somers’ $`D`$ and top-1
(93% for global Spearman; Appendix C, Table 7). With three judges we do
not generalize this to a capability law.

### 3.2 Nominal Granularity is Not Effective Granularity

Numeric-100 offers twenty times the levels of Likert-5, but judges
emitted only 33–37 of its 101 values and placed 74.7%, 95.9% and 99.6%
of their mass (Nemotron, Qwen, DeepSeek) on multiples of five. Counting
distinct values overstates resolution, because a value used twice counts
the same as one used nearly 3,800 times. The effective number of equally
used levels, $`D_{\alpha}=\exp(H_{\alpha})`$ for Rényi entropy
$`H_{\alpha}`$, corrects this: numeric-100 is worth 9.1–12.4 levels at
$`\alpha=1`$ and 7.0–9.9 at $`\alpha=2`$, against 3.3–4.4 for Likert-5,
two to three times the effective resolution rather than twenty.
$`D_{2}`$ is exactly the inverse tie probability, so the tie column of
Table 1 already reports it. It is also stable where counts are not: at a
third of the pool, distinct values fall from 37 to $`28.0\pm 2.0`$ while
$`D_{2}`$ moves by about 0.1 s.d. or less (Appendix D, Table 10).

### 3.3 Kendall’s $`\tau_{b}`$ Misleads a Selection Audit

By $`\tau_{b}`$, binary is the best format under DeepSeek and Nemotron
and second under Qwen; by $`\tau_{a}`$ it is last under DeepSeek and
Qwen. $`\tau_{a}`$, Somers’ $`D`$ and top-1 agreement all rank
numeric-100 first, Likert-5 second and binary last under DeepSeek and
Qwen, differing only on anchored versus tournament. $`\tau_{b}`$
(Kendall, 1945) divides by $`\sqrt{(n_{0}-n_{1})(n_{0}-n_{2})}`$, where
$`n_{1}`$ counts judge-tied pairs, so coarse formats are divided by a
smaller number. When the reference orders a pair strictly and the judge
ties it, the selector breaks the tie at random, so the pair’s expected
concordance is zero, which is how $`\tau_{a}`$ scores it and not how
$`\tau_{b}`$ does. For example, with reference order $`a>b>c`$ and a
binary judge accepting $`a,b`$, $`\tau_{a}=2/3`$ and
$`\tau_{b}\approx 0.82`$, yet the selector picks $`a`$ only half the
time. This is the common case here: binary leaves the top score tied in
77–78% of prompts (TT in Table 1), against 40–47% for numeric-100. The
argument extends Deutsch et al. (2023) from meta-evaluation to
selection.

### 3.4 Comparative Formats Inherit Slot Bias

Read each comparative verdict as a margin in favor of Response A. A
genuine preference for the candidate follows the candidate, so its sign
flips when the candidate moves from slot A to slot B; a preference for a
slot does not move. Running 500 anchored candidates per judge in both
orders therefore gives $`v_{\text{fwd}}=q+b`$ and
$`v_{\text{rev}}=-q+b`$, separating the mean preference for the
candidate $`q`$ from the slot-A bias $`b`$ (Table 4, Appendix B). The
bias differs in sign on identical inputs: $`b=-0.33`$ ($`\pm 0.09`$) for
DeepSeek and Qwen, toward slot B, and $`+0.89`$ \[$`0.79`$, $`0.99`$\]
for Nemotron. Self-comparisons confirm it without the additive model:
the anchor is one of the candidates, so 4,000 queries compare a response
with itself, where any verdict other than TIE can only reflect position.
DeepSeek and Qwen return TIE on 95.8% and 99.6% of these, Nemotron on
42.3%, with a mean verdict of $`+0.92`$ toward slot A. Because $`q`$ is
a mean preference rather than discrimination, we also correlate each
item’s $`q_{i}`$ with its GPT-4 gap to the anchor: $`0.44`$ \[$`0.37`$,
$`0.51`$\] for DeepSeek, $`0.37`$ \[$`0.30`$, $`0.44`$\] for Qwen, and
$`0.10`$ \[$`0.01`$, $`0.19`$\] for Nemotron, whose anchored scores are
mostly position.

The tournament inherits the bias through scheduling: pairs were
enumerated in listing order, so the first-listed candidate is always
Response A and the last always Response B. Listing order is unrelated to
quality (Spearman 0.05 with GPT-4 score), yet it drives selection in
each judge’s direction. DeepSeek selects the last-listed response in
44.6% of four-candidate prompts, Nemotron the first-listed in 67.5%,
against 25% without a position effect. The tournament’s low agreement
(global Spearman 0.06–0.30) is therefore at least partly due to slot
bias amplified by unbalanced scheduling.

The omnibus Friedman test detects differences among the training
conditions at every retention rate under every evaluation judge
($`p\leq 0.017`$). Every contrast that replicates across judges is a
curated format beating a baseline (Table 3): three hold under all four
evaluation judges and five more under three. A difference of $`+0.09`$
means winning against the reference on about nine more prompts in 100
than Random. Against Full, trained on all 15,406 candidates, a
400-example curated subset ($`r=10\%`$) is at least as good: 18 of 20
format$`\times`$judge differences are positive (up to $`+0.059`$),
though only 2 survive Holm correction, and Random at 10% stays within
0.02 of Full.

#### Shared judge taste.

Curated students write 1,044–1,257 characters with markdown in 38–46% of
responses, against 1,072–1,113 and 36–40% for Random. Adding log length
relative to the reference and a markdown indicator to a
prompt-fixed-effects regression changes every curated-versus-Random
contrast by less than 0.02 (Appendix C, Table 8), so the gains are not a
length or markdown effect.

#### Different data, similar students.

The formats do not select the same examples. The Jaccard overlap between
two formats’ DeepSeek-curated subsets averages 0.02 at $`r=10\%`$, 0.06
at 25% and 0.15 at 50%, so each format trains on a largely disjoint set.
That such different subsets yield statistically indistinguishable
students suggests that, in this pool, many selections clear the bar that
separates curated from random data, and the large scoring-stage
differences are compressed by training.

#### Between formats.

No format beats another under more than one evaluation judge. Binary
does trail the finer formats in direction, below Likert-5 in all 12
judge$`\times`$rate comparisons and below numeric-100 in 11 of 12,
matching its weaker scoring-stage agreement. These gaps are too small
for the design to resolve: with 300 prompts and three seeds it detects a
difference of about 0.03–0.05 win-rate points with 80% power, and its
95% CIs rule out format differences larger than 0.064–0.113, depending
on the judge. The missing ordering should therefore be read as
undetected; it is not evidence that the formats are equivalent.

## 5 Conclusion

Under a fixed decoding rule, the format sets a judge’s tie structure
almost independently of the judge, and tie structure decides how often
selection is random; comparative formats also import a judge-specific
slot bias, and the format alone largely changes which examples are
selected. We propose reporting the following: (1) the format verbatim,
with the decoding rule; (2) tie probability (or $`D_{2}`$), with any
distinct-value count given alongside the pool size; (3) for comparative
formats, how positions were swapped or balanced, and the estimated
$`b`$; and (4) the verdict parse rate. In practice, we recommend
Likert-5 or numeric-100 over a binary verdict, swap and balance slots in
comparative formats, and validate formats with $`\tau_{a}`$, Somers’
$`D`$ or top-1 agreement rather than $`\tau_{b}`$.

## Limitations

Decoding. All verdicts use greedy decoding. Probability-weighted scores
(e.g., the expected Likert value under the verdict-token distribution,
as in G-Eval; Liu et al., 2023) or repeated sampling would make binary
and Likert-5 effectively continuous. Our structural results describe
format together with greedy decoding, not format alone. Variance
decomposition. There is one observation per cell, so the residual
absorbs the format$`\times`$judge interaction; three judges give the
judge factor two degrees of freedom, and the judge share in reference
agreement is driven by one judge (Table 2). Reference. Agreement is
measured against UltraFeedback’s GPT-4 scores, not human labels. The
same scores choose the anchor shown to the judge in the anchored format
and the evaluation reference. Downstream design. Training data come from
one curation judge (DeepSeek), so we do not test whether a less accurate
curation judge yields worse students; one Nemotron-curated condition
would. We have no untuned student or GPT-4-oracle selector baseline, so
we cannot say whether better reference agreement yields better training
data. Our length and markdown adjustment conditions on properties of the
trained model’s own outputs, so it is a sensitivity check rather than a
causal control. The Length baseline is confounded with truncation (the
1,024-token cap removes 11.4–25.2% of its examples against 1.8–6.2%
elsewhere). Format-specific confounds. Binary’s disadvantage is
inseparable from its random tie-breaking. Anchored is intentionally
unswapped, and 26% of its queries compare the anchor with itself, which
assigns every anchor the same score. The tournament’s slot assignment
follows listing order, so its selections, including the DeepSeek-curated
training data, carry each judge’s slot bias; its soft win rate is
computed within small slates ($`\bar{k}=3.85`$), so a weak slate’s
winner can score as high as a strong slate’s. Scope. We only use one
candidate pool, one student, and one language.

## Appendix A Method Details

#### Prompts.

Pointwise formats:

> You are evaluating the quality of a response to an instruction.
> Consider helpfulness, correctness, completeness, and clarity.  
> Instruction: {instruction}  
> Response: {response}  
> {format line}

with format lines Binary: “Is this response of acceptable quality? Reply
with exactly one word: ACCEPT or REJECT.” Likert-5: “Rate the response
on a 1-5 scale (1=very poor, 2=poor, 3=acceptable, 4=good, 5=excellent).
Reply with only the number.” Numeric-100: “Rate the response on a 0-100
scale, where 0 is unusable and 100 is flawless. Reply with only the
number.” Comparative formats use “You are comparing two responses to the
same instruction. Consider helpfulness, correctness, completeness, and
clarity.” followed by the instruction, Response A, Response B, and
“Which response is better? Reply with exactly one of: A++ (A much
better), A+ (A slightly better), TIE, B+ (B slightly better), B++ (B
much better).” Verdicts map to $`+2,+1,0,-1,-2`$.

#### Anchored.

The anchor is the candidate at index $`\lfloor k/2\rfloor`$ after
sorting the prompt’s candidates by GPT-4 score (mean anchor score 6.93
against 6.45 for all candidates, so $`q<0`$ is expected); the candidate
is always Response A, and the score is $`(v+2)/4`$. Because the anchor
is drawn from the candidates, 4,000 of the 15,406 queries compare the
anchor with itself.

#### Tournament.

All $`\binom{k}{2}\leq 6`$ within-prompt pairs are judged once, in the
order produced by enumerating the prompt’s candidates in listing order,
so candidate $`i`$ is Response A against every later candidate. A
verdict $`v`$ gives the first response win probability $`\sigma(v)`$ and
the second $`1-\sigma(v)`$; a candidate’s score is its mean win
probability over its matches.

#### Fine-tuning.

Each model is trained with LoRA (rank 16, $`\alpha=32`$, dropout 0.05)
for 2 epochs, using AdamW with learning rate $`2\times 10^{-4}`$, an
effective batch size of 8 and bf16 precision. Training sequences are
capped at 1,024 tokens. The cap truncates 4.3% of the full pool; across
curated subsets it ranges from 1.8% (numeric-100) to 6.2% (tournament),
but reaches 11.4–25.2% for Length, which selects the longest responses
by design (see Limitations). All training ran on a single NVIDIA RTX 5090. Curation and evaluation judges were queried through hosted APIs,
except Olmo-3-7B-Instruct, which we ran locally with vLLM.

#### Statistics.

Every format is evaluated on the same 300 prompts, a repeated-measures
design, so we use the Friedman test per judge and rate, paired bootstrap
contrasts resampling both prompts and seeds, and Holm correction across
format pairs within each rate.

## Appendix B Anchored Order-Swap Decomposition

Verdicts map to a slot-A-oriented margin: “A much better” $`\to+2`$, “A
slightly better” $`\to+1`$, tie $`\to 0`$, “B slightly better”
$`\to-1`$, “B much better” $`\to-2`$. The candidate’s preference travels
with the candidate, so its sign flips when the candidate moves to slot
B; a position preference stays with the slot. Hence
$`v_{\text{fwd}}=q+b`$, $`v_{\text{rev}}=-q+b`$,
$`q=(v_{\text{fwd}}-v_{\text{rev}})/2`$,
$`b=(v_{\text{fwd}}+v_{\text{rev}})/2`$. The additive model treats the
five verdicts as an interval scale and can be distorted near the clipped
endpoints. The reverse pass uses a seeded sample of 500 candidates per
judge, excluding self-comparisons; 95% CIs below are from 10,000
bootstrap resamples of items.

## Appendix D Effective Granularity

For a score distribution $`p`$, the Rényi entropy of order $`\alpha`$ is
$`H_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\sum_{i}p_{i}^{\alpha}`$ and
$`D_{\alpha}=\exp(H_{\alpha})`$ is the number of equally used levels
with the same entropy. $`D_{1}`$ is the exponentiated Shannon entropy;
$`D_{2}=1/\sum_{i}p_{i}^{2}`$ is the inverse tie probability, and
$`D_{2}\leq D_{1}`$. A distinct-value count weights a score emitted
twice the same as one emitted 3,791 times: DeepSeek’s four most frequent
numeric-100 scores (85, 75, 45, 65) carry 70.2% of the mass. Figure 1
shows the numeric-100 distributions, and Table 9 lists nominal, emitted
and effective levels for every pointwise format.
