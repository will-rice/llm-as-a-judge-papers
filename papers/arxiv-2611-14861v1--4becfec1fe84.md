###### Abstract

Generative large language models are widely used for multilingual text
evaluation, yet whether decision models that directly output judgment
probabilities can serve as multilingual judges has received limited
attention. We evaluate Jev against generative LLM judges on four
multilingual benchmarks with human annotations, covering up to 47
language varieties and three evaluation formats: binary decisions,
pairwise comparisons, and graded ratings. At approximately $`1/31`$ to
$`1/61`$ of GPT-6.1’s evaluation cost, Jev is competitive on most
multilingual judging tasks, but falls behind GPT-6.1 on
knowledge-intensive answer verification. Further analysis shows that Jev
is more likely to accept incorrect answers in low-resource languages
than in English and that its probability outputs help identify errors
across tasks. Based on these findings, we offer recommendations on
evaluation instructions, confidence-based selection, and model routing,
providing empirical guidance for using Jev as a multilingual judge.

## 1 Introduction

Generative large language models are widely used as judges to evaluate
model outputs, drawing on their strong language understanding and
reasoning capabilities (Zheng et al., 2023). Many evaluation tasks,
however, require only binary decisions, pairwise preferences, or graded
ratings, rather than free-form text. For these tasks, decision models
such as Jev (TypeSafe AI, 2026) offer an alternative by directly
predicting probabilities over possible judgments. Evaluations on English
benchmarks have shown that Jev can achieve performance comparable to
generative judges on some evaluation tasks at substantially lower cost,
highlighting its potential as an automated judge (Li et al., 2026b; Rao
and Callison-Burch, 2026).

As large language models are increasingly used across languages,
automated judges must also support multilingual evaluation. However,
existing evaluations of Jev as a judge have focused primarily on English
(Li et al., 2026b; Rao and Callison-Burch, 2026), while multilingual
evaluations have examined its reading comprehension and
question-answering abilities (Deußer et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a).
Its ability to evaluate model outputs across languages has yet to be
systematically studied. It therefore remains unclear how reliably Jev
can serve as a multilingual judge across languages and evaluation tasks.

To fill this gap, we systematically evaluate Jev against generative LLM
judges on four multilingual benchmarks: MEMERAG for RAG faithfulness
(Cruz Blandón et al., 2025), Global-MMLU-Lite for answer verification
(Singh et al., 2025), MENLO for response quality (Whitehouse et al.,
2026), and METAL for summary quality (Hada et al., 2024). These
benchmarks cover up to 47 language varieties and three evaluation
formats: binary decisions, pairwise comparisons, and graded ratings. We
assess judgment quality against human annotations and use parallel data
to examine whether judgments remain consistent across languages.

Our results show that Jev is competitive on most multilingual judging
tasks at approximately $`1/31`$ to $`1/61`$ of GPT-6.1’s evaluation
cost, but falls behind GPT-6.1 on knowledge-intensive answer
verification. On parallel data, Jev’s judgments are more consistent
across languages than Qwen’s, but less consistent than GPT-6.1’s,
particularly in low-resource languages. Further analysis shows that the
gap in low-resource languages is primarily driven by Jev accepting
incorrect answers that it correctly rejects in English.

Beyond judgment quality, we examine whether Jev’s probability outputs
can help identify potentially incorrect judgments. We find that the
confidence assigned to a judgment helps distinguish correct from
incorrect decisions, although this distinction is clearer in binary
verification than in graded ratings. We further investigate how these
findings can inform the practical use of Jev. Our results support 1)
using English evaluation instructions by default; 2) retaining only
high-confidence judgments when accuracy takes priority over coverage;
and 3) selectively routing low-confidence samples to models that perform
better on the target task for additional performance gains, although
these gains are modest on most tasks.

Our contributions are threefold:

- •
  We systematically compare Jev with generative LLM judges on four
  multilingual benchmarks covering up to 47 language varieties and three
  evaluation formats.
- •
  We analyze the judgment changes underlying cross-lingual inconsistency
  and assess whether Jev’s decision probabilities help distinguish
  correct from incorrect judgments.
- •
  We provide practical recommendations for using Jev through experiments
  on instruction language, confidence-based selection, and model
  routing.

## 2 Related Work

#### Multilingual LLM Judges.

Generative LLMs are widely used as automated judges (Zheng et al.,
2023), with evaluation increasingly extending to multilingual settings.
Benchmarks such as METAL (Hada et al., 2024), MM-Eval (Son et al.,
2025), M-RewardBench (Gureja et al., 2025), and MENLO (Whitehouse et
al., 2026) assess agreement between model judgments and human
annotations across tasks and languages. Prior studies report variation
in judge performance across languages (Son et al., 2025), more lenient
ratings in lower-resource languages (Zhou et al., 2026), and
inconsistent judgments of the same content across languages (Fu and Liu,
2025). Pairwise judgments are also sensitive to candidate presentation
order (Zheng et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024). Other work explores
dedicated training to improve multilingual judging (Pombal et al., 2025;
Doddapaneni et al., 2025). We examine the capabilities and behavior of
decision models that directly output judgment probabilities in
multilingual evaluation.

#### Decision Models as Judges.

Existing evaluations of Jev (TypeSafe AI, 2026) fall into two broad
categories. The first examines its general decision-making capabilities.
Deußer et al. (2026) evaluate Jev across classification, moderation,
rubric scoring, and other tasks, while Li et al. (2026a) examine its
knowledge, reasoning, and multilingual understanding. The second
investigates Jev’s ability to evaluate other models’ outputs. Li et al.
(2026b) study judgment quality and confidence-based escalation, while
Rao and Callison-Burch (2026) compare decision models with generative
judges under shared evaluation criteria. These studies report
competitive judging performance at lower cost in several settings, but
focus primarily on English, leaving Jev’s multilingual applicability
unclear. We extend this line of work by evaluating Jev across languages
and judgment formats, examining both judgment quality and cross-lingual
consistency.

#### Probability Reliability and Judge Cascades.

The reliability of probability estimates concerns both their
correspondence with observed correctness and their ability to identify
likely errors. Prior work examines calibration differences across
languages (Ahuja et al., 2022), while research on model cascades uses
confidence to refer uncertain judgments to other models, balancing
quality and cost (Chen et al., 2024; Jung et al., 2025). Studies of Jev
find this strategy effective in some English evaluations (Li et al.,
2026b), but report limited gains when judges share many of the same
errors (Rao and Callison-Burch, 2026). We examine the reliability of
Jev’s probability outputs in multilingual evaluation and evaluate
confidence-based routing to other judges.

## 3 Experimental Setup

### 3.1 Benchmarks

We evaluate Jev on four multilingual benchmarks covering answer
verification, RAG faithfulness, response quality, and summary quality.
These benchmarks support three evaluation formats—binary decisions,
pairwise comparisons, and graded ratings—as summarized in Table 1.

#### MEMERAG.

We evaluate the faithfulness of model responses to retrieved passages at
the sentence level (Cruz Blandón et al., 2025). For each judgment, the
judge receives a sentence from the response, together with the full
response and retrieved passages, and determines whether that sentence is
grounded in the retrieved evidence and relevant to the question. We
evaluate these judgments against reference labels for 2,034 sentences
across five languages.

#### Global-MMLU-Lite.

We use multilingual multiple-choice questions (Singh et al., 2025) to
evaluate whether judges can distinguish correct from incorrect answers.
Specifically, we construct two verification samples from each question
by pairing it with either the correct answer or a randomly selected
distractor. The judge sees the full question, including all four
options, and determines whether the paired answer is correct. We use the
same correct and distractor options across the dataset’s language
versions, allowing us to examine whether judgments remain consistent
across languages.

#### MENLO.

We use MENLO’s human ratings of response quality across 47 language
varieties (Whitehouse et al., 2026) to construct two evaluation tasks:
graded rating and pairwise comparison. Specifically, the graded-rating
task asks the judge to assign a response one of five levels according to
the dataset’s evaluation criteria, using the human rating as the
reference. For pairwise comparison, the judge selects the better of two
responses to the same prompt. To construct these pairs, we exclude pairs
with equal human ratings and treat the higher-rated response in each
pair as preferred.

#### METAL.

We evaluate summary quality in ten languages through hallucination
detection and graded rating (Hada et al., 2024). Specifically, the judge
receives a summary and its source article and determines whether the
summary contains hallucinations. It also assigns separate three-level
ratings for linguistic acceptability, output content quality, and task
quality. We evaluate these judgments against reference labels aggregated
from three human annotators.

Further preprocessing and sample-selection details are provided in
Appendix B.

### 3.2 Judges and Evaluation Protocol

We compare Jev (jev-1.13.0) with two generative LLM judges, Qwen3.8-27B
(Qwen) and GPT-6.1. All judges receive the same task instructions,
evaluation criteria, and content. Instructions are written in English
unless otherwise specified, while the evaluated content remains in its
original language.

Jev directly outputs decision probabilities, whereas the generative
judges are instructed to return only the requested judgment without an
explanation. We disable thinking for Qwen and use low reasoning effort
for GPT-6.1. Although both generative judges return discrete judgments,
we additionally derive answer probabilities from Qwen’s output-token
probabilities.

For binary verification and graded rating, GPT-6.1’s judgments are taken
directly from its discrete outputs. For Jev and Qwen, we obtain binary
judgments using a default acceptance threshold of 0.5 and graded ratings
by selecting the most probable level. For pairwise comparison, we
evaluate each response pair in both presentation orders. We align Jev’s
and Qwen’s predictions to the same candidate and average their
probabilities to determine the final preference. For GPT-6.1, matching
choices across the two orders yield a preference, while conflicting
choices are treated as a tie.

Prompt templates are provided in Appendix A, and further inference and
output-processing details are given in Appendix C.

### 3.3 Evaluation Metrics

We evaluate judges in terms of judgment quality and consistency.
Judgment quality measures agreement with human annotations or reference
answers. For binary tasks, we report balanced accuracy (BAcc), defined
as the mean of positive- and negative-class recall, and AUROC where
answer probabilities are available. For pairwise comparisons, we report
preference accuracy, assigning 1 to a correct preference, 0 to an
incorrect preference, and 0.5 to a tied prediction. For graded ratings,
we report macro-F1 and Spearman correlation with human ratings.

Judgment consistency measures whether judgments remain unchanged when
the same content is presented in different languages or orders. For
cross-lingual consistency, we report agreement with English on
Global-MMLU-Lite, defined as the proportion of candidate answers
receiving the same judgment in English and a target language. We also
report Fleiss’ $`\kappa`$ to measure agreement across all 23 language
versions. For order consistency, we evaluate each pair of candidate
responses in MENLO in both presentation orders, A–B and B–A, and
calculate the proportion of pairs for which the judge selects the same
response in both orders.

Details of metric aggregation are provided in Appendix E.

We next examine Jev’s judgment quality. Tables 3–5 compare its
performance with the generative judges across binary decisions, pairwise
preferences, and graded ratings. At approximately $`1/31`$–$`1/61`$ of
GPT-6.1’s evaluation cost, Jev performs competitively on several tasks,
achieving 74.6% accuracy on MENLO preference judgments, close to Qwen
and 2.6 percentage points above GPT-6.1. It also achieves the highest
balanced accuracy on METAL hallucination detection at 85.9% and the
highest macro-F1 on MENLO ratings at 33.3%.

However, this competitiveness does not extend uniformly across tasks or
metrics. Jev trails both generative judges in balanced accuracy on
MEMERAG and falls 6.5 percentage points below GPT-6.1 on
Global-MMLU-Lite answer verification. Moreover, Jev’s higher macro-F1 on
MENLO does not translate into stronger rating correlation. Its Spearman
correlation is similar to the baselines on MENLO and lower on METAL.
These results indicate that Jev’s competitive performance depends on the
evaluation task and does not consistently extend across rating metrics.

#### Cross-lingual consistency.

We compare judges’ consistency across languages using parallel data from
Global-MMLU-Lite, measuring agreement as the proportion of candidate
answers receiving the same judgment in English and a target language. As
shown in Table 6, Jev achieves an average agreement of 93.4% across all
22 non-English languages, higher than Qwen’s 85.7% but lower than
GPT-6.1’s 97.8%. We further break down agreement by language and present
the results in Figure 1. Jev maintains high agreement in high- and
mid-resource languages, with relatively small gaps to GPT-6.1. However,
in most low-resource languages, Jev shows lower agreement and a larger
gap to GPT-6.1, although it still outperforms Qwen. For example, Jev’s
agreement with English falls to 77.5% in Yoruba. This decline is not
uniform across low-resource languages, as Albanian maintains an
agreement rate of 96.8%.

In addition to agreement with English, we use Fleiss’ $`\kappa`$ to
compare the three judges’ overall consistency across all 23 language
versions. Table 6 shows that Jev achieves a higher $`\kappa`$ value than
Qwen but a lower value than GPT-6.1, consistent with the agreement
results.

#### Order consistency.

We compare judges’ consistency on MENLO after swapping the positions of
the candidate responses, reporting the proportion of pairs for which the
same response is selected in both orders (agreement rate). As shown in
Table 6, Jev achieves an agreement rate of 93.2%, close to GPT-6.1 and
substantially higher than Qwen. These results indicate that Jev’s
pairwise preference judgments are relatively insensitive to candidate
positions, with stability close to that of GPT-6.1.

Overall, Jev offers competitive multilingual judging performance at
substantially lower cost, although its relative performance varies
across tasks. While its order consistency is close to GPT-6.1’s, the gap
in cross-lingual consistency is small in high- and mid-resource
languages but larger in most low-resource languages.

## 5 Inconsistency Analysis

Figure 3: A Global-MMLU-Lite example of judgment changes from English to

Section 4.3 shows that Jev’s cross-lingual consistency falls between
Qwen’s and GPT-6.1’s, with a larger gap to GPT-6.1 in low-resource
languages. However, disagreement with English judgments can reflect
either newly introduced errors or corrections of errors made in English.
To examine which changes account for the consistency gaps, we analyze
all three models’ judgments on the full Global-MMLU-Lite evaluation set.
Specifically, we decompose judgment changes from English to the target
language into four categories: (1) false acceptances, where incorrect
answers change from rejected to accepted; (2) false rejections, where
correct answers change from accepted to rejected; (3) corrected false
acceptances, where incorrect answers change from accepted to rejected;
and (4) corrected false rejections, where correct answers change from
rejected to accepted. For each language, we divide each category’s count
by the total number of candidate answers, then average these rates
equally across non-English languages within each resource group defined
in Section 4.3. Figure 2 shows the results. See Appendix G.1 for
calculation details.

Most of Jev’s disagreement with English comes from newly introduced
errors rather than corrections of English errors. This pattern is
particularly pronounced in low-resource languages, where 10.5% of
judgments change from correct to incorrect, while only 1.4% change from
incorrect to correct. Among the newly introduced errors, false
acceptances account for the larger share. Their contribution rises from
1.88 and 2.22 percentage points in high- and mid-resource languages to
8.25 points in low-resource languages, compared with only 1.03 points
for GPT-6.1 in the low-resource group. This difference accounts for
about 78% of the disagreement gap between the two models in that group,
indicating that Jev’s lower consistency primarily reflects its
acceptance of incorrect answers that it correctly rejects in English. By
contrast, Qwen’s disagreement with English is mainly driven by newly
introduced false rejections rather than false acceptances across all
three resource groups.

Figure 3 illustrates these contrasting error patterns on a
Global-MMLU-Lite question. All three models correctly reject candidate A
and accept candidate D in English. In Welsh, however, Jev incorrectly
accepts A and Qwen incorrectly rejects D, while GPT-6.1 correctly
rejects A and accepts D.

## 6 Confidence-Based Error Detection

Figure 4: How well Jev’s confidence distinguishes correct from incorrect
judgments, measured by AUROC. Colors distinguish judgment types, and the
dashed line marks chance level (0.5).

Jev outputs not only a judgment but also the probabilities associated
with its decisions. We ask whether these probabilities provide useful
information about judgment reliability. To examine this, we take the
probability assigned to the final judgment as its confidence and test
whether correct judgments receive higher confidence than incorrect ones.
Specifically, for binary verification and pairwise comparison, we use
the probability assigned to the selected decision as confidence, while
for graded rating, confidence is defined as the probability assigned to
the selected score level. We compute AUROC using judgment correctness as
the binary label and confidence as the score, with correct judgments as
the positive class. An AUROC of 0.5 indicates chance-level
discrimination, while higher values indicate better discrimination.
Figure 4 shows the results across three judgment types. See Appendix G.2
for more details.

The AUROC results are above 0.5 across all five tasks, indicating that
Jev’s probability outputs provide additional information for identifying
errors, although their usefulness varies across judgment types. For
binary verification, AUROC reaches 0.839 on Global-MMLU-Lite and 0.740
on MEMERAG, showing that correct judgments tend to receive higher
confidence. Confidence also distinguishes correct from incorrect choices
in MENLO pairwise comparison to some extent, with an AUROC of 0.682. By
contrast, AUROC is closer to chance level for graded rating, at 0.599 on
MENLO and 0.550 on METAL. These results indicate that Jev’s probability
outputs help identify errors in binary verification, while providing a
weaker reliability signal for graded rating.

Multilingual judging requires evaluating content in different languages,
but it is unclear whether evaluation instructions also need to match the
language of that content. We compare English and target-language
instructions across 22 non-English languages in Global-MMLU-Lite and 8
languages in MENLO, keeping the evaluated content and evaluation
standards unchanged. As shown in Table 7, translating the instructions
changes BAcc from 89.39% to 89.21% for answer verification and
preference accuracy from 67.42% to 67.02% for pairwise comparison. For
graded rating, macro-F1 increases slightly, but correlation with human
ratings does not improve (see Appendix H.1). Overall, translation
provides no clear performance gains, while increasing mean input token
counts by 13.5%, 27.1%, and 38.6% for the three judgment types,
respectively. We therefore recommend using English evaluation
instructions by default for multilingual judging, without translating
them into every target language.

### 7.2 Confidence-Based Selection

Section 6 shows that Jev’s confidence helps distinguish correct from
incorrect judgments. To assess how this signal can be used when higher
accuracy is required, we compare the accuracy of judgments retained at
different confidence thresholds on Global-MMLU-Lite answer verification,
MENLO pairwise comparison, and MENLO response rating. Table 8 shows that
higher thresholds improve accuracy on all three tasks, but both the
gains and the proportion of samples retained vary across tasks. Raising
the threshold from 0.7 to 0.95 improves accuracy from 94.90% to 99.18%
for answer verification and from 78.66% to 86.69% for pairwise
comparison, while still retaining 28.00% and 38.24% of samples,
respectively. In contrast, response rating achieves a larger accuracy
gain, from 46.89% to 69.27%, but retains only 1.72% of samples. We
therefore recommend raising the confidence threshold when accuracy takes
priority over coverage. When both matter, users should choose a
threshold that maximizes coverage while meeting their target accuracy,
particularly for rating tasks.

### 7.3 Model Routing

Section 6 shows that Jev’s confidence helps distinguish correct from
incorrect judgments. We therefore examine whether routing low-confidence
samples to another model improves overall performance. Given GPT’s
stronger overall performance in Section 4, we primarily consider routing
to GPT and include Qwen for comparison. To determine which samples to
route, we select a confidence threshold on the development set such that
approximately 10% of samples fall below it. We then apply this threshold
to the test set, using the selected model’s judgments for samples below
the threshold and retaining Jev’s judgments for the rest. For
comparison, we randomly route the same number of samples within each
test fold.

Table 9 shows that routing low-confidence samples improves performance
to different degrees across the three tasks. On Global-MMLU-Lite,
routing to GPT increases performance from 89.57% to 92.73%, while
routing to Qwen yields a smaller gain. On MENLO pairwise comparison and
METAL summary rating, Qwen is the better receiving model, but the gains
over Jev alone are only 0.40 and 0.68 percentage points, respectively.
Confidence-based routing also outperforms random routing for both
receiving models on all three tasks. The advantage is largest on
Global-MMLU-Lite, where routing to GPT achieves 92.73%, compared with
90.22% under random selection. These results show that confidence helps
select samples for routing, although the gains over Jev alone can be
small. We therefore recommend using a development set to select the
confidence threshold and the model that receives the routed samples when
the budget allows, then applying the selected configuration to new
samples. Under a limited budget, we recommend using Jev alone, given the
small gains on most tasks.

## 8 Conclusion

We systematically evaluated Jev as a multilingual judge across four
benchmarks. Jev achieves competitive performance on most tasks at
substantially lower evaluation cost than GPT-6.1, and its probability
outputs help identify incorrect judgments. Building on our analysis of
its capabilities and limitations, we further evaluated practical
configurations and developed recommendations on evaluation instructions,
confidence-based selection, and selective routing.

## Appendix B Data Processing

#### MEMERAG.

We evaluate individual sentences with the full response and retrieved
passages as context. Support probability is the product of grounding and
relevance probabilities. Jev evaluates both in one request; generative
judges use separate requests. Sentences with indeterminate reference
labels are excluded, leaving 2,034 test sentences shared across judges.
We reserve 50 labeled sentences per language for development.

#### Global-MMLU-Lite.

Each question yields two independent judgments, one for the correct
answer and one for a randomly selected distractor shared across
languages. We exclude questions with inconsistent answer labels, missing
or corrupted content, duplicate options, or unavailable model outputs,
applying exclusions across all languages and judges. The aligned test
set contains 311 questions in 23 languages, separate from 47 development
questions.

#### MENLO.

Reference preferences are derived from individual human ratings;
equal-rated pairs are excluded from pairwise evaluation. We reserve two
unequal-rated pairs per language variety for development and exclude one
pair exceeding a judge’s context limit. The evaluation contains 5,166
preference pairs, each judged in both orders, and 12,658 individually
rated responses across 47 varieties. We adapt the dataset instructions
to each judgment format and remove cross-level references from Jev’s
rating criteria. Localized-factuality rubrics missing the lowest rating
level are completed using the dataset’s corresponding five-level rubric.

#### METAL.

Summaries are matched to their source articles. Reference labels use the
majority of three annotators, or the middle rating when all three
differ. We reserve two summaries per language for development and
exclude summaries with unavailable model outputs for all judges and
dimensions, leaving 968 test summaries.

## Appendix C Inference Details

Jev receives task instructions, evaluation criteria and inputs through
its structured API and returns decision probabilities. Generative judges
receive the corresponding text prompts and output discrete judgments,
with thinking disabled for Qwen and low reasoning effort for GPT-6.1.

Qwen3.8-27B uses temperature 0; answer probabilities are obtained by
renormalizing the top 20 first-token probabilities over valid answers.
GPT-6.1 allows up to 8,000 output tokens, including internal reasoning;
we use its discrete judgments without token probabilities.

## Appendix E Metric Aggregation

#### Binary verification.

BAcc is the mean of positive- and negative-class recall within each
language. BAcc and answer-label AUROC are then averaged equally across
languages. Resource-group results use the same averaging within each
group. For METAL hallucination detection, the positive label denotes a
summary without hallucinations.

#### Pairwise comparison.

We map predictions from both presentation orders to the same candidate
and average their probabilities. A pair receives credit 1 for selecting
the higher-rated response, 0 for the lower-rated response, and 0.5 for a
tie. GPT-6.1’s discrete choices are encoded as probabilities of 0 or 1.
Accuracy is calculated within each language variety and then averaged
equally across varieties. Order agreement compares the choices from the
two presentation orders after mapping them to the same candidates, using
probability-based choices for Jev and Qwen and discrete choices for
GPT-6.1. Agreement is calculated within each language variety and
averaged equally across the 47 varieties.

#### Graded rating.

MENLO macro-F1 and Spearman correlation pool rated responses across
languages and evaluation dimensions. For METAL, each metric pools
languages within a dimension and is then averaged equally across the
three dimensions. Results use discrete predicted ratings.

## Appendix G Analysis Details

### G.1 Cross-lingual judgment transitions

Candidate judgments are matched to English by question ID and option.
Each transition rate uses all candidates as its denominator, regardless
of correctness in English. Rates are averaged equally across languages
within each resource group, excluding English. The high-resource group
contains ar, de, es, fr, hi, it, ja, pt and zh; the mid-resource group
contains bn, cs, hu, id, ko and sk; the low-resource group contains cy,
my, or, sq, sw, tg and yo. Overall rates weight these group means by 9,
6 and 7 languages, respectively.

The false-acceptance contribution to the Jev–GPT consistency gap is the
difference in their false-acceptance rates divided by the difference in
their total disagreement rates. Using unrounded values gives 78.3% in
the low-resource group.

### G.2 Confidence-based error identification

For binary verification, confidence is $`c=\max(p,1-p)`$, where $`p`$ is
the acceptance probability. MENLO preferences use the same formula after
mapping both presentation orders to the original candidate A and
averaging their probabilities. We exclude 19 pairs with $`p=0.5`$,
leaving 5,147 pairs. For ratings, confidence is the probability of the
selected level, and correctness requires an exact match with the
reference rating.

Confidence AUROC pools all languages, including English, and all
evaluation dimensions within each task. Each METAL summary–dimension
pair counts as one judgment.

### H.3 Development-set routing thresholds

We use five-fold content-disjoint evaluation, with four folds for
development and one for testing. Groups are question IDs across
languages and candidates for Global-MMLU-Lite, questions within language
for MEMERAG, prompts for MENLO, and source articles for METAL.

The threshold is the development-set 10th percentile of confidence,
applied unchanged to each test fold. Judgments below it are replaced by
GPT or Qwen judgments in separate experiments. For the random-routing
baseline, we sample without replacement once within each test fold,
matching the exact number of low-confidence samples routed. We use seed
87123 and the same selected samples for both receiving models.
Confidence ties are not split, so actual routing proportions differ
slightly from 10%. Results aggregate held-out predictions using the main
evaluation metrics. Routing includes all 5,166 MENLO preference pairs,
with half credit for tied decisions. Table 17 gives the thresholds and
actual test routing proportions.
