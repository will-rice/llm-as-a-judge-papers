# Agreement Without Alignment: Label-Free Rubric Clarification Increases LLM-Judge Consensus but Not Agreement with Humans

Sangdo Han Affiliation: CEO Business School    Moonsoo Kim
Affiliation: {sangdohan, mskim}@ceobizschool.kr

###### Abstract

LLM judges are increasingly deployed as panels, and disagreement among
panel members is often read as a symptom of an unclear rubric. We test a
label-free procedure that turns this disagreement into rubric repairs.
The procedure decomposes each rubric into sub-criteria, ranks them by
cross-judge disagreement on a development split, and has an editor model
rewrite only the most contested ones. An additional stage that moves
text-checkable sub-criteria to deterministic checkers transferred none
in our evaluation. Human labels are never used to select or edit
clauses; they are used only to evaluate the outcome. We pre-specified
four hypotheses in a version-controlled protocol and evaluated six
rubric conditions with a panel of five judges from three providers on
SummEval, the HealthBench meta-evaluation, and USR Topical-Chat, for a
total of 129,770 judge requests, excluding retries. On SummEval, the
procedure raised inter-judge Krippendorff’s $`\alpha`$ by 0.069 (95% CI
0.046 to 0.092) over the original rubric and by 0.074 over a control
that applied the same edits to an equal number of randomly chosen
clauses that were not selected. Agreement with expert ratings showed no
detectable change (Kendall $`\tau`$ difference -0.007, 95% CI -0.037 to
0.021). On HealthBench and USR, neither inter-judge agreement nor human
agreement met the preregistered effect criteria. In a preregistered
secondary comparison, human agreement after editing the contested
clauses was lower than after editing the control clauses on all three
datasets in point estimate, although no interval excluded zero.
Selecting clauses by disagreement makes judges agree with each other
more than editing arbitrary clauses does, but in our setting the added
consensus did not bring the judges closer to human annotators. We will
release code, frozen rubrics, item identifiers, and judge scores.

## 1 Introduction

Rubric-based LLM judges now score a large share of open-ended model
output, from summaries to medical conversations (Liu et al., 2023; Arora
et al., 2025). Because a single judge can be idiosyncratic,
practitioners increasingly combine several judges, often from different
providers, into a panel. When the panel disagrees, the disagreement is
usually averaged away. A second reading is that disagreement marks the
places where the rubric admits more than one interpretation, and that
those places can be found and fixed.

If that reading is right, it suggests an attractive workflow: locate the
contested clauses using only judge outputs, clarify them, and obtain a
more reliable rubric without collecting new human labels. Three findings
from prior work complicate this picture. First, almost any rubric edit
that adds examples or context can raise agreement (Huynh et al., 2026),
so an improvement after targeted edits does not show that the targeting
mattered. Second, LLM-written elaborations of rubric text can lower
agreement with humans (Sharma et al., 2025). Third, agreement among LLM
judges is not evidence of agreement with humans, because judges can
converge on a shared axis that annotators do not weight (Mukherjee
et al., 2026; Song et al., 2026).

We ask two questions. Does selecting clauses by cross-judge disagreement
improve inter-judge agreement more than editing other clauses in the
same way? And does any gain in inter-judge agreement carry over to
agreement with human annotators? To answer them, we specify a
three-stage procedure with deterministic selection rules (Section 3) and
evaluate it on three benchmarks with existing human labels (Section 4).
The procedure never reads human labels. We compare it against the
original rubric, a published judging method, a one-shot LLM rewrite, and
a matched control that edits the same number of unselected clauses with
the same editor prompt and example rule. Hypotheses, metrics, and tests
were fixed in a version-controlled protocol before any evaluation call.

Our contributions are as follows.

- •
  A fully specified, label-free procedure that maps cross-judge
  disagreement to a bounded set of clause edits, with selection and
  example rules that can be rerun without human judgment.
- •
  A preregistered evaluation with a matched random-clause control, five
  judges from three providers, and three human-annotated benchmarks.
- •
  Evidence that targeting contested clauses increases inter-judge
  agreement beyond untargeted edits on SummEval, that this gain does not
  transfer to agreement with expert ratings, and null results on
  HealthBench and USR.
- •
  Practical observations on running multi-provider judge panels,
  including a case where structured-output constraints were not enforced
  on one provider’s batch transport.

## 2 Related Work

Rubric-based LLM judges. G-Eval prompts an LLM with task criteria and
evaluation steps and reported improved correlation with human ratings on
SummEval (Liu et al., 2023). HealthBench grades medical conversations
against physician-written criteria with an LLM grader (Arora et al.,
2025). Autorubric unifies rubric-based evaluation on non-verifiable
tasks (Rao and Callison-Burch, 2026b), and Bagaria et al. (2026) show
that rubric wording itself can drive judge scores. We take rubrics from
existing benchmarks as given and study how to repair them.

Effects of rubric edits. Huynh et al. (2026) quantify how rubric
modifications affect human-autorater agreement on essay scoring and
instruction following, finding that representative examples, added
context, and separate calls per criterion tend to help while higher
rubric complexity hurts. ResearchRubrics (Sharma et al., 2025) reports
gains from concrete examples and losses from LLM-based augmentation of
criteria. These studies vary the type of edit. We hold the edit
operation fixed and vary which clauses receive it, selecting them
without labels.

Label-free reliability signals. CalibratedRubric estimates the
measurability of each rubric item from inter-judge agreement alone and
filters out items with low measurability, raising agreement with human
gold labels on JudgmentBench (Chen et al., 2026). Our procedure uses the
same signal for a different purpose: it keeps contested clauses and
rewrites them rather than discarding them. PReMISE audits and repairs
rubrics using pairwise human preference data (Roy et al., 2026); our
procedure uses no human data.

Consensus and alignment. Mukherjee et al. (2026) show geometrically that
LLM judges can agree with one another as much as humans do while
agreeing much less with humans. Song et al. (2026) argue that judge
consensus can rest on shared surface heuristics. These results motivate
our decision to treat human agreement (H2) as a primary outcome separate
from inter-judge agreement (H1).

Reporting agreement. Rao and Callison-Burch (2026a) show that several
commonly reported agreement statistics coincide on binary data and
propose a reporting checklist covering scale, tie and abstention
handling, and aggregation level. We follow it in Section 4.5. On the
human side, Borgohain and Mariathas (2026) decompose physician
disagreement in HealthBench, which bounds what any judge can achieve on
that benchmark.

## 3 Procedure

The procedure takes a rubric for one evaluation dimension, a development
split of items, and a panel of J judges, and returns an edited rubric.
All model-generated steps use fixed prompts and are run once.

Decomposition. An editor model splits the rubric for each dimension into
atomic sub-criteria. For HealthBench, each of the 34 official criteria
categories is used unchanged as a sub-criterion. Sub-criteria are
identified as C1, C2, and so on within each dimension; for HealthBench
these identifiers index the official categories in sorted order. Across
the three datasets this yields 75 sub-criteria.

Probing. Each judge scores every development item with a probe output: a
verdict for each sub-criterion (pass, fail, or not applicable), a one-
or two-sentence rationale, and an overall score. Probe outputs are used
only for selection and examples; they are not part of the evaluation.

Stage 1: transfer to code. The editor maps each sub-criterion to one of
four deterministic checkers (n-gram repetition, truncated ending,
formatting artifacts, and lexical overlap with the source or knowledge
snippet) or to none. For a mapped sub-criterion, a threshold is chosen
on the development split to maximize agreement with the judge majority,
counting only items where at least three of five judges gave a pass or
fail verdict. A sub-criterion is transferred only if this agreement is
at least 0.9. Transferred sub-criteria are removed from the judge
rubric, and the checker result is combined with the judge score through
a declarative rule. In our evaluation the editor mapped none of the 75
sub-criteria to a checker, so no sub-criterion was transferred and the
0.9 threshold was never tested. The Stage 1 condition therefore differs
from the original rubric only in presenting the rubric as a list of
sub-criteria, and our results do not speak to the effect of transferring
criteria to code.

Stage 2: localization and clarification. For each remaining
sub-criterion, we compute the rate at which the 10 judge pairs give
different verdicts, excluding not-applicable and missing verdicts. Let n
be the number of remaining sub-criteria in a dimension. We set k =
min(5, $`\lfloor`$n/2$`\rfloor`$) and select up to k sub-criteria with a
positive disagreement rate, in decreasing order of rate, breaking ties
by identifier. The editor rewrites each selected clause once, given the
clause, the development items on which judges split, and the judges’
rationales. If the development split contains items on which all five
judges passed and items on which all five failed the sub-criterion, up
to two pass-fail pairs are attached as examples; otherwise no example is
attached. Rubric text outside the selected clauses is not changed.

The bound $`\lfloor`$n/2$`\rfloor`$ guarantees that at least as many
unselected clauses remain as were selected, so a matched control can
always be formed (Section 4.3). It replaced an initial bound of five
after a rehearsal showed that with three to six sub-criteria per
dimension the initial rule selected every clause in most dimensions. The
change was made before any evaluation call (Appendix A).

Stage 3: residual reporting. Disagreement that remains after
clarification is not edited further. We report it in three layers:
variation across repeated runs of the same judge, differences among
judges from the same provider, and differences across providers.

lowest reasoning setting its provider supports. A judge that fails a

### 4.3 Conditions

Each evaluation item is scored by all five judges under six rubric
conditions.

- •
  Original: the benchmark’s annotation guideline.
- •
  External: a published judging method run with the same judges. For
  SummEval we use the G-Eval prompts verbatim, without probability
  weighting. For USR we use the same template with evaluation steps
  generated once by the editor. For HealthBench we use the official
  grader template.
- •
  Rewrite: the editor rewrites the original rubric once without
  disagreement information, using the same editor instructions as
  clarification, which forbid adding new constructs. HealthBench
  criteria are rewritten one at a time.
- •
  Stage 1: the decomposed rubric after transfer to code. Since no
  sub-criterion was transferred, this is the original rubric presented
  as a list of sub-criteria.
- •
  Full: Stage 1 plus clarification of the selected clauses.
- •
  Random clause: Stage 1 plus the same clarification procedure applied
  to m clauses drawn with seed 20261005 from the clauses not selected in
  that dimension, regardless of their disagreement rate. The editor
  prompt and example rule are identical to the full condition.

We also compute majority and mean aggregates of the original-condition
scores, which require no additional calls. Each aggregate yields one
score per item, so inter-judge $`\alpha`$ is not defined for them and we
report only their agreement with humans. The majority aggregate requires
at least three judges to give the same score and is missing otherwise.

### 4.4 Pre-flight checks

Before development probing and evaluation, every judge scored 30
synthetic items (five clearly good and five clearly bad per dataset) in
the score-only and probe formats, and the HealthBench subset of these
items in the official grader format. The items were written for this
purpose and contain no benchmark text. A judge passed if, within one
retry, all outputs parsed without truncation, the first-attempt parse
failure rate was at most 10%, no pair was inverted, at most one pair was
tied, and the mean score of good items exceeded that of bad items. The
tie allowance was added after one tied pair in the Claude Haiku 4.5
official-format check; it is a post hoc relaxation, and a retrospective
check confirmed that no other past failure would have passed under it
(Appendix A).

### 4.5 Metrics and statistical tests

For each condition, dataset, and dimension we compute Krippendorff’s
$`\alpha`$ over the five judges (Hayes and Krippendorff, 2007), with an
ordinal distance for 3- and 5-point scales and a nominal distance for
binary scales. Missing scores are handled by $`\alpha`$’s pairwise
coincidence formulation. Agreement with humans is computed for each
judge and for a five-judge ensemble, which uses the first run of an item
only when all five judges returned a valid score: Kendall $`\tau`$ with
the expert mean for SummEval, macro F1 against the physician majority
for HealthBench, and Spearman $`\rho`$ with the annotator mean for USR.
For SummEval and USR the ensemble is the unrounded mean of the five
scores. For HealthBench it is the 3-of-5 majority verdict, and macro F1
averages the F1 of the two verdict classes, not of the 34 criteria;
Cohen’s $`\kappa`$ is reported as a secondary metric. Metrics that are
undefined, for example when a class is absent, are reported as missing.
Multi-dimension datasets use the equally weighted mean of per-dimension
differences.

The preregistered primary comparisons, three per dataset and nine in
total, are full minus original in $`\alpha`$ (H1), full minus original
in human agreement (H2), and full minus random clause in $`\alpha`$
(H3). Each comparison uses items scored under both conditions and a
paired cluster bootstrap with 10,000 resamples (Efron, 1979), reporting
percentile 95% intervals and two-sided p-values adjusted with Holm’s
procedure over the nine tests (Holm, 1979). An $`\alpha`$ difference
counts as an effect only if it is at least 0.05 and its interval
excludes zero. The 0.05 threshold is not applied to human agreement
metrics. H4, an exploratory comparison of pairwise weighted $`\kappa`$
among the three Claude judges against the three small judges from
different providers, is not included in the correction.

### 4.6 Preregistration and execution

The hypotheses, metrics, tests, and selection rules were committed under
a version-control tag before any evaluation call or label extraction.
This is a self-registration, not an entry in an external registry. All
six rubric conditions, the judge settings, the sample, and the
submission order were sealed in a manifest before evaluation (SHA-256
prefix 3f49c9c4). Every change to the original plan is listed with its
date and the state of the experiment at that time in Appendix A.

## 5 Results

### 5.1 Execution

All 129,770 planned judge requests (25,954 per judge) were collected
over 23.95 hours. After at most one retry, 27 outputs (0.021%) remained
unparseable; the highest rate in any judge-condition cell was 0.105%. No
request ended in a provider-side failure. Evaluation calls cost
US\$236.69 at batch prices. No sub-criterion was transferred to a
checker in Stage 1. Every dimension had at least one selected clause, so
the full and random-clause conditions differ from Stage 1 in all 11
dimensions; 23 clauses were edited in each of the two conditions.
Example pairs were attached to 12 of the 23 selected clauses and to 16
of the 23 control clauses; none of the five selected HealthBench
criteria had an eligible example pair.

SummEval. The full procedure met the effect criterion against both the
original rubric (H1) and the random-clause control (H3). The gain over
the control (0.074) was as large as the gain over the original (0.069),
so editing an equal number of unselected clauses with the same editor
and example rule produced little of the increase. Agreement with expert
ratings showed no detectable change (H2). Following the preregistered
reporting rule, the primary result for SummEval is that consensus
increased without moving the judges closer to human annotators.

HealthBench. Inter-judge $`\alpha`$ showed no detectable change under
either comparison. Macro F1 against the physician majority decreased by
0.023; the unadjusted interval excludes zero, but the Holm-adjusted
p-value is 0.155, so we do not report a significant decrease.

USR. Inter-judge $`\alpha`$ increased by 0.027 with an unadjusted
interval excluding zero, below the preregistered effect size of 0.05 and
not significant after adjustment (p = 0.070). The other two comparisons
showed no difference.

Run-to-run disagreement ranged from near zero for Claude Haiku 4.5, run
at temperature 0, to 0.246 for GPT-6 Luna on SummEval. On SummEval,
clarification lowered run-to-run disagreement for the two small judges
from other providers and raised it for Claude Opus 5.5 and Claude Sonnet
5.5. The gain in cross-judge agreement was therefore not accompanied by
uniformly more stable individual judges.

## 6 Discussion

Targeting contested clauses matters for consensus. On SummEval, editing
the clauses on which judges disagreed raised inter-judge agreement,
while editing an equal number of other clauses with the same editor
prompt and example rule did not (examples were attached to 12 selected
and 16 control clauses). This argues against the simplest explanation,
that any edit with examples raises agreement, at least for this dataset.
Disagreement on a development split was a usable signal for where judges
read a rubric differently.

Consensus did not become alignment. The same edits produced no
detectable change in agreement with expert ratings. This is consistent
with reports that LLM judges can converge on an axis that human
annotators do not weight (Mukherjee et al., 2026). A label-free
procedure has no information about which of several readings of a clause
the annotators held. When the editor resolves an ambiguous clause, it
may commit the panel to a reading that is internally consistent but not
the annotators’ reading. Our results suggest using disagreement to
locate ambiguous clauses, and a small set of human labels or the
original annotation instructions to decide how to resolve them.

Why HealthBench and USR showed no effect. On HealthBench, only 5 of 34
criteria were edited, and the items assessed under those criteria make
up 120 of the 799 evaluation units (15%), which dilutes any effect on
the pooled $`\alpha`$. None of the five selected criteria had a
unanimous pass-fail example pair on the development split, so their
edits changed wording only. Physician agreement on this benchmark is
itself limited (Borgohain and Mariathas, 2026). On USR, several
dimensions already had high development agreement, and the dataset is
small. We cannot separate these explanations with the present design.

Edits that raise consensus are not the edits that help alignment. The
secondary conditions sharpen this point. Editing unselected clauses
barely changed inter-judge agreement but gave the highest human
agreement of any edited condition on SummEval and USR, while editing
contested clauses did the reverse; in paired comparisons the
human-agreement gap favored the control on all three datasets, with
intervals that include zero. A one-shot rewrite without any disagreement
signal showed no detectable change in human agreement on any dataset,
unlike the degradation reported for LLM-elaborated criteria (Sharma
et al., 2025); our rewrite was a single pass over the existing rubric
under an instruction not to add new constructs, rather than an expansion
with synthetic detail. In paired exploratory comparisons, the rewrite
changed human agreement by less than 0.01 on every dataset (Appendix E).
Apart from the secondary comparison, none of these contrasts were
planned tests, and none of the comparisons in this paragraph is
significant; taken together, they argue against using inter-judge
agreement alone as the objective when repairing rubrics. We did not run
equivalence tests, so the absence of a detectable change should not be
read as evidence of no change.

A negated criterion as a case study. In pre-flight checks, two judges
handled HealthBench criterion C31, which passes a response only if it
does not ask the user follow-up questions, with the same incorrect
verdict: they marked a response consisting only of follow-up questions
as meeting the criterion. One judge’s explanation correctly identified
the failure before emitting criteria_met = true (Appendix B). The
judge’s reasoning was right and its verdict inverted. This is the kind
of clause-level ambiguity, here in the mapping from a negated criterion
to an output value, that the procedure is meant to find. GPT-6 Luna
returned only a score, so we cannot tell whether its error had the same
cause.

Running multi-provider panels. Structured-output enforcement differed by
provider and by transport. For one provider, a response schema passed
through the batch API was not enforced (0 of 20 outputs conformed in a
controlled test), whereas a different documented field was enforced (20
of 20) (Appendix C). Simple score-only checks passed under both, and the
failure appeared only with nested probe outputs. Panels that span
providers need format checks for every output format they use.

## 7 Limitations

Panel composition. Three of the five judges are Claude models, and the
other two providers are represented only by small models. Reasoning
settings and output caps differ across judges (Table 2), so provider,
size, and reasoning effects are confounded. The editor model belongs to
the same family as three judges, which may favor edits that those judges
read the same way.

Changes before evaluation. Several decisions were changed before any
evaluation call or label access: the clause-selection bound before the
self-registered protocol was committed, and the following after it: the
Google judge model, one reasoning-step increase for GPT-6 Luna, the Opus
output cap, the HealthBench output instruction, the removal of prompt
caching, and a relaxation of the pre-flight ordering criterion after a
failure. Each is listed in Appendix A. They were not part of the
original plan and should be read as such.

Procedure scope. Each clause is edited once with a single editor prompt;
we did not study iteration or alternative editors. The procedure’s
original formulation included a step for system defects in a deployed
pipeline, which public benchmarks cannot exercise; we exclude it. The
code-transfer stage was not exercised: the editor mapped none of the 75
sub-criteria to the four available checkers, which target surface
defects such as repetition and truncation. Testing that stage would
require rubrics that name such defects explicitly or a larger checker
library.

Data. The evaluation uses existing human labels, which are themselves
noisy, and three English datasets. SummEval is widely used and may
appear in judges’ training data; this affects all conditions equally but
may raise baseline agreement. HealthBench edits affect a small share of
items. The external baseline re-runs published prompts with current
judges, and our G-Eval variant omits probability weighting.

Statistics. Percentile bootstrap intervals are unadjusted, while
p-values are Holm-adjusted; an interval that excludes zero is therefore
not by itself a significant result. H4 is exploratory and confounds
provider with model size.

## Appendix B Case Study, Criterion C31

#### Synthetic context

User: In this fictional clinic directory, the desk for collecting my
printed visit summary is Desk Azure. Where does the directory say I
should collect it? The directory gives all the information needed.

#### High target

Collect the printed visit summary at Desk Azure.

#### Low target

What desk does your directory name, and what document are you
collecting?

#### Criterion (verbatim)

Step 1: Does the response seek more context from the user by asking
follow up questions that have the intent of gaining more information for
the model to produce a better next response? If yes, fail. Step 2: Does
the response seek more context from the user by stating what information
would be helpful for the user to provide to the model so the model can
produce a better response? If yes, fail. If both steps pass, mark
criteria_met = true. If any of the steps fail, mark criteria_met =
false.

#### Haiku high response (verbatim)

{"criteria_met": true, "explanation": "The response does not seek more
context from the user by asking follow-up questions (Step 1 passes), nor
does it state what information would be helpful for the user to provide
(Step 2 passes). The assistant directly answers the user’s question
based on the information provided in the fictional clinic directory.
Both steps pass, so the criteria is met."}

#### Haiku low response (verbatim)

{"criteria_met": true, "explanation": "The assistant’s response asks
follow-up questions (’What desk does your directory name, and what
document are you collecting?’) with the intent of gaining more
information to produce a better next response. According to Step 1 of
the rubric, if the response seeks more context from the user by asking
follow up questions that have the intent of gaining more information, it
should fail. Since the response does ask follow-up questions with this
intent, the criteria is met (meaning it fails the rubric requirement)."}

#### Luna high response (verbatim)

{"score":1}

#### Luna low response (verbatim)

{"score":1}

The synthetic user states the desk name and says the directory gives all
needed information. The bad response asks which desk the directory names
and which document the user is collecting. C31 fails a response that
seeks more context through follow-up questions. Claude Haiku 4.5’s
explanation states that the response asks follow-up questions and
therefore fails Step 1, then outputs criteria_met = true. GPT-6 Luna at
its lowest reasoning setting gave the same response a passing score,
with no explanation available.
