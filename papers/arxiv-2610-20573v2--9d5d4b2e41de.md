# Beyond LLM Judge: Mechanical Verification for Agentic Autoformalization

Anna Mei Affiliation: Harvard University
Email: [amei@g.harvard.edu](mailto:)    Michael R. Douglas
Affiliation: CMSA, Harvard University and AletheAI, Inc
Email: [mdouglas@cmsa.fas.harvard.edu](mailto:)

###### Abstract

We introduce a novel method for verifying Lean formalizations that goes
beyond LLM-judged faithfulness. Unlike most verification methods, which
rely on LLMs or trained models, our method combines LLM-based semantic
review with mechanical numerical and vacuity checks, providing checkable
feedback and detecting errors missed by LLM judges. We apply this
method, along with our Extract-Verify-Proof autoformalization pipeline,
to construct the Formalized Library of Mathematical Functions (FLMF), a
partial formalization of the NIST Digital Library of Mathematical
Functions (DLMF). On three labeled datasets, our method achieves the
lowest accepted-error rate among the evaluated methods, measured as the
fraction of incorrect statements accepted by the verification method.
Expert review of the results also identifies translation and source
defects in existing datasets. Code available at
[https://github.com/annamei1/FLMF-Ch7ErrFunc-Release.git](https://github.com/annamei1/FLMF-Ch7ErrFunc-Release.git).

## 1 Introduction

With the rapid development of agentic coding assistants, the
formalization of mathematics has become very accessible to researchers
and has received a lot of attention. However, to this day, the most
trusted and widely used library is still Mathlib (The mathlib Community,
2020), whose correctness is guaranteed by careful human review and which
is therefore trusted by its downstream users. For the same reason, it
also faces a slow review process and limited coverage of existing
mathematics, blocking the formalization of more advanced mathematical
work. Efforts have been made to resolve this problem by building agentic
systems to formalize textbooks, research blueprints, and
dependency-guided libraries at increasing scale (Gloeckle et al., 2026;
Rammal et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Cabral et al., 2026; Milikic et
al., 2026; Douglas et al., 2026).

In contrast to Mathlib, autoformalizations of this kind face severe
challenges to their reliability. An autoformalizer can change a domain,
index, sign, or branch convention, or introduce contradictory
hypotheses, and still produce a provable statement. Audits of formal
benchmarks document such defects (An et al., 2026).

We therefore propose a verification method that combines numerical
verification, an LLM check, and a vacuity check, along with an agentic
autoformalization pipeline, to allow the creation of trustworthy
reusable formal libraries. We chose the Digital Library of Mathematical
Functions (DLMF) as our testing ground, and propose the Formalized
Library of Mathematical Functions (FLMF) as the product of our pipeline
and verification method.

This paper makes three contributions:

- •
  Numerical and vacuity verification method (Section 3). We introduce
  numerical and vacuity checks on Lean statements, which are mechanical
  and provide checkable feedback on points of failure. As indicated by
  their names, a numerical check verifies numerical validity of a Lean
  statement, and a vacuity check looks for vacuous formalization, for
  example a tautological definition. So far, all verification in
  autoformalization relies on LLM judgements, and our method complements
  the ambiguity and arbitrariness of an LLM judge’s verdicts. Its
  feedback is tractable and informative, and passing these checks adds
  another layer of certainty about the correctness of a Lean statement.
- •
  Autoformalization pipeline (Section 4). Our pipeline divides the
  formalization process into three stages: extract, verify, and prove.
  We emphasize human gating at each stage, requiring every item either
  to pass a gate or to be documented with a reason for failure. We then
  use an auditor agent to revise those documents against written
  guidelines. We also keep track of all statement corrections to allow
  future studies of them.
- •
  Corrections to existing datasets (Section 5). Expert review of our
  verification flags identifies multiple defects in existing datasets.
  Among the statements that are labeled correct, we found 2 defects
  among ProofNet items labeled faithful by Ground False, 61 defects
  among correct-labeled Beyond Compilation items, and 205 defects in
  RLM25 (Table 3). We will release corrected statements for confirmed
  defects.

## 2 Related Work

#### Evaluating formalization faithfulness.

Some recent works use reference-based metrics to evaluate the
faithfulness of formalization: BEq and its variants check bidirectional
provable equivalence (Liu et al., 2025c; Poiroux et al., 2025a), GTED
compares operator trees (Liu et al., 2025b), and ASSESS combines
semantic and structural similarity (Liu et al., 2026). These metrics
need a reference formal statement, which is a limitation when it comes
to the formalization of natural language statements. On the other hand,
reference-free methods score the informal–formal pair directly.
FormalAlign trains a model to predict alignment from sequence certainty
and representation similarity (Lu et al., 2025a). LeanScorer breaks a
statement into components and has an LLM judge each one (Xuejun et al.,
2025). Beyond Compilation evaluates LLM judges for faithful
natural-language-to-Lean translation (Zhang et al., 2026). FormalMATH
uses back-translation followed by LLM judgments to filter formalizations
that do not preserve the original problem (Yu et al., 2025). As a
result, Poiroux et al. show that automatic metrics disagree with humans
on research-level statements and release RLM25 (Poiroux et al., 2025a).
Ground False audits formal benchmarks for kernel-checked but wrong
targets, classifies each ProofNet statement’s relation to its informal
source, and releases both that taxonomy and a corrected
ProofNet-Verified (An et al., 2026). In this paper, we compare our
verification method against them using formal–informal pair datasets.

#### Agentic formalization.

Agentic formalization extends single statement translation to the
construction of formal libraries. Gloeckle et al. coordinate parallel
agents on a shared code base to formalize a graduate textbook (Gloeckle
et al., 2026), while AutoformBot combines dependency-aware scheduling
and collaborative version control to build libraries across multiple
textbooks (Rammal et al., 2026). At the workflow level, LeanFlow studies
how proof workflows and toolsets affect the completion and efficiency of
formalizing entire papers (Milikic et al., 2026); Requena Pozo et al.
combine iterative proof refinement, library search, and context
management in a minimal agent (Requena Pozo et al., 2026); and OpenGauss
provides an open-source Lean autoformalization harness (Math, Inc.,
2026). FormalScience incorporates domain-expert feedback into an agentic
pipeline for formalizing scientific reasoning (Meadows et al., 2026).
These systems motivate our emphasis on statement verification and human
gates within a larger formalization workflow.

## 3 Faithfulness Verification

Our verification method consists of three components: a semantic (LLM)
check, a numerical check, and a vacuity check. The first two evaluate
the Lean theorem statement without the proof body. The vacuity check
runs after the proof is constructed and determines if the proof was
vacuous. No single check establishes faithfulness, and we treat
verification as a stack in which each layer has a distinct failure model
and any substantive mismatch blocks automatic acceptance. The following
discussion is based on our formalization of DLMF, but a large part of it
is applicable to other large-scale formalizations of natural language
math. For each theorem, we attached a verification tag, which is
unverified by default and turns to verified after passing the checks.

### 3.1 Semantic Check

The semantic checks consist of a direct LLM inspection and a
back-translation check. The direct inspection passes both the Lean
statement and the informal statement to an LLM judge, and the judge
returns a verdict about the faithfulness of the Lean translation.

The back-translation check consists of two steps. In the first step, an
agent (translator) reads the Lean theorem statement, along with all
definitions involved, without comments, and translates it into plain
English. Masking the comments reduces the risk of hallucination on the
agent’s side, forcing it to adhere to the exact Lean code. The
translation is formatted to include the statement, variables,
constraints, and symbols.

As an example, the translation of §DLMF5.5.3
$`\Gamma(s)\Gamma(1-s)=\pi/\sin(\pi s)`$ would be:

> STATEMENT: For any complex number $`z`$ that is not an integer,
> $`\Gamma(z)\Gamma(1-z)=\pi/\sin(\pi z)`$.
>
> VARIABLES: $`z`$: A complex number, $`z\in\mathbb{C}`$.
>
> CONSTRAINTS: $`z\neq m`$ for every $`m\in\mathbb{Z}`$.
>
> SYMBOLS: Gamma - the complex Gamma function. Complex.sim - the complex
> sine function. $`\uparrow\pi`$ - the mathematical constant $`\pi`$,
> cast to a complex number.

A second agent (judge) reads this English rendering and compares it with
the equation extracted from the DLMF source.¹¹ 1 We designed an
extractor to pull information from DLMF metadata, which involves an XML
file along with the website display, allowing more reliable extraction
of equation content and constraints. A sample extracted JSON file is
available in Appendix C. In addition, we supply a
STANDING_CONSTRAINTS.md document to capture the constraints stated
earlier in the source that may be missed by the equation parser.

The direct inspection may be the most intuitive thing to do, but having
a back-translation check guarantees that the Lean statement is accurate
enough to preserve its meaning after a back-translation. Having two
separate agents working independently also avoids the failure mode in
which an agent recognizes an inconsistency but chooses to ignore it. The
verdicts from the judges fall into three categories: FAITHFUL, DIVERGE,
or UNCERTAIN. For the latter two, the judge also documents why it
considers the translation unfaithful and waits for further action. When
a theorem obtains a FAITHFUL verdict from both the direct inspection and
the back-translation check, its verification status can change to
verified.

### 3.2 Numerical Check

When possible²² 2 Exceptions include definitions, limits, infinite sums,
etc., that cannot be converted into a numerical check., a stated theorem
undergoes a numerical check in which a mechanical extractor translates
the Lean theorem into evaluations in *mpmath*x, an open-source Python
library for arbitrary-precision numerical computation and special
functions. A special function is bound to its mpmath counterpart with
the appropriate convention. The checker samples points from the stated
domain and compares the function values on both sides of the identity.
Exact identities use residual gates; asymptotics use ratio or rate
gates. Failure of the numerical check vetoes the verified status granted
by the back-translation check.

One natural question is how to guarantee the correctness of the
Lean-mpmath binding. This binding is trustworthy because we require that
every special function is only defined once, and the binding is attached
to the definition. When performing the numerical check, the program
traces the definitions used in the Lean statement and uses the
corresponding bindings. We can also observe how many times each
definition/binding has been used independently for numerical checks,
further confirming its reliability.

There are also limitations to the numerical check. As we are working
with special functions in DLMF, we can only sample points in the domain
and evaluate the functions at those points. A common failure is the
inability to identify an incorrect domain for a functional equality. For
example, the Lean statement may have a smaller domain than the actual
statement, or it may fail to exclude isolated points from the domain. If
those points are not selected by the numerical checker, it will fail to
flag the error. Therefore, the numerical check should only be used as a
partial check for correctness, not a complete faithfulness check.

### 3.3 Vacuity Check

A vacuity check scans the theorem statement and proof body to detect
empty statements. Simple checks include testing whether trivial proof
methods such as rfl or simp close the goal. In addition, we have a
checker that scans for goal-independent proofs, which works by replacing
the target goal with an opaque conclusion VacQ and checking whether the
proof still compiles. If it does, then the proof is goal-independent,
and the most likely cause is a set of contradictory hypotheses from
which one can derive False³³ 3 Any conclusion can be derived from
False.. If the checker flags a vacuous proof, the verified status is
also reverted to unverified.

We would like to emphasize that none of these checks is perfect; they
all have different domains of effectiveness and limitations, and the
stack is undergoing active development as the project progresses. For
example, the case that motivated the vacuity check was the original
formalization of DLMF 7.13.5: its back-translation was judged faithful,
yet proof review found that its completeness and ordering hypotheses for
the Fresnel zeros were jointly contradictory. We also made these checks
strict on purpose, so we would rather have them flag a correct statement
than miss an incorrect one. (Section 6 gives more detail on this
particular case.)

## 4 Autoformalization Pipeline

The DLMF is organized in chapters and equations. Distinct chapters in
the DLMF focus on different types of functions and rarely depend on each
other; therefore, parallelization is quite feasible in FLMF. There are
also special “methods” chapters, which describe mathematical techniques
used to establish some of the identities. In these cases, we need to
formalize, or at least state, the content of the methods before we can
prove the related identities. These dependency relations are captured in
a DAG (Directed Acyclic Graph) and are dynamically updated as
formalization progresses. When the proving agent attempts to prove a
theorem, it first looks it up in the DAG and reports whether
infrastructure needed to construct the proof is missing.

This strategy is not unique to DLMF. Indeed, related works have used
similar approaches (Wang et al., 2026; Cabral et al., 2026), and it
plays a crucial role in the autoformalization of more sophisticated math
objects that have complicated dependencies on each other.

In FLMF, we proceed with formalization in three stages: Extract, Verify,
and Prove.

| Stage   | Agent work                                         | Gate on every theorem / equation                                          |
| ------- | -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| DAG     | Enumerate equations and dependencies               | Open-world gap report                                                     |
| Extract | Write typed definitions and theorem statements     | (i) Formalized with appropriate tag or (ii) documented in DISPOSITIONS.md |
| Verify  | Run numerical and back-translation fidelity checks | (i) Obtained a verified stamp or (ii) documented in HUMAN_REVIEW.md       |
| Prove   | Fill frozen theorem bodies in waves                | (i) Proved with clean build and 0 sorry or (ii) documented in UNPROVEN.md |

Table 1: Autoformalization pipeline for FLMF.

### 4.1 Extract

At this stage, an agent extracts all listed equations in a chapter by
their equation numbers. The agent then writes each equation in the DAG
and establishes its dependencies on existing nodes. The agent identifies
all _definitions_ needed to state the extracted equations, for example,
the definitions of Bessel functions or theta functions. The definitions
are only written once to guarantee consistency in downstream usage.
After that, parallel agents are deployed to write Lean statements using
these definitions. A complete proof is not required. Each extracted
theorem carries 4 tags: DLMF equation number, Fidelity check method,
Verification status, and its corresponding DAG node. The Extract
workflow is gated on completion: every listed equation in the DLMF has
to either (i) appear in FLMF as a theorem with appropriate tags or (ii)
be documented in DISPOSITIONS.md with an explanation of why it is not
stated. This is an example of a formalized statement:

> /-- \*\*DLMF 5.5.3\*\* --- $`\Gamma(s)\Gamma(1-s)=\pi/\sin(\pi s)`$
> (complex)  
> (DLMF-faithful restriction of
> ‘complex_reflection’:$`s\neq 0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\ldots`$).  
> DAG: E-5.5.3.  
> Fidelity: numeric PASS  
> Verified: verified  
> -/  
> theorem complex_reflection_dlmf  
>  (s : $`\mathbb{C}`$)  
>  (\_hs : $`\forall`$ m : $`\mathbb{Z}`$, s $`\neq`$ (m :
> $`\mathbb{C}`$)) :  
>  Complex.Gamma s \* Complex.Gamma (1 - s) =  
>    (Real.pi : $`\mathbb{C}`$) /  
>    Complex.sin ((Real.pi : $`\mathbb{C}`$) \* s) := by  
>  sorry

### 4.2 Verify

When a theorem is first stated, its verification status is unverified by
default. A successful verification check, which is the combination of
semantic, numerical, and vacuity checks discussed in Section 3, allows a
stamper to set this tag to verified. If a theorem fails a verification
check, an agent analyzes the reason for failure. For the easy fixes,
e.g., missing premises, a wrong sign, or incompatible conventions, an
agent fixes the theorem and reruns the verification check; otherwise it
is directed to human review. This stage is gated by requiring every
equation to have either (i) a verified stamp or (ii) a documented entry
in HUMAN_REVIEW.md. If a statement does get corrected, it will be logged
in an ERRATA file, keeping track of the mistakes made by agents and
allowing further error analysis.

### 4.3 Prove

After the Extract and Verify stages, we have the full list of theorems
that are faithful but await to be proved. An agent surveys all open
sorrys and proposes a proof plan. It contains a methods component,
consisting of general proof strategies that apply to many identities,
and waves of proofs that resolve the remaining theorems in dependency
order. This step also observes and modifies the DAG dependency
structure. The proving stage is gated on either (i) a sorry-free proof
body or (ii) documentation in UNPROVEN.md.

### 4.4 Audit

Beyond the pipeline, we have auditor agents to inspect the human review
documents at each stage (dispositions, human_review, unproven, etc.).
Very often, these documents contain a mix of simple mistakes, such as a
wrong convention, and serious issues, such as a hidden assumption that
is not stated in the informal text. The auditors are prompted with the
human evaluation guidelines and make simple fixes while providing
analysis and suggestions for the harder issues that really need a human
decision.

We would like to emphasize the need for gating and human interaction at
each stage. Asking the agent to work alone risks two failure modes: the
agent spends many tokens working on a single problem and gets stuck, or
it secretly skips the work and does not report its true status. Human
review is also crucial for design choices, such as definition
conventions, or special cases in which implicit constraints or
specifications are conveyed but not listed.

Having the auditor significantly reduces the work of a human expert. The
human expert can focus on the hard and subtle cases in which we do not
readily trust the model’s judgement, while the rest are taken care of
autonomously. Having an auditor does not reduce the reliability of the
output, since the edited statement will have to go through the
verification check again before it can be stamped verified. On very
special occasions, for example, one that involves a source misprint, a
human can stamp a theorem verified despite it not passing the
verification check, but that is only possible after careful review by
the expert, and no autonomous agents can do that.

Combined with our verification method, the workflow allows
autoformalization of large-scale libraries without losing credibility.
As part of this work in progress, we were able to complete the
formalization of about 10% of DLMF, comprising around 1,000 entries, in
2 man-months. Further details are available in Appendix A.

## 5 Experiments

In the following experiments, we compare our verification method with
other baseline methods across different datasets. We demonstrate that
ours has the lowest rate of accepting an incorrect formalization, which
is the goal of building a reliable autoformalization verification. We
also report runs of our method on external datasets, presenting
potential defects in them.

### 5.1 Setup

#### Datasets.

The experiment contains 1,810 informal–formal pairs from four datasets,
and the datasets provide a correctness evaluation for each pair. FLMF is
a selected set of 471 statements from the ongoing DLMF formalization,
combining library statements, mutations⁴⁴ 4 We included some mutations
that are intended to be incorrect, mutation mechanisms are extracted
from historical errors, historical errors, and source-misprint pairs.
This dataset focuses on special function equalities. ProofNet uses the
original release (Azerbayev et al., 2023), with Ground False’s
faithfulness verdicts as labels (An et al., 2026). It contains 371
undergraduate math problems from analysis, algebra, and topology. Beyond
Compilation is a set of 400 graduate-level math problems with semantic
evaluations (Zhang et al., 2026). RLM25 extracts 617 informal--formal
statement pairs from six research-level formalization projects across
number theory, harmonic analysis, combinatorics, etc⁵⁵ 5 RLM25 doesn’t
contain any evaluation component, so we assume all statements are
labeled “correct”.(Poiroux et al., 2025a). Appendix D gives a more
detailed breakdown of dataset construction process.

#### Methods and models.

_Ours_ is the union of the three checks as discussed in Section 3, where
direct inspection uses GPT-5.5, and back-translation uses Gemini 3.1 Pro
as the translator and GPT-5.5 as the judge. The baseline methods are
GPT-5.5 and Opus 4.6 as direct judges, each called once per pair and
once in joint batches of 16 (“joint”); LeanScorer (Xuejun et al., 2025),
which uses LLMs (GPT-5.5) to assess how faithfully each component of a
statement is formalized; and FormalAlign (Lu et al., 2025a), a trained
model that evaluates alignment between informal and formal statements,
at a certainty threshold of 0.3 (Appendix E gives more detail). Every
method sees the same informal statement and formal context.

#### Method coverage.

The semantic checks, direct LLM judges, LeanScorer, and FormalAlign
apply to every informal–formal pair. However, the goal-independent part
of the vacuity check requires a completed proof and has no coverage on
the original ProofNet or Beyond Compilation. In these cases, the vacuity
check only looks for trivial proof methods. Similarly, the numerical
check requires a statement to be numerically evaluable, which is not the
case for, for example, infinite sums statements.

#### Metric.

We evaluate acceptance by a verification method against each dataset’s
labels, which are categorized into “correct” and “incorrect”. Our
primary metric is the _accepted-error rate_: among the items a
verification method accepts (does not flag), the fraction that are
labeled incorrect. This metric aligns with our goal of ensuring that a
Lean statement passing the gate is correct. We also report the
percentage of total statements flagged.

## 6 Case Studies

We present two case studies on formalization defects identified by our
method. One is identified by the vacuity checker alone and not by any
other method, which is a subtle formalization defect involving
contradictory hypotheses. The second is a false Taylor bound from the
Beyond Compilation dataset that was faithfully translated to Lean and
flagged only by our numerical check and none others. We therefore argue
that even if we can make sure an LLM translates an informal statement
seemingly well, the statement itself can contain subtleties or defects
that are not captured by the model, resulting in an incorrect
formalization.

### 6.1 Case 1: vacuous statement in FLMF.

#### DLMF §7.13(iii)

In the first quadrant of $`\mathbb{C}`$, the Fresnel integral $`C(z)`$
has an infinite set of zeros $`z_{n}=x_{n}+iy_{n}`$, $`n=1,2,3,...`$
arranged in order of increasing absolute value. As $`n\to\infty`$, the
$`x_{n}`$ and $`y_{n}`$ corresponding to the zeros of $`C(z)`$ satisfy

```math
x_{n}\sim\lambda+\frac{\alpha(\alpha\pi-4)}{8\pi\lambda^{3}}+\cdots,\qquad y_{n}\sim\frac{\alpha}{2\lambda}+\cdots,
```

with

```math
\lambda=\sqrt{4n-1},\qquad\alpha=(2/\pi)\ln(\pi\lambda).
```

The extracted Lean statement was (definitions stripped)

> theorem erf_7_13_5 (z : $`\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}`$)  
>  (hzero : $`\forall`$ n, cfresnelC (z n) = 0)  
>  (hquad : $`\forall`$ n, 0 \< (z n).re $`\wedge`$ 0 \< (z n).im)  
>  (hmono : StrictMono (fun n $`\mapsto`$ (z n).re))  
>  (hall : $`\forall`$ w : $`\mathbb{C}`$, cfresnelC w = 0 $`\to`$  
>       0 \< w.re $`\to`$ 0 \< w.im $`\to`$ $`\exists`$ n, z n = w) :  
>  (fun n $`\mapsto`$ (z n).re - fresnelCZeroReApprox n)  
>    =o\[atTop\] (fun n $`\mapsto`$ fresnelCZeroLambda n ˆ ($`-3`$ :
> $`\mathbb{Z}`$)) $`\wedge`$  
>  (fun n $`\mapsto`$ (z n).im - fresnelCZeroImApprox n)  
>    =o\[atTop\] (fun n $`\mapsto`$ fresnelCZeroLambda n ˆ ($`-1`$ :
> $`\mathbb{Z}`$)) := by

This Lean theorem assumes that a sequence $`z_{n}`$ enumerates all
first-quadrant zeros of $`C(z)`$ while their real parts are strictly
increasing (hmono). However, the informal source explicitly orders the
zeros by increasing absolute value. The assumptions in the Lean
statement are contradictory because the large-index zeros will approach
the real axis, and a mirror symmetry transforms them into first-quadrant
zeros with arbitrarily small positive real parts. Therefore, there must
be a first-quadrant zero whose real part is smaller than that of
$`z_{0}`$, the first entry of the sequence, violating the monotonicity
imposed by hmono. The resulting contradiction among the hypotheses
proves False, from which the stated asymptotic conclusion can be
obtained vacuously by False.elim.

In verification, this Lean statement obtained a FAITHFUL verdict from
the semantic checks because the checker was not able to catch this
subtle mistake. Indeed, all other baseline methods accepted it as a
correct translation, and the proof compiled without error. Only the
vacuity check was able to spot the problem.

### 6.2 Case 2: A defective informal source in Beyond Compilation.

The informal statement says that if $`f`$ is holomorphic on a
neighborhood of the closed unit disk and $`f(0)\neq 0`$, then there
exists a constant $`C\in\mathbb{R}`$ such that

```math
\left|1-\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{f^{(k)}(0)}{k!\,f(0)}z^{k}\right|\leq C|z|^{m+1}\qquad(m\in\mathbb{N},\ |z|\leq 1).
```

The corresponding formalization, with its theorem name abbreviated to X,
is

> theorem X {f : $`\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}`$}  
>  (hf : $`\exists`$ U : Set $`\mathbb{C}`$, IsOpen U $`\wedge`$
> Metric.closedBall (0 : $`\mathbb{C}`$) 1 $`\subseteq`$ U $`\wedge`$  
>    DifferentiableOn $`\mathbb{C}`$ f U)  
>  (h0 : f 0 $`\neq`$ 0) :  
>  $`\exists`$ C : $`\mathbb{R}`$, $`\forall`$ m : $`\mathbb{N}`$,
> $`\forall`$ z : $`\mathbb{C}`$, $`\lVert`$z$`\rVert`$ $`\leq`$ 1
> $`\to`$  
>    $`\lVert`$(1 : $`\mathbb{C}`$) - ($`\sum`$ k $`\in`$ Finset.range
> (m + 1),  
>      ((derivˆ\[k\] f) 0) / ((k.factorial : $`\mathbb{N}`$) \* f 0) \*
> z ˆ k)$`\rVert`$  
>     $`\leq`$ C \* $`\lVert`$z$`\rVert`$ ˆ (m + 1)

This is a faithful translation of the informal statement. However, the
numerical check finds the admissible counter example $`f(z)=e^{z}`$.
With $`m=1`$, for real $`0<z\leq 1`$ the claimed bound becomes

```math
|1-(1+z)|=|z|\leq C|z|^{2}.
```

Dividing both sides by $`|z|^{2}`$, we get $`C\geq 1/|z|`$, which no
constant $`C`$ can satisfy as $`z\to 0`$.

In the experiment, this correct-labeled entry is flagged only by the
numerical check among all methods. Back-translation, GPT-5.5 and Opus
4.6 in both single-pair and joint modes return FAITHFUL; LeanScorer
accepts it with a score of $`0.67`$ at a threshold of $`0.6`$;
FormalAlign accepts it with a certainty score of $`0.394`$ at a
threshold of $`0.3`$. None of the stored semantic explanations
identifies the impossible uniform bound.

## 7 Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced our verification method, conducted
experiments across different benchmarks, and demonstrated that our
method yields the lowest accepted-error rate, therefore yielding the
most trustworthy outcome. We integrate these methods into an
Extract–Verify–Prove pipeline with human gates and audits, ensuring
completeness and faithfulness of the formalization results. FLMF
provides the development setting and a preliminary evaluation panel, and
its broader dataset remains under construction and will be presented in
a separate paper.

We highlight the numerical and vacuity checks in our verification, which
are complementary to the verdicts of an LLM or model-based judge, the
most common approach used across all other formalization work. According
to our experiments, these checks are able to spot mistakes ignored by
all LLM judges, including subtle translation defects or informal source
errors. The checks are also informative in pointing out the exact
numerical misalignment or proof inconsistency. A theorem passing these
checks can be trusted to be at least numerically consistent and not
trivially vacuous. Combined with our autoformalization pipeline, we can
then obtain a promising Lean formalization of the informal source.

There are certain limitations to our current methods. Numerical and
vacuity checks are only applicable to numerically evaluable theorems or
complete proofs. There could be defects in these checks as well, for
example, a wrong binding in the numerical check or a subtly vacuous
proof that does not fall within the detection range of our vacuity
detector. Passing these checks doesn’t guarantee correctness either, for
example a narrower domain in the Lean statement compared to the informal
source is a common failure mode.

Future work will extend the numerical and vacuity checks to handle
additional corner cases encountered during formalization, while
guaranteeing faithfulness of the checks. We aim to reduce vacuity false
alarms by distinguishing contradictory assumptions and missing
mathematical content from legitimate definitional identities and routine
proofs. We will also explore additional mechanical checks that
complement the existing methods and provide independently checkable
evidence.

## Appendix D Dataset construction, panel composition, and detection

### D.1 Dataset construction and labels

The evaluation contains 1,810 informal–formal pairs from four datasets.
Table 6 gives their composition. Each dataset supplies its own criteria
for labeling a pair correct or incorrect; these labels do not represent
a common judgment of faithfulness or mathematical validity.

#### FLMF (182 correct / 289 incorrect).

The FLMF dataset contains 471 statements in eight components: 40 library
seeds, 40 copies with bound variables renamed, 7 human-approved
corrections to DLMF misprints, 160 intentional mutations of the seeds,
17 historical FLMF errors, 7 printed forms of DLMF misprints, 100
sampled verified theorems, and 100 statements from the project’s errata
log. The seeds and renamings each contain 38 correct and 2 incorrect
items, and the sampled verified theorems contain 99 correct and 1
incorrect item. Corrected misprints are labeled correct. Mutations,
historical errors, printed misprints, and errata items are labeled
incorrect.

The mutations target eight mechanisms (M1–M8): impossible hypotheses,
wrong objects, branches, totalization at poles, quantifier strength,
indices and coercions, normalization, and lost source content. There are
12–20 mutations per mechanism and 24 simpler sanity mutations (Table 7).
We exclude 20 independent control nominations. We also exclude two
Fresnel coefficient pairs, comprising four items, because their stronger
asymptotic claims are unresolved. Historical Fresnel errors remain
included.

The 100 sampled verified theorems satisfy the same eligibility criteria
as the seeds: a semantic verified stamp at the pinned commit, a single
numbered DLMF equation, and a completed proof. Selection is
deterministic and stratified by library area, with a cap of 20 items per
stratum. This component is disjoint from the seeds, renamings,
mutations, historical artifacts, and misprint pairs by both declaration
name and DLMF equation.

Each of the 100 errata items is a declaration recovered from git history
in its form immediately before correction. Of these, 76 correspond to
errors found by the numerical or vacuity check and 24 to errors found by
other methods. They form 65 erratum clusters, and 99 of the 100
statements elaborate. Together with the mutations, historical errors,
printed misprints, and five library entries rejected in human review,
they account for the 289 incorrect items. The 129 incorrect items other
than mutations provide examples of errors arising in formalization or in
the source.

#### ProofNet (163 correct / 204 incorrect)

(Azerbayev et al., 2023). We use 367 original ProofNet statements from
the Lean 4.28 port distributed with ProofNet-Verified. The formal
statements are those of ProofNet, and the labels are the Ground False
verdicts (An et al., 2026). The 163 items labeled _faithful_ are
correct. The incorrect items comprise 75 labeled _stronger_, 55
_weaker_, 64 _incomparable_, and 10 whose informal statement is
ambiguous (9) or wrong (1).

#### Beyond Compilation (245 correct / 106 incorrect)

(Zhang et al., 2026). We use 351 of the 400 released sources. The
exclusions are 42 reported compilation failures, 6 entries from which no
single statement can be separated from the proof, and 1 informal source
that states no claim. A pair is labeled correct if the release’s GPT-5.2
judge assigns a score of at least 9. Human annotations are available for
156 items whose recorded judge score matches the candidate’s released
score, and we use them as secondary labels. The annotations are
predominantly acceptances and concern translations selected from model
acceptances, so they do not independently assess the items labeled
incorrect by the judge.

#### RLM25 (621 reference pairs)

(Poiroux et al., 2025a). The reconstructed dataset contains reference
pairs from six projects: Carleson (111), FLT (56), FLT3 (84), PFR (146),
PrimeNumberTheoremAnd (99), and TestingLowerBounds (125). The published
release contains 619 pairs. Reconstruction from public sources yields
621, including one extra eligible node in PFR and one in
TestingLowerBounds, so membership does not exactly match the release
manifest. RLM25 provides no correctness labels. We treat all reference
pairs as correct and report their flag rate.

The formal context includes project definitions referenced by each
statement, including definitions declared in other files and those
introduced with irreducible_def or noncomputable abbrev. For 310 of the
621 items, this supplies definitions absent from the source prefix in
the statement’s own file. Statement extraction accounts for local let
expressions, which is necessary to preserve two statements in full.
FormalAlign’s released adapter does not receive formal context.

#### Proof availability.

The goal-independent part of the vacuity check requires a completed
proof. The 100 sampled verified FLMF theorems have completed proofs. For
errata items, coverage depends on the availability of a replayable proof
of the statement as written before correction. Beyond Compilation and
the original ProofNet statements provide no proofs, so the vacuity check
on these datasets is limited to trivial proof methods, as described in
Section 5.1.
