###### Abstract.

Law students are examined through open-text answers: case solutions,
essays and memos that apply the law to a set of facts. Such answers are
expensive to grade, human graders disagree widely on them, and large
language models (LLMs) now offer to grade them at scale, both in
teaching and in the benchmarks that test legal AI. This paper
systematizes how open-text legal answers can be evaluated. We derive
eight desiderata from educational measurement and from the law that
governs exams, among them reasons, credit for defensible alternative
solutions, robustness to gaming and human oversight. We then compare
eight evaluation paradigms against them, from unstructured human grading
to LLM judges with instance-specific rubrics and pairwise comparison.
Three findings stand out. A rubric written for the task is the strongest
lever for agreement between an LLM judge and human graders, but studies
measure that agreement against different references and rarely report
how far the human graders agree with each other. The central validation
gap lies where examination law is most demanding: defensible alternative
solutions, follow-on errors, and the examiner’s correction of model
judgments. Rubric-centred grading is the most auditable family, because
it makes the standard, its application and the final decision checkable.
From this we derive the trustworthy rubric judge: an instance rubric
that credits arguments and alternative paths, evidence checked in code,
probes, repeated runs, calibration against independent human grades,
logging, and an examiner who reads every script. Today the evidence
supports such a judge for formative feedback and for benchmarks, and as
an assistant to the examiner, not a replacement, where credit is at
stake.

## 1. Introduction

Legal education tests a skill that is hard to measure: applying the law
to facts in writing. A German student receives a fact pattern
(_Sachverhalt_) and writes an opinion in appraisal style
(_Gutachtenstil_); a US student analyses a hypothetical in IRAC form
(issue, rule, application, conclusion); a bar candidate drafts a memo or
a judgment. The exam case stands in for the file a lawyer or a court
receives, and the opinion rehearses the analysis that precedes advice or
a decision. These answers run to many pages, admit more than one
defensible solution, and are graded by experts who often disagree. The
same German exam can receive grades several points apart on the 18-point
scale (Hufeld, 2024), and when 17 US law professors graded the same
essays without a scoring guide, a single professor’s grade reached an
intraclass correlation (ICC) of .58 with the others (Klein and Hart,
1968). Grading is also slow, so students get little feedback before the
graded exam, although individual feedback measurably improves their
grades (Schwarcz and Farganis, 2017).

Large language models (LLMs) have changed both sides of this problem.
They write answers that pass law exams (Choi et al., 2022; Katz et al.,
2024; Martínez, 2025), so legal AI needs benchmarks that grade open
text. And they offer to grade answers themselves, as an _LLM judge_
(Zheng et al., 2023; Gu et al., 2026). Since 2025, legal benchmarks and
teaching tools have converged on one design: an expert writes a rubric
for each task, and an LLM checks an answer against it (Shi et al., 2026;
Chen et al., 2026; Drozdov et al., 2026; Al Zubaer et al., 2026; Cope
et al., 2026). At the same time, LLM judges are known to drift between
runs (Haldar and Hockenmaier, 2025), to favour long answers (Saito
et al., 2023), to obey instructions embedded in the answer they grade
(Li et al., 2026), and to reward whatever an optimizer learns about
their disposition (Elganayni et al., 2026). Examination law, data
protection law and the EU AI Act set further limits on what a machine
may decide about a candidate (Bundesverwaltungsgericht, 2019; Court of
Justice of the European Union, 2023; European Parliament and Council of
the European Union, 2024).

The literature on these questions is scattered across law reviews,
educational measurement, natural language processing (NLP) and court
decisions. This paper brings them together. In NLP, an automatic grader
is a predictor of the human grade, validated by its agreement with that
grade. In examination law, a grade is the application of a stated
standard to an answer that may legitimately differ from the reference,
and it must be justified. We adopt the legal definition: a grading
method must apply a stated standard and justify its result, not only
agree with human graders. Our question is _which way of evaluating
open-text legal answers the evidence supports, for which use, and what
must still be shown before it can be trusted_. The evidence is of three
kinds: direct studies of automatic graders of legal answers, rubric
grading and rater research from other domains, and the law that governs
grading (Section 3). Answering the question means asking what a grade
should measure (Section 4), how that performance is translated into
criteria and points (Section 7), and who answers for that translation
and can correct it in the single case (Section 8).

Our contributions are:

1.  1.  Desiderata. Eight requirements for evaluating open-text legal
        answers, from validity theory (Messick, 1995; Kane, 2013) and from
        examination, data protection and AI law (Section 4, Table 1).
2.  2.  A taxonomy of eight paradigms, from unstructured human grading to
        LLM judges with instance-specific rubrics and pairwise comparison,
        with the evidence for and against each (Section 5, Table 2).
3.  3.  A systematization of legal evaluations of automatic graders:
        task-specific content is the strongest lever, agreement is measured
        against different references, and validation is often thin
        (Section 6, Table 3).
4.  4.  A design space of legal rubrics, from the unit of credit to the key
        that maps points to grades, with the evidence and law behind each
        choice (Section 7).
5.  5.  The trustworthy rubric judge: seven safeguards, each tied to a
        weakness, a legal requirement and the test that remains, with the
        uses the evidence supports today (Section 8, Section 9, Section 10).

An analytic rubric written for the task is the strongest lever for
agreement between an automatic grader and human graders. No architecture
wins on every axis: when attorneys ranked answers written at three
quality levels, comparing them in pairs recovered the order better than
rubric scores did (Yang et al., 2026); multiple-choice formats are more
reliable; and no rubric judge is yet robust to manipulation. We argue
that rubric-centred grading, grading that checks each answer against
criteria stated in advance, should still be the default for auditable
legal assessment. Grading is auditable when the standard, its
application to each script and the final decision can each be checked; a
rubric externalizes the standard, makes each criterion application
contestable and leaves the decision with the examiner. These are design
advantages motivated by examination law, not a claim that the law
mandates one architecture. We propose the _trustworthy rubric judge_, a
rubric judge wrapped in seven safeguards, and state the tests it must
pass before it grades exams for credit. A companion study, in
preparation, builds this judge for German law exams (Nagl et al., 2026a)
(Section 10).

## 2. Background: open-text legal answers and how humans grade them

_The tasks._ We call an answer _open-text_ when the candidate writes
free prose that applies law to facts and no answer key can list every
acceptable text. In Germany the core format is the case solution
(_Falllösung_): an opinion that states each question, defines the legal
requirements, subsumes the facts and reaches a result, graded on the
statutory scale from 0 to 18 points (Bundesministerium der Justiz,
1981). Practice tasks include memos, contract drafting and research
answers (Schwarcz et al., 2026; Drozdov et al., 2026) and draft
judgments (Karp et al., 2025). Our core case is the exam; the other
tasks differ in the performance measured, in who sets the standard and
in what is at stake, and we do not claim that the findings transfer.

_Human graders disagree._ In the 1968 study of 17 US law professors, the
mean of all their grades reached a reliability of .96 against .58 for
one professor, so single graders and panel means are different
yardsticks (Klein and Hart, 1968). On 15 scripts of a German
beginner-level mock exam, each graded by 15 or 16 correctors who had the
model solution but no point scheme, the highest and lowest grade for a
script lay 6.47 points apart on average (Hufeld, 2024), and in BenGER, a
benchmark of German case solutions curated on an open platform (Nagl and
Grabmair, 2026), three blind reviewers reached a single-rater ICC(2,1)
of .63 (Nagl et al., 2026b). Grades in the German state exams also
differ by gender and by the origin of a candidate’s name (Towfigh
et al., 2014; Towfigh et al., 2018), and by the _Land_ in which the exam
is taken (Kähler et al., 2017). Grades rise with length (Augustine-Adams
et al., 2016), legible handwriting (Greifeneder et al., 2010) and legal
jargon (Linn et al., 1972), and graders are drawn towards a grade they
have already seen (Vidal Rodeiro, 2007; Attali, 2011).

_Structure helps, but not alone._ An analytic rubric breaks the expected
answer into criteria, each with points or levels; it is
_instance-specific_ when it is written for one task rather than for a
type of task (Dawson, 2017). Rubrics raise agreement between human
raters, especially when they are analytic and task-specific and come
with exemplars or rater training (Jonsson and Svingby, 2007), and
rubrics used formatively improve student performance (Panadero et al.,
2023). In German law, some correctors note what each part of the
expected solution is worth (Frenzel, 2011), and a few make this binding
in a grading sheet (_Bewertungsbogen_), described for take-home papers
(Heidebach, 2015), which narrows the spread between correctors (Table
3). Yet rubrics do not remove rater effects: raters still differ in
severity (Woitschach et al., 2018; Engelhard, 1994), read the same
criteria differently (Bloxham et al., 2016), and without moderation
shared criteria did not make law marking consistent (Hanlon et al.,
2004). High-stakes testing surrounds the rubric with machinery:
calibration on common scripts, hidden scripts with known scores to catch
drifting severity (Olson, 2019; Congdon and McQueen, 2000), independent
double grading and adjudication (Light et al., 2024), which the US
NextGen bar exam now adopts for its written answers (Light et al., 2024;
Bok and Wesenberg, 2024; National Conference of Bar Examiners, 2026).

## 3. Method of this systematization

_Scope._ We draw on three bodies of sources and keep them apart in the
text, since they carry different weight. Direct evidence is work that
grades or evaluates open-text answers to legal tasks, by humans or
machines. Transferred evidence is work from other domains that tests
rubric-based LLM grading against human graders, and the measurement
literature on rubrics and raters. Normative sources are the legal rules
that govern grading: German examination law as the worked example, the
EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and AI Act, and US cases on
academic judgment. We exclude closed formats except where compared with
open text, and supervised essay-scoring models, for which we found no
use on law exams.

_Search._ We searched the ACL Anthology, arXiv, Google Scholar, SSRN,
the ACM Digital Library, legal education journals and German and EU
case-law databases up to 12 October 2026, in English and German, with
keywords on law exam grading, legal benchmarks, rubric and checklist
evaluation, LLM-as-a-judge, automated essay and short-answer scoring,
and exam law, and followed references in both directions; language
models assisted by proposing and retrieving candidate sources. A legal
evaluation enters Table 3 if it grades open-text legal answers
automatically and reports how, or whether, the grader was compared with
humans; reuse of another study’s scripts or rubrics is marked, and so
are preprints.

_Prior surveys._ Surveys and critiques cover LLM judges (Gu et al.,
2026; Chehbouni et al., 2025), essay and short-answer scoring outside
law (Ke and Ng, 2019; Burrows et al., 2015; Gao et al., 2024; Li et al.,
2025a) and legal benchmarks by task and metric (Hu et al., 2026); none
relates grading methods to the law that governs grading or treats the
rubric as the object of design.

_Coding._ For each legal evaluation we recorded the paradigm
(Section 5), the source of the rubric, what the grader was compared
with, and the reported agreement of the grader and of the human graders
(Table 3). Agreement is reported as the source reports it: Pearson’s
$`r`$ or Spearman’s $`\rho`$, Cohen’s $`\kappa`$ or its quadratic
weighted form (QWK), and the intraclass correlation (ICC) for rater
reliability. We rated each paradigm against each desideratum as
supported, partly or under conditions, contradicted, or not tested, from
the evidence discussed in Section 5. The basis of a mark is a comparison
on legal answers, evidence from another domain, a legal source, or our
reading.

## 4. Desiderata

What makes an evaluation good depends on what it is used for. We follow
argument-based validation: a score is valid for a use when each
inference from the answer to the decision is backed by evidence (Kane,
2013; American Educational Research Association et al., 2014). The law
adds requirements of its own, which we state first.

_German examination law._ Exams that control access to a profession fall
under the freedom of occupation (Art. 12(1) of the Basic Law, GG).
Examiners have a margin of appreciation. Each examiner sets their own
standards for a task and must apply them to every script of that task
alike Bundesverfassungsgericht, 1991b, p. 52; Bundesverwaltungsgericht,
2018, para. 8; the law does not demand one scheme shared by all
examiners. On judicial review of an exam decision, courts check in full
whether a defensible answer was marked wrong; examination-specific
judgments of persuasiveness, structure and consistency are reviewed only
for the limits of that discretion, a complete and correct reading of the
script, equal application of the examiner’s standards, and freedom from
improper considerations and contradictions (Bundesverwaltungsgericht,
2018, paras. 9 to 11). The candidate has an _answer latitude_
(_Antwortspielraum_): a solution that is defensible (_vertretbar_) and
argued consistently with weighty reasons must not be marked as wrong
Bundesverfassungsgericht, 1991a, p. 79; Bundesverfassungsgericht, 1991b,
p. 55. Latitude protects the result, not the mark: a defensible but
weakly argued solution may earn fewer points than a stronger one. An
error must also not be counted twice. A follow-on error (_Folgefehler_)
is reasoning that is wrong or open to criticism only because the
candidate took an earlier decision on the path of the solution, and
penalizing it again is an impermissible double counting
(_Doppelverwertung_) (Verwaltungsgericht Braunschweig, 2018); how much a
follow-on error still weighs is left to the examiner’s judgment
(Verwaltungsgericht Lüneburg, 2024). The candidate is owed the essential
reasons for the grade (Bundesverwaltungsgericht, 2019, para. 23), but
has no right to margin notes that justify every single point, and a
point scheme is an aid the examiner may depart from, not a binding rule
(Bundesfinanzhof, 2014). On substantiated objections the examiners
themselves must reconsider their evaluation; no other body may do it for
them, and written reasons do not replace this reconsideration
(Bundesverwaltungsgericht, 2019). The examiner must personally and
directly acquaint themselves with each script and evaluate it
independently; an open second correction, in which the second examiner
sees the first grade, is not forbidden by federal constitutional law
(Bundesverwaltungsgericht, 2002). US courts likewise defer to the
academic judgment of faculty (Supreme Court of the United States, 1978;
Supreme Court of the United States, 1985), so a professor’s evaluation
must stay substantive when a model assists.

_Data protection._ A candidate’s answers and the examiner’s comments on
them are personal data (Court of Justice of the European Union, 2017),
so sending scripts to a model provider needs a legal basis and a
processing contract. Art. 22 GDPR gives a person the right not to be
subject to a decision based solely on automated processing that has
legal or similarly significant effects, unless the decision is necessary
for a contract, authorized by Union or Member State law with suitable
safeguards, or based on explicit consent (European Parliament and
Council of the European Union, 2016). The Court of Justice of the
European Union (CJEU) has held that a score is such a decision when a
third party’s decision draws strongly on it (Court of Justice of the
European Union, 2023), so an examiner who adopts a model grade as given
risks a solely automated decision; human involvement must be meaningful
(Colonna, 2024), and the person may require an explanation of the
procedure and principles applied (Court of Justice of the European
Union, 2025).

_AI regulation._ The AI Act classifies AI systems intended to evaluate
learning outcomes as high-risk (Annex III, point 3(b)), which brings the
provider duties of risk management, data governance, technical
documentation, logging, transparency, human oversight, accuracy and
robustness against manipulation (Arts. 9 to 15) (European Parliament and
Council of the European Union, 2024). A university that uses such a
system is a deployer: it must assign oversight to persons with the
competence and authority to intervene, keep the logs and inform
candidates that the system is used on them (Art. 26); a public
university must also assess the impact on fundamental rights before
first use (Art. 27); and a candidate may demand an explanation of the
system’s role in the decision (Art. 86). Systems developed and put into
service solely for scientific research fall outside the Act (Art. 2(6)),
and a system that only prepares a human evaluation may escape the
high-risk class (Art. 6(3)), but a grade proposal that the examiner
reviews shapes the decision and is unlikely to escape it. The Digital
Omnibus postponed the provider and deployer duties for Annex III systems
to 2 December 2027 (European Parliament and Council of the European
Union, 2026) (law as of October 2026).

### 4.2. Eight desiderata

From the measurement literature and the requirements above we derive
eight desiderata.

- •
  D1 Construct validity. The score supports the interpretation it is
  used for: it reflects the legal skill the task tests, such as spotting
  issues, stating rules, subsuming facts and weighing arguments, and not
  task-irrelevant features such as length, surface style or the grader’s
  identity (Messick, 1995; Williamson et al., 2012). Fairness is part of
  this: the score should not move with task-irrelevant features of a
  candidate’s language, such as phrasing or dialect.
- •
  D2 Reliability. The score changes little with the grader, the run or
  the occasion. In generalizability theory these are separate sources of
  error (Brennan, 2001).
- •
  D3 Reasons. The grade comes with reasons that let the candidate
  understand and contest it (L3, L5); a reason per criterion and passage
  is the form we propose (Section 8).
- •
  D4 Defensible alternatives. A defensible solution that departs from
  the reference is not marked wrong, and an error is not counted again
  in the steps that depend on it (L2).
- •
  D5 Robustness. The score cannot be raised by padding, copying the
  case, citing fake authority or embedded instructions (Powers et al.,
  2002; Chen et al., 2024; Li et al., 2026).
- •
  D6 Cost and scale. The method can grade many answers fast enough for
  feedback and benchmarks.
- •
  D7 Formative value. The output tells a student what was missing and
  why (Hattie and Timperley, 2007).
- •
  D8 Examiner control. The examiner’s standards apply to every script
  alike (L1); the examiner, not the system, decides and reconsiders (L4,
  L5); and the process is documented as the AI Act’s oversight duties
  require (L6).

Whether a deployment is lawful depends on context and procedure, not on
the method alone. The desiderata overlap: D3, D4 and D8, where the law
adds most, all favour designs that make the standard explicit, so a
paradigm’s count of marks is not a score. Agreement with human graders,
the usual yardstick in NLP, is evidence for D1 and D2, not proof: humans
disagree, an automatic grader can match a noisy reference by chance
(Elangovan et al., 2025), and consistency is not validity (Han and Kim,
2026).

## 5. Eight evaluation paradigms

We distinguish eight paradigms by who grades (human or model) and what
the grade is anchored to (nothing, a reference answer, general criteria,
criteria written for the task, or another answer). They are the
configurations found in the literature, not disjoint classes: the design
space behind them has more axes, and real systems mix them, as a
pairwise judge with a rubric does (Cope et al., 2026). Table 2 rates
each paradigm on the desiderata.

_P1 Unstructured human grading (“vibe grading”)._ An expert reads the
answer and gives a grade, perhaps with margin comments. It is the legal
default and the reference most studies validate against, and an expert
can recognize a defensible alternative that no key foresaw. But the two
studies that measured it put single-rater reliability at intraclass
correlations of .58 and .63 (Klein and Hart, 1968; Nagl et al., 2026b),
correctors’ grades spread widely (Hufeld, 2024), and length,
handwriting, identity and prior grades leak into the grade (Section 2).

_P2 Human grading with an analytic rubric._ A rubric makes the standard
explicit, cuts the spread between human graders (Strecker et al., 2026;
Jonsson and Svingby, 2007) and gives students criteria to learn from
(Panadero et al., 2023; Sparrow, 2004). Its weaknesses are cost and the
remaining rater effects, and it credits alternatives only if the rubric
lists them or the grader departs from it.

_P3 Closed formats._ Multiple choice, classification and extraction
tasks are graded exactly and cheaply, which is why most legal NLP
benchmarks use them (Chalkidis et al., 2022; Guha et al., 2023; Zhong
et al., 2020; Fei et al., 2024). But they measure a different construct.
With GPT-4’s help, law students improved by 29 percentile points on
multiple-choice questions and not at all on essays (Choi and Schwarcz,
2025), and GPT-4’s bar exam percentile is lowest on the essays, about
the 42nd against the 62nd overall (Martínez, 2025).

_P4 Similarity to a reference._ Metrics such as ROUGE, BLEU and
BERTScore compare the answer with a reference text (Lin, 2004; Papineni
et al., 2002; Zhang et al., 2020). On legal summaries, experts’ scores
correlated with ROUGE at .13 to .21 and with BERTScore at .07 (Shukla
et al., 2022); the model with the highest ROUGE on case-law analyses
produced the highest rate of unsupported citations (Hou et al., 2025),
and near-identical ROUGE on German court press releases hid very
different expert ranks (Nagl et al., 2025). Similarity cannot give
reasons, punishes a defensible alternative path for using other words,
and rewards copying the reference.

_P5 LLM judge with a reference answer._ The judge compares the answer
with a reference answer written by an expert, as in LEXam (Fan et al.,
2026). Without a reference, judges agree with experts only on questions
they can answer themselves (Krumdick et al., 2026), and judging accuracy
tracks solving accuracy (Tan et al., 2025). With a reference, LEXam’s
judge passed the alternative annotator test against three experts who
agreed with each other at a quadratic weighted kappa of .49 (Calderon
et al., 2025; Fan et al., 2026). A single reference also fixes one path.
In BenGER, the judge disagreed with human graders most on presentation
and on results reached by other reasoning paths than the reference
solution’s (Nagl et al., 2026b), which is exactly the answer latitude
the law protects (L2). Such verdicts are also judge-relative: in our
earlier work, prompts optimized against a strict judge on LEXam overfit
to that judge’s disposition, while prompts optimized against a lenient
judge transferred better (Elganayni et al., 2026).

_P6 LLM judge with generic criteria._ The judge scores every answer on
the same generic dimensions (Liu et al., 2023). In law this fails
without task-specific guidance. Open models grading German criminal-law
case solutions without a sample solution or rubric reached correlations
near zero (Wendlinger et al., 2024), and a G-Eval-style correctness
judge, LeMAJ’s baseline (Table 3), correlated with legal experts at .02
to .08 (Enguehard et al., 2025). Generic dimensions combined with a
reference fare better: BenGER’s judge, which scores each case solution
on ten general criteria of German correction practice against the
reference solution, agreed with the mean of three blind human graders at
$`r`$ = .76, within the graders’ own disagreement (Nagl et al., 2026b),
and a judge with general criteria ranked systems for German court press
releases much as experts did (Nagl et al., 2025). What makes them work
is the task-specific content (P7).

_P7 LLM judge with an instance-specific rubric._ An expert writes, for
each task, the criteria an answer must meet, often as atomic yes-or-no
items, and the judge checks each criterion. Outside law, this design
gives judges high agreement with experts per criterion. On HealthBench’s
34 consensus criteria, GPT-4.1’s agreement with physicians fell within
the range of the physicians’ agreement with each other (Arora et al.,
2025), and similarly on expert rubrics in other fields (Starace et al.,
2025; Wang et al., 2026); controlled comparisons favour
instance-specific criteria over general ones, over a plain prompt and
over free grading (Kim et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2025; Du et al., 2026;
Liu et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2024). In law, the within-domain
comparisons point the same way, with caveats (Table 3). On German exams,
giving GPT-5 the grading scheme raised its agreement with the human
graders more than giving it the sample solution did, and both together
raised it most (Al Zubaer et al., 2026). The gain is not universal: the
same additions lowered the agreement of GPT-5.1, and the scripts are 71
take-home solutions and 16 timed mock-exam scripts reused from earlier
studies (Wendlinger et al., 2024; Strecker et al., 2026). On four US
law-school finals, GPT-5 with the professor’s rubric tracked the
professor’s grades closely and better than with a bare prompt; a score
per rubric element and pairwise comparison with the rubric added nothing
(Cope et al., 2026). On the one question a professor regraded, the two
human gradings were closer to each other than to the model, a gap the
authors do not find significant. On attorney-written binary rubrics for
legal research, judges from three providers agreed with the attorneys’
majority label somewhat better than the attorneys agreed with each
other, a comparison that is not like for like (Drozdov et al., 2026).

The weaknesses are real. Expert rubrics cost time: building each
PLawBench item, rubric included, took about three expert hours (Shi
et al., 2026). Generated rubrics fall short of expert ones (Wang and
Blanco, 2026; Dhole and Agichtein, 2026; Zhou et al., 2026), and
LLM-written elaborations of human rubrics lowered agreement (Sharma
et al., 2025). Finer label schemes lower agreement (Deng et al., 2025;
Sharma et al., 2025) (Section 7). Judges also side with the majority. In
a Thai bar-exam study of reference-based judges, where the grading
regulation was silent, 22 of 26 LLM judges scored in or near the
majority reading of three examiners and none reproduced the defensible
minority reading; the dissent was among graders about the same answer,
not an alternative path taken by the candidate (Akarajaradwong et al.,
2026). And a rubric does not catch every fundamental error. In a Polish
study of the exam for the public-procurement appeals board, a judge
given the committee’s criteria and the correct legal analysis gave three
model-written judgments 85 to 90 of 100 points; the committee had failed
all three for cardinal errors, with 8 to 37 points (Karp et al., 2025).
Grading against a rubric also invites manipulation: instructions
embedded in an answer succeeded in 56.9% of attacks on average across
rubric-based graders (Li et al., 2026).

_P8 Pairwise comparison._ The grader compares two answers and picks the
better one; many comparisons yield a ranking, as in Chatbot Arena (Zheng
et al., 2023) or comparative judgment. This paradigm is the strongest
challenge to rubrics, for one question: ordering quality. In
JudgmentBench, practising attorneys graded answers to 30 BigLaw Bench
tasks written at three quality levels. Their pairwise judgments
recovered the order of the levels almost perfectly, their rubric scores
barely, and the rubric raters agreed with each other only moderately; a
GPT-5.4 autograder tracked the attorneys’ orderings better with pairwise
judgments than with the rubric (Yang et al., 2026) (Table 3). Pairwise
ranking also beat absolute scoring for essays (Shibata and Miyamura, 2025) and resisted universal adversarial phrases better (Raina et al.,
2024). JudgmentBench’s levels were induced by prompting and moved all
dimensions together, and its rubrics were not validated against graded
answers; real answers differ in which issues they treat, which is what
rubrics capture. Pairwise judges show position and length bias (Shi
et al., 2025; Dubois et al., 2024). Above all, a bare preference is
relative: it ranks a script among others but gives no
criterion-referenced reasons, no feedback, and no grade that holds when
the cohort changes. That conflicts with the duty to give reasons (L3)
and with criterion-referenced grading, towards which even the bar exam
is moving (Light et al., 2024). The objection is to the protocol, not to
comparison as such: a pairwise judge with the rubric matched absolute
rubric grading on US exams (Cope et al., 2026), and anchored comparisons
with a reason per criterion are untested. As used today, P8 suits
leaderboards better than exams.

_The field has moved from P3 and P4 to P7._ Legal benchmarks long
reduced law to closed formats or scored generation with overlap or
keyword metrics (Chalkidis et al., 2022; Fei et al., 2024; Li et al.,
2024; Li et al., 2025b). Since 2025, the leading benchmarks and the
practitioners’ own evaluations have adopted instance-specific rubrics,
written or seeded by experts, with an LLM judge (Shi et al., 2026; Chen
et al., 2026; Oh et al., 2026; Drozdov et al., 2026; Grupen et al.,
2026; Vals AI and Legaltech Hub, 2025).

_Task-specific content is the strongest lever, within limits._ Within
the same domain, a scheme or rubric raised the agreement of the same
model, with the caveats of Section 5. Decomposition can also start from
the answer instead of the expected performance: LeMAJ splits a model’s
answer into legal data points and checks each against the source
material without a reference answer (Enguehard et al., 2025). The two
are not the same. A rubric fixes in advance what counts as performance
and who is accountable for that choice. Decomposing the answer only
organizes the checking, omissions included; it supplies neither weights
nor a grade.

_Validation is often thin, and human-level claims depend on the unit of
comparison._ Several rubric benchmarks report agreement between judges
but not with humans (Pires et al., 2026), validate on three candidate
scripts that all passed (Pires et al., 2025), or publish no agreement at
all (Harvey Team, 2024; Grupen et al., 2026). Agreement between judges
is not validity (Rao and Callison-Burch, 2026; Han and Kim, 2026). Few
report how far their human graders agree with each other. Where both
figures are reported, the comparison is rarely like for like: the judge
is scored against a majority or a mean, the humans against each other
(Drozdov et al., 2026; Chlapanis et al., 2025; Nagl et al., 2026b), and
the alternative annotator test is the exception (S5). A panel of three
judges agrees with itself better than three examiners agree with each
other, which does not show that it agrees with them (Akarajaradwong
et al., 2026). The sharpest contrasts come from quality levels
constructed by prompting (Yang et al., 2026). And correlation is not
accuracy: a correlation of one is compatible with every grade three
points too high, harmless for a ranking and decisive for pass or fail.
Table 3 shows judges that agree with human graders, not grades that hold
up in the single case. The next two sections turn to a design that
could.

rubrics, but “rubric” covers very different instruments (Dawson, 2017).

_What earns credit._ Graders of law essays reward the treatment of major
issues more than minor ones (Linn et al., 1972), and a correct result
without the authority behind it is a different achievement from a
correct, authority-grounded answer (Liao, 2026). A rubric that credits
bare results penalizes defensible alternatives and rewards guessing; one
that credits the reasoning (the requirement stated, the facts subsumed
and the argument weighed) fits both legal method and the answer latitude
(L2).

_Granularity and scale._ Yes-or-no verdicts agree better than
partial-credit or finer label schemes (Sharma et al., 2025; Deng et al.,
2025), and on binary expert criteria, experts agree almost perfectly
(Wang et al., 2026). But splitting criteria into ever smaller items does
not always help (Kortemeyer et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2026). Agreement
is not the construct: coarser labels raise agreement partly by
discarding information. The granularity should follow what the step
tests, and the states _not met_, _not applicable_, _uncertain_ and
_check failed_ must stay apart even where the criterion is binary. The
middle ground is a step for each expected legal performance, with a few
binary requirements per step; on US exams the most granular rubric, not
the most granular output, gave the highest agreement, a hypothesis from
four exams (Cope et al., 2026).

_Structure and weights._ German point schemes come in three types. In
the first, every point belongs to a step of the expected solution,
either as absolute points per step (Heidebach, 2015; Frenzel, 2011) or
as a percentage of the total for each item of the outline, so that no
conversion key is needed (Strecker et al., 2026, pp. 12 f.). In the
second, a fixed share goes to steps and a pool is bound to named
criteria, typically method points (_Strukturpunkte_) for structure,
appraisal style and language, for which 15 to 25% is proposed (Strecker
et al., 2026, p. 14); the same criteria can instead be credited within
each section. In the third, the pool is free: the grader distributes it
on an overall impression. A free pool is tied to nothing a second grader
or a model could check and reintroduces the variance the sheet was meant
to remove, so it has no place in a rubric judge. A bounded overall
component that must name what it rewards, for instance up to a tenth of
the points for the weighting of the issues or for an unforeseen but
sound solution, awarded only with a written reason, can be stated,
limited and reviewed; it belongs in the design space as a documented
hybrid (Section 10).

_Alternatives and follow-on errors._ Where a dispute changes only a
result or its reasons, a criterion can credit the argument whatever the
result. Where another defensible decision removes or adds steps, the
rubric needs a separate path whose steps are weighted for equal
performance, not for equal length, and answers that take a path no one
foresaw should go to the examiner. A worked example shows the three
cases a rubric must keep apart. Suppose question A decides whether step
B is reached. If the candidate defensibly answers A in the negative,
step B does not arise and neither earns nor loses points, unless the
task asks for an auxiliary opinion (_Hilfsgutachten_), in which case B
is still expected. If the candidate denies A wrongly, what follows from
that decision is a follow-on error, judged against the candidate’s own
decision with the weight of the wrong decision left to the examiner,
while a new error inside B counts on its own. The rubric must record why
a step is absent, or it credits work not shown, or penalizes a lawful
path for omitting steps it did not need; and a judge must not pick, from
a contradictory answer, the path that scores best. Marking B not
applicable does not settle the total: if A carries four points and B
six, a denominator of ten penalizes the lawful path, and rescaling to
four turns a shorter performance into a full score. Alternative paths
need an examiner’s rule for comparable scoring opportunities and for the
total.

_Cardinal errors._ Examiners disagree on which errors alone should take
a script below the pass mark, and the literature asks each sheet to
define them in advance, with minus points or a grade below the computed
one (Strecker et al., 2026, pp. 15 f.). The Polish study of Section 5
shows the stakes: a judge with the committee’s criteria and solution key
gave near-full marks to judgments the committee had failed for cardinal
errors (Karp et al., 2025). Whether such a rule would have caught them
was not tested; a deduction is itself a grading decision that needs a
reason.

_From points to grade._ The key that maps points to the legal grade
scale is part of the examiner’s evaluation: the performance required for
a passing grade cannot be fixed rigidly without regard to average
results (Bundesverfassungsgericht, 1991b, pp. 51 f.), and once set, it
applies to every script alike (Bundesverwaltungsgericht, 2018, para. 8).
Since models mis-add (Kortemeyer et al., 2024; Baeumel et al., 2025),
the sum and the key belong in code, not in the judge’s output.

_Who writes the rubric, and when._ The examiner sets the standard (L4).
The rubric is fixed before grading; a defect found while grading is
corrected in a new version, the change is documented, and every script
graded under the old version is regraded on the changed criteria (L1).
Who drafts the rubric is a separate question. A model can draft it, but
drafting is more than extracting the reference solution: the solution
does not say which steps earn which points, what can be expected under
exam conditions, which alternatives are equal or which errors weigh
most. A draft should mark, per criterion, whether it restates the task,
the solution or the examiner’s notes, adds an alternative, proposes a
weight or assumes something the examiner must confirm, and the examiner
must review and adopt it (Section 10).

_The rubric we propose._ Table 4 lists our choice first in each row:
credit for arguments and requirements met, not for bare results (L2);
binary criteria with the four states above kept apart; every point on a
step of the expected solution, method and form credited inside each
section so that each point stays tied to checkable content; paths of
their own for alternatives, follow-on errors judged against the
candidate’s own earlier decision, and cardinal errors defined in
advance; the key from points to grade set by the examiner and applied by
code; the rubric written or reviewed by the examiner before grading and
versioned. The next section wraps the judge that applies it in the
safeguards the remaining gaps call for.

## 8. The trustworthy rubric judge

Table 2 shows no paradigm without weaknesses. P7 leads on validity,
reasons and scale, but it is open to manipulation, its reliability is
not guaranteed, and whether it credits alternatives or improves learning
is untested. We call a rubric judge _trustworthy_ when safeguards close
these gaps far enough for the intended use, and we propose seven (Figure
1): two fix what the judge works with and produces (S1, S2), three test
it before and during use (S3 to S5), and two keep the examiner in charge
(S6, S7). Each safeguard has support in the literature, from law or from
other domains; the combination is new, and Section 10 states the tests
it must pass. Most of them would also fit a reference-based or pairwise
judge, but only a rubric makes every reason checkable against a
criterion. The closest prior design outside law is RULERS, a rubric
judge that locks the rubric, checks quoted evidence in code and fits its
scores to human grades by regression (Hong et al., 2026). What is
specific here is the legal standard: alternative paths, follow-on
errors, weights set by the examiner and the examiner’s decision.

S1 An instance rubric of atomic criteria that credits arguments. The
judge applies the rubric of Section 7. The examiner writes or reviews
the criteria for each task (Arora et al., 2025; Drozdov et al., 2026);
credit goes to arguments, alternatives have paths of their own, and
follow-on errors are judged on the candidate’s own decisions (L2). The
rubric is fixed before grading and versioned, so that a correction
reaches every script (L1). The examiner sets the weights and code does
the arithmetic (Kortemeyer et al., 2024). Where a model drafts the
rubric, an expert must review it, since generated rubrics fall short of
expert instance rubrics (Wang and Blanco, 2026; Dhole and Agichtein,
2026). No automatic grader of legal answers has yet been probed with
alternative paths or follow-on errors; S3 makes both a standard probe.

S2 Evidence for every credit, checked in code. The judge must quote the
passage of the answer that earns each credit, and a program verifies
that the quote is in the answer. Three questions are distinct: does the
passage exist, does it mean in context what the judge says, and does
that performance justify the points? A string check answers only the
first. The second needs care: a candidate may state a rule only to
reject it, and an omission has no passage to quote, so it needs a reason
instead. An invalid quote is an error of the grading system, not of the
candidate: it triggers a repeated run, an abstention or the examiner,
never a deduction. The quote gives the examiner and the candidate a
checkable basis for each point (L3), beyond what the law requires
(Bundesfinanzhof, 2014, paras. 8 f.); the second and third questions
remain the examiner’s.

S3 Probes before use. Before grading real answers, judge and rubric must
pass answers with known outcomes. Fake answers come first: an empty
answer, an off-topic one and a copy of the facts must score near zero,
the reference solution must score high, and an answer with embedded
instructions must not gain. Controlled variants of one real answer come
second, each with its permitted change in score fixed in advance:
padding must not raise the score, a paraphrase that keeps the legal
performance must not change it, a defensible alternative path must keep
its credit, the removal of a decisive reason must lose it, an appended
instruction must change nothing, and an invented authority must not
gain. A follow-on error probe changes an upstream decision and keeps
later steps consistent with it: the score may change only as the
examiner’s dependency rule says, and a new error inside a dependent step
must still be found. Known attacks give the adversarial probes (Raina
et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026; Milani et al., 2025;
Chen et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2025; Powers et al., 2002). Probes
answer the AI Act’s demands for testing before use and for robustness
(Arts. 9(8), 15(4) and (5), L6), but show only that known attacks fail.

S4 Repeated runs and variance decomposition. A judge can grade the same
answer differently on another run (Haldar and Hockenmaier, 2025;
Schroeder and Wood-Doughty, 2024). The AI Act asks for declared accuracy
metrics and consistent performance (Art. 15(1) and (3), L6), and equal
standards (L1) ask for the same. One agreement coefficient cannot show
either when the judge’s error has several sources. Generalizability
theory separates these sources, such as runs, judges and rubrics
(Brennan, 2001), and has been applied to LLM raters (Song et al., 2026).
The rubric matters most where rubrics are generated: a poor rubric can
force the same error on every script, so that every grade is consistent
and wrong; less spread is not success. A minimal design grades a sample
of scripts on several runs, under each rubric version and by two judges.
It reports the variance from runs, rubrics and judges against the
variance between scripts, and counts wrong passes and wrong fails apart.
The run and rubric shares must be small before a grade is used.
Self-consistency does not prove validity (Norman et al., 2026;
Sunkavalli, 2026), so S4 complements S5 and does not replace it.

S5 Calibration against independent human grades. The judge is validated
on a sample of answers graded blind and independently by humans, and its
agreement is read against the agreement among the humans, for instance
with the alternative annotator test (Calderon et al., 2025; Nagl et al.,
2026b). The human grades must be independent, since second graders who
see the first grade agree more (Vidal Rodeiro, 2007). Where several
grades are defensible, validation should accept a set of grades rather
than one: judges selected against forced single labels performed up to
31% worse (Guerdan et al., 2025). Calibration and the final test must
use different data, split by task rather than by script if the claim is
about new exams. Calibration must be repeated when the model changes:
model updates shifted judge severity by up to 133 of 1,000 points
(Sunkavalli, 2026). Calibration can mean two things. Improving how the
judge applies the criteria, through prompts, examples or quote checks,
leaves the standard untouched. Fitting the criterion weights or the
point-to-grade key to human scores, as RULERS does by regression (Hong
et al., 2026), changes the standard itself; that is a grading decision,
and the examiner must review and adopt it (L4).

S6 Escalation to the examiner. The judge proposes, and the examiner
decides (L4, L5). For a graded exam the examiner must read every script
personally (Bundesverwaltungsgericht, 2002), so the proposal is a draft,
not a filter. Confidence is not the only flag: answers on an unforeseen
path, contradictions within the answer, criteria the judge could not
apply, failed quote checks, zeroed credits and grades near the pass mark
are flagged whatever the judge’s confidence, since a confidently wrong
grade is the failure that matters. Where the judge is uncertain, it
abstains and leaves the script to the examiner. Such selective grading
can hold the agreement with humans on the graded scripts at a chosen
level, for scripts of the kind it was calibrated on (Jung et al., 2025),
and raised it on one physics exam (Kortemeyer and Nöhl, 2025); the
guarantee is an average over many scripts and says nothing about one
candidate. Second readers are drawn towards a grade they have seen
(Vidal Rodeiro, 2007), so the examiner should form a view before seeing
the model’s grade; this is a method, not a legal duty (Section 4.1).
Whether examiners catch a confidently wrong proposal is the test this
safeguard must pass (Section 10).

S7 Documentation and data protection. Every grade records what produced
it: the rubric version, the judge and model version, the prompts, the
quotes and the checks. It also records the judge’s original proposal and
every human change with its reason, classed as a corrected criterion
application, a rubric change or a reasoned individual decision. This
record serves the logging and transparency duties of the AI Act (Arts.
12, 13 and 26(6), L6), and it is the basis for the explanation of the
procedure and principles actually applied that a candidate may request
where the grade is a solely automated decision (Court of Justice of the
European Union, 2025, paras. 58 to 61). Pseudonymized scripts and local
models are building blocks, not a data protection concept: the
controller, the legal basis, access rights and retention, deletion, and
any external service each need a decision of their own (L7).

The safeguards have costs: in one calculus exam, about 70% of items had
to go to humans to reach human accuracy (Kortemeyer et al., 2025), so
the judge assists human graders and does not replace them.

## 9. Which uses the evidence supports today

The uses differ in stakes and in who bears an error, and the evidence
supports them unequally.

_Formative feedback._ Feedback on practice exams is where the case is
strongest. Rubric use and feedback improve learning (Panadero et al.,
2023; Wisniewski et al., 2020), individual feedback raised law students’
grades (Schwarcz and Farganis, 2017), and the American Bar Association
(ABA) now requires a formative assessment in every course of the first
third of the J.D. (American Bar Association, Section of Legal Education
and Admissions to the Bar, 2026). Whether model-written feedback
improves learning in law is untested; students in German law still
tended to prefer tutor feedback (Hackl et al., 2024), and generous model
grades mislead, so feedback should state its uncertainty.

_Benchmarks of legal AI._ For comparing models, an expert-rubric judge
validated against legal experts is now the best-supported design
(Drozdov et al., 2026; Shi et al., 2026).

_A second reader in graded exams._ A rubric judge that flags scripts
where its total and the examiner’s differ is plausible, and a review of
the largest discrepancies on US exams found errors by the professor as
well as by the model (Cope et al., 2026), but it is barely tested.
German law requires the examiner to decide (L4). Since the features of
the procedure that shape the result, such as the number of examiners and
the handling of their differences, must be fixed by rule
(Bundesverwaltungsgericht, 2019, paras. 15 to 17), we argue that a
machine role that shapes the assessment belongs in the exam regulations
too, as pre-marking by assistants is admissible only where the procedure
provides for it (Bundesverwaltungsgericht, 2002).

_Grading without an examiner._ No study validates it for exams for
credit, and the legal obstacles are two and separate. German examination
law requires the examiner’s personal evaluation (L4), which no exception
in data protection law removes. Art. 22 GDPR adds that a solely
automated grade with legal effect needs one of the exceptions of its
paragraph 2 and the safeguards that go with it (L5), which would have to
be shown for the specific use.

## 10. Open problems

_Who writes the rubric, and how is a generated one judged?_ Expert
rubrics are the bottleneck. Generating them from the reference solution
is the obvious step, but generated rubrics fell short of expert ones in
the comparisons made so far, which generated them without a reference
solution (Wang and Blanco, 2026; Zhou et al., 2026), the one legal set
generated from expert solutions was only partly reviewed (Oh et al.,
2026), and whether expert review can close the gap at acceptable cost is
open; rubrics refined against human-scored answers matched detailed
human-written ones in essay scoring (Harada et al., 2025), with the
caveat of S5. A study of generated legal rubrics must validate three
things apart: the rubric itself (does it measure the performance the
task tests?), its application to real scripts (does the judge apply it
correctly?), and the whole workflow with the examiner in it (does the
final grade improve?). It needs strong baselines (an independent expert
sheet, generated rubrics before and after expert revision, and a judge
without rubric), a split by task rather than by script, the expert sheet
as a reference rather than the only right answer, so that deviations are
classified before they count as errors, and endpoints beyond
correlation: wrong passes and fails, criterion errors, alternatives and
follow-on errors, robustness to the probes of S3 and the examiner’s
time. Our companion study on one German exam is the first step on this
programme (Nagl et al., 2026a).

_Holistic quality._ Some legal quality, such as weighing, judgment and
persuasiveness, may resist criteria fixed in advance (Sadler, 2009; Yang
et al., 2026). Hybrids with a bounded, reasoned overall component or a
pairwise component (Section 7) need testing.

_Teaching to the rubric._ Students given rubric feedback may learn the
rubric, not the law, as prompts optimized against a judge learn its
disposition (Elganayni et al., 2026); whether skill or only scores
improve is open.

## 11. Limitations

Our search is structured but not exhaustive, and several of the most
relevant studies are 2026 preprints. The ratings in Table 2 are one
author’s reading of uneven evidence, much of it from outside law.
Several entries in Table 3 share data or instruments (marked _reused_)
and BenGER is our own, so the number of papers overstates the number of
independent confirmations. German law is the worked example, and other
jurisdictions constrain grading differently. Five cited works are our
own, one of them in preparation (Nagl et al., 2026b; Nagl and Grabmair,
2026; Nagl et al., 2025; Elganayni et al., 2026; Nagl et al., 2026a),
and the author works on rubric judges, which may bias the reading
towards them. To limit this, every study, our own included, is coded on
the same fields in Table 3.

## 12. Conclusion

Evidence from law, education and NLP shows that a rubric written for the
task is the strongest lever for agreement with human graders, and
examination law shows why rubric-centred grading is the family to build
on. The evidence does not yet show that any judge credits defensible
alternatives, counts a follow-on error once, resists manipulation, or is
corrected by examiners when confidently wrong. The trustworthy rubric
judge is our answer: seven safeguards, each tied to a legal requirement,
and a test programme for what is not yet shown. Today it is best placed
for feedback and benchmarks, and as an assistant, not a replacement,
where credit is at stake.
