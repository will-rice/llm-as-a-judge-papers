# Beyond Prompts: A Systematic Study of LLM-Based Machine Translation Evaluators

Dmitry Popov    Roman Bokhyan    Ekaterina Enikeeva    Artem Mekhraliev
   Stepan Vysotsky    Nikolay Karpachev$`\clubsuit`$
Yandex,$`\diamondsuit`$ HSE University

###### Abstract

LLM-based evaluators have rapidly become a standard tool for machine
translation evaluation, yet the design choices that shape their behavior
remain poorly understood. We present a systematic study of how
instruction design, few-shot demonstrations, backbone model choice, and
multi-step workflows affect span-level and ranking-based MT evaluation.
We collect new expert RATE annotations on WMT24 and WMT25
English–Russian translations, with transfer validation on MQM- and
ESA-annotated benchmarks across five additional language pairs. Our
experiments reveal four robust findings: instruction structure shapes
evaluator behavior, high-quality demonstrations matter more than
quantity, frontier backbones exhibit distinct evaluator profiles
(recall-, precision-, or ranking-oriented), and fusing outputs across
diverse backbones outperforms single-evaluator multi-call setups.
Although evaluator design exposes a large combinatorial space, several
discovered design principles transfer consistently across annotation
protocols and language pairs. We synthesize these findings into Orbit, a
family of LLM-based MT evaluators. Our strongest configurations achieve
expert-level performance in error span detection and in Accuracy and
Fluency ranking at roughly $`20\times`$ lower cost than expert human
annotation.

## 1 Introduction

Machine translation (MT) quality has improved rapidly in recent years,
with frontier LLM-based systems approaching professional human
translation quality on many language pairs and domains (Kocmi et al.,
2024a). As translation quality improves, evaluation increasingly becomes
the bottleneck: identifying subtle translation errors requires trained
annotators, substantial annotation time, and careful quality control.
Automatic metrics remain useful at scale, but still lag behind expert
human judgment on fine-grained translation assessment, particularly for
span-level error localization and diagnostic analysis (Lavie et al.,
2025; Freitag et al., 2021).

Large language models have recently emerged as strong MT evaluators
(Kocmi and Federmann, 2023b). Prior work has explored both score-based
approaches, which predict a single quality score for a translation
(Juraska et al., 2024), and span-based approaches, which attempt to
identify concrete translation errors following human evaluation
protocols such as MQM (Freitag et al., 2021) and ESA (Kocmi et al.,
2024b). However, evaluator design choices — prompt structure,
demonstrations, workflow decomposition, and aggregation — are typically
treated as implementation details, despite substantial evidence from
in-context learning research that demonstration design strongly affects
downstream behavior (Liu et al., 2022; Agrawal et al., 2023). PrExMe
explores prompt variation for scalar-score MT evaluation (Leiter and
Eger, 2024). We instead study protocol-grounded evaluator design for
fine-grained span-level and ranking-based assessment against expert
annotations.

This leaves a central question largely unexplored: which evaluator
design choices robustly improve LLM-based MT evaluation quality across
datasets, protocols, and annotator settings?

In this work, we study LLM-based MT evaluation as a structured evaluator
design problem rather than as a fixed prompting configuration. We
conduct a systematic study across four major design dimensions:
instruction design, few-shot demonstration design, backbone model
selection, and multi-step evaluator workflows. Our focus is span-level
MT evaluation under the RATE protocol (Popov et al., 2025), which
provides fine-grained error spans, severity annotations, and separate
Accuracy and Fluency judgments.

To support this study, we collect new expert annotations on
English–Russian WMT24 and WMT25 translations under the RATE protocol.
Each translation is independently annotated by multiple professional
linguists, enabling direct evaluation of error span detection against
human annotations rather than relying only on scalar quality-score
correlations. Span-level evaluation is particularly important for
diagnostic MT assessment, since it localizes concrete translation
failures and provides actionable feedback for both system developers and
human editors.

Our experiments reveal four robust findings: instruction structure
shapes evaluator behavior, high-quality demonstrations matter more than
quantity, frontier backbones exhibit distinct evaluator profiles
(recall-, precision-, or ranking-oriented), and multi-model fusion
substantially outperforms single-call evaluation. Although evaluator
design exposes a large combinatorial space, several discovered design
principles transfer consistently across annotation protocols and
language pairs. Across these experiments, our strongest configurations
consistently exceed assessor-level agreement and match expert-level
error span detection performance (Table 1).

We encode these design principles in Orbit, a family of evaluator
configurations instantiating the most robust choices identified in our
study. We refer to the resulting single-call and multi-call
configurations as Orbit-SC and Orbit-MC respectively. Our strongest
configurations achieve expert-level error span detection performance.
Orbit-MC also reaches expert-level Accuracy and Fluency ranking
(Table 7) and exceeds assessor agreement on MQM ranking (Table 1). We
additionally provide a preliminary study of how expert annotators
interact with LLM-generated reports in collaborative annotation
workflows (Appendix H).

Our contributions are: (i) A systematic study of evaluator design for
fine-grained LLM-based MT evaluation. (ii) Evidence that several
evaluator design principles transfer across annotation protocols (RATE,
MQM, ESA), annotator pools, and five additional WMT language directions:
en-de, zh-en, cs-de, en-it, and en-zh. (iii) New expert RATE annotations
on WMT24 and WMT25 English--Russian translations spanning multiple
annotator expertise levels.¹¹ 1

## 2 Background and Related Work

### 2.1 Human MT Evaluation Protocols

MQM (Lommel et al., 2014; Freitag et al., 2021) and ESA (Kocmi et al.,
2024b) serve as de facto gold standards in WMT evaluation campaigns.
Both ask annotators to identify error spans in translations. MQM
additionally assigns each span an error category and severity, which are
aggregated into a final score, while ESA removes categories and instead
uses spans as guidance for a final 0–100 quality score.

RATE (Popov et al., 2025) extends this setup with five-point severity
labels, a streamlined semantic/non-semantic taxonomy, separate Accuracy
and Fluency scores, and an overall score derived from span severities.

### 2.2 LLM-Based MT Evaluation

LLM-based evaluation has emerged as a general paradigm for natural
language assessment (Zheng et al., 2023; Gu et al., 2026; Liu et al.,
2023; Bavaresco et al., 2025). In MT evaluation, existing approaches can
be broadly divided into score-based, span-based, and supervised methods.

Score-based approaches such as GEMBA-DA (Kocmi and Federmann, 2023b) and
MetricX (Juraska et al., 2024) predict a single quality score for a
translation segment. Prompt-based span evaluators more closely follow
human annotation workflows by asking LLMs to identify translation errors
directly. Canonical examples include GEMBA-MQM (Kocmi and Federmann,
2023a) and AutoMQM (Fernandes et al., 2023), which prompt LLMs using
MQM-style instructions and a small set of demonstrations. Subsequent
work extends this paradigm with decomposition, post-editing validation,
and rubric-based prompting (Lu et al., 2025; Kim, 2025; Lu et al.,
2024). Multi-agent approaches such as M-MAD (Feng et al., 2025) further
decompose evaluation into interacting specialist agents.

PrExMe (Leiter and Eger, 2024) systematically explores more than 720
prompt templates for open-source LLM-based MT and summarization metrics,
showing that seemingly minor prompt and output-format choices can alter
metric behavior. Our study has a different focus: we measure direct
agreement with multiple professional experts on error localization and
severity, and jointly study demonstrations, backbone choice, and
multi-call workflows in addition to instruction variation.

Supervised approaches instead train models on MQM-style annotations to
predict scores, spans, or both. For example, xCOMET (Guerreiro et al., 2024) combines sentence-level scoring with span detection, while recent
work such as GemSpanEval (Juraska et al., 2025) adapts frontier LLMs to
span-level evaluation tasks.

Span-level results are sensitive to annotation quality and annotator
expertise, while systematic comparisons against multiple professional
experts remain limited (Freitag et al., 2021; Lavie et al., 2025).

Concurrent work by Perrella et al. (2026) proposes alternative
span-matching formulations, which we discuss in Appendix E.

## 3 Experimental Setup

### 3.1 Design Study Protocol

Evaluator design exposes a large combinatorial space of potentially
interacting decisions, including instruction structure, few-shot
demonstration selection, workflow decomposition, aggregation, and
backbone model choice. Exhaustive joint optimization across all
dimensions is impractical. We therefore study design dimensions
sequentially, fixing variables after each stage to isolate the effect of
subsequent decisions. Our objective is not to find a universally optimal
evaluator configuration, but to identify design choices that
consistently improve span-level MT evaluation quality and transfer
across protocols and language pairs.

### 3.2 Dataset and Annotation Setup

We collect new annotations on 600 source–translation pairs from
WMT24 Kocmi et al. (2024a) and WMT25 Kocmi et al. (2025) English–Russian
translations, extending the RATE-annotated coverage to newer frontier
LLM translation systems. Detailed dataset statistics are in Appendix A.

##### Sources and translations.

Source segments are sampled from WMT24 and WMT25 across multiple
domains. We select 300 source segments and pair each with two
translations, enabling segment-level pairwise ranking accuracy
(Section 3.4) alongside span-level evaluation. Rather than sampling
uniformly, we apply diversity sampling to ensure coverage across
translation systems and quality levels, producing a challenging
evaluation set.

##### Annotation layers and human baselines.

Each translation receives annotation at three levels of expertise.
Professional expert linguists annotate the dataset under the full RATE
protocol in implicit side-by-side mode, yielding three independent
expert annotations per segment (Expert $`\times`$ 3). Assessors —
Russian-native annotators with verified C1 English proficiency but no
formal translation training — follow the same protocol (Assessor). We
additionally include standard ESA annotations from the WMT evaluation
campaign (WMT annotator), providing a link to the public benchmark
setting.

For evaluation, we keep two of the three expert annotations as gold
references. Human*(E) represents pairwise expert agreement, computed by
evaluating the third expert against each gold annotation and averaging —
the same procedure used for all evaluated systems. Human*(A-SbS) and
Human*(A-PW) correspond to assessors evaluated in implicit side-by-side
and point-wise setups respectively, while Human*(WMT) follows standard
ESA annotation conditions. Full annotator criteria and statistics are in
Appendix C.

##### Few-shot pool.

Separately from the evaluation set, we construct a dedicated few-shot
calibration pool consisting of 8 source segments across multiple
domains, each translated by 12 systems. Professional linguists annotate
the pool collaboratively under the RATE protocol with consensus
reconciliation, yielding more reliable demonstrations than
single-annotator outputs. This pool is used exclusively for few-shot
selection (Section 4.2) and does not overlap with the evaluation set.

##### Multilingual validation.

To test whether discovered evaluator design choices transfer beyond
English–Russian and the RATE protocol, we additionally evaluate our best
single-call configuration on five WMT language directions with publicly
available annotations. From the WMT23 Metrics Shared Task (Freitag
et al., 2023), we use MQM annotations on en-de and zh-en, sampling 450
examples per direction. From the WMT25 evaluation shared task (Lavie
et al., 2025), we use ESA annotations on cs-de, en-it, and en-zh,
sampling 300 examples per direction. Full results are in Section 5.

### 3.3 LLM Baselines

We compare against baselines representing several major evaluator
paradigms: concise prompting, detailed protocol prompting,
decomposition-based workflows, and supervised span-level evaluation.

Prompt-based baselines include GEMBA-MQM (Kocmi and Federmann, 2023a),
GEMBA-ESA, a reference-free LLM judge used in the WMT25 General MT
shared task (Kocmi et al., 2025), and UMB-v5, our abbreviation for the
unified-mqm-boosted-v5 schema released with Perrella et al. (2026). The
latter is a detailed prompt that thoroughly describes the annotation
task. We additionally include MQM-AE and ESA-AE as our own adaptations
of MQM and ESA human annotation guidelines into LLM prompts, keeping
deviations from the originals minimal. As a supervised baseline we
include xCOMET-XXL (Guerreiro et al., 2024), trained on MQM-style
annotations to predict both scores and spans. All evaluation prompts are
available in our [Hugging Face
release](https://huggingface.co/datasets/yandex/orbit-mt-eval). Omitted
methods are discussed in Appendix D.

### 3.4 Evaluation Metrics

We evaluate MT evaluators along two dimensions: span-level error
detection and segment-level pairwise ranking accuracy. Throughout the
paper, MQM scores are derived from RATE annotations following Popov
et al. (2025): RATE severity levels 2–3 map to MQM minor errors and
levels 4–5 to major errors. As in the original RATE-to-MQM conversion,
RATE severity 1 (“negligible or arguable” errors) is excluded because
standard MQM contains no equivalent severity level.

##### Span-level metrics.

Predicted error spans are matched to gold annotations via bipartite
matching with character overlap (MP with $`\tau{=}1`$). We report
micro-averaged recall $`R`$, precision $`P`$, and harmonic mean
$`F_{1}`$, following Perrella et al. (2026). Segment-level
macro-averaging gives equal weight to each segment regardless of its
number of annotated errors and can therefore overstate performance for
low-recall evaluators. To evaluate severe-error behavior, we
additionally report two complementary diagnostics: $`R_{4+}`$, recall
over gold spans of severity $`\geq 4`$ regardless of predicted severity,
and $`P_{4+}`$, precision for predictions assigned severity $`\geq 4`$
against gold major-error spans. For MQM- and ESA-annotated data,
$`R_{4+}`$ and $`P_{4+}`$ are computed over major and critical errors.
We verify robustness under alternative span-matching formulations in
Appendix E.

##### Ranking accuracy.

For each pair of translations of the same source, we measure whether the
evaluator correctly predicts which translation is better across Accuracy
(Acc), Fluency (FL), and MQM dimensions.

## 4 Systematic Study of LLM Evaluation Design

### 4.1 Instruction Design

How closely should evaluator instructions mirror human annotation
protocols? Existing prompt-based evaluators adopt substantially
different assumptions: GEMBA-MQM and AutoMQM rely on concise
language-agnostic prompts, while UMB-v5 follows the opposite strategy
with a detailed protocol-oriented specification. Neither line of work
ablates the instruction content itself, leaving the relationship between
protocol detail and evaluator behavior unmeasured.

We compare three families of protocol-adapted prompts (MQM-AE, ESA-AE,
RATE-AE), with RATE-AE ablated into three variants of decreasing detail:
RATE-AE*(full+ex) contains full category definitions with inline
error-span examples (as category-boundary disambiguators, not few-shot
demonstrations), RATE-AE*(full) keeps definitions and severity anchors
without examples, and RATE-AE\_(brief) compresses to a minimal task
description, severity scale, and output format. All experiments in this
and the following section (Section 4.2) use GPT-5.2 with temperature 0.

Table 2 shows large differences across instruction variants, with span
F1 ranging from 27.0 (GEMBA-MQM) to 39.3 (RATE-AE*(full+ex)). MQM- and
ESA-style prompts achieve high precision but low recall, including on
high-severity errors — indicating genuinely missed problems rather than
annotation granularity differences. GEMBA systems exhibit similar
behavior despite using few-shot demonstrations. The strongest RATE
variant improves span F1 and major-error recovery over the protocol
baselines in this GPT-5.2 comparison. Appendix G further shows that all
tested RATE variants outperform the protocol baselines on F1 for Claude
Opus 4.6 and Gemini 3 Flash. This effect is not specific to
RATE-annotated gold: RATE-AE*(brief) also outperforms MQM-AE on F1 and
$`R_{4+}`$ when evaluated against MQM-annotated WMT data (Section 5).

Within the RATE family, the relationship between instruction detail and
evaluator quality is non-monotonic: removing the inline examples is
costly, with RATE-AE*(full) falling behind even the much shorter
RATE-AE*(brief). This indicates that detailed taxonomy definitions alone
overconstrain evaluator behavior, while compact instructions preserving
only task-critical guidance generalize more effectively. At the same
time, the strong performance of RATE-AE\_(full+ex) indicates that
detailed protocols remain useful when augmented with concrete examples,
foreshadowing the demonstration-based calibration we study in
Section 4.2. Ranking accuracy remains stable across variants, indicating
that segment-level judgments are less sensitive to instruction structure
than to the backbone (Section 4.3).

Overall, human annotation guidelines transfer effectively to LLM
evaluators, but exhaustive taxonomy descriptions become less important
once high-quality demonstrations or compact task specifications are
available. We use RATE-AE\_(full+ex) as our primary zero-shot setup and
revisit this choice in Section 4.2.

### 4.2 Few-Shot Design

#### 4.2.1 Does Annotation Quality Matter?

We compare three sources of few-shot demonstrations: WMT25 shared task
ESA annotations (manually mapped to the RATE taxonomy and severity scale
by our expert annotators), single-expert RATE annotations on WMT24
from Popov et al. (2025), and our collaboratively curated annotations
produced by multiple experts with consensus reconciliation.

Table 3 shows that demonstration quality matters substantially more than
demonstration presence alone. WMT annotations push F1 below the
zero-shot baseline across all three runs (37.0 vs 39.3 mean), suggesting
that noisy demonstrations can actively mislead the evaluator about what
constitutes a translation error. RATE-WMT24 broadly matches the
zero-shot baseline on mean F1. Only our collaboratively curated
annotations consistently improve both overall span recovery and
major-error detection.

In contrast, ranking metrics improve over the zero-shot baseline more
uniformly across demonstration sources, indicating that span-level
evaluator behavior is substantially more affected by demonstration
design than pairwise ranking judgments. We therefore fix our curated
annotation pool for all subsequent few-shot experiments.

#### 4.2.2 How Many Examples?

With the demonstration source fixed, we next vary the number of examples
($`k\in\{1,2,4,8\}`$, Figure 1).

Increasing $`k`$ produces only modest changes in mean performance.
Instead, the primary effect of larger few-shot sets is variance
reduction: $`k{=}1`$ exhibits noticeably less stable behavior, while
$`k\geq 2`$ yields broadly consistent results across independently
sampled configurations. The most consistent mean trend is a slight
improvement in R₄₊, suggesting that additional demonstrations primarily
improve major-error recovery rather than general error detection. We
adopt $`k{=}2`$ for the remaining experiments, revisiting selection
strategy in Section 4.2.3.

#### 4.2.3 Coverage-Based Selection

The previous experiments showed that evaluator behavior depends strongly
on demonstration design, motivating a more structured selection
strategy. We construct compact few-shot sets that span the protocol’s
error categories and severity range. We evaluate $`k\in\{2,3,4\}`$,
corresponding to approximately 85%, 96%, and 100% category/severity
coverage.

Performance is broadly comparable across $`k`$, with a mild
recall–precision trade-off: smaller $`k`$ favors recall, while larger
$`k`$ improves severity-aware precision at the cost of recall. This is
consistent with the earlier observation that demonstration quantity has
limited effect on mean performance, primarily reducing run-to-run
variance.

The coverage criterion itself is defined without optimizing metric
scores. We select $`k{=}2`$ as the smallest configuration with
comparable validation performance, balancing quality, prompt complexity,
and inference cost.

Revisiting instruction choice under this setup reverses the zero-shot
result from Section 4.1: RATE-AE*(brief) now slightly surpasses
RATE-AE*(full+ex) on span F1, suggesting that high-quality few-shot
demonstrations partially substitute for detailed taxonomy descriptions.
Instruction design nevertheless remains important: replacing the
RATE-style prompts with GEMBA-MQM or GEMBA-ESA substantially reduces
span-level quality even under the same few-shot configuration.

We refer to this single-call configuration — RATE-AE\_(brief) paired with
coverage-based $`k{=}2`$ demonstrations — as Orbit-SC. Section 4.3
evaluates this configuration across a range of frontier LLM backbones.

### 4.3 Model Comparison

How do frontier LLM backbones compare as MT evaluators? Using the
Orbit-SC configuration, we evaluate a range of frontier models as
evaluator backbones. Table 5 reports the main results. Extended metrics
are provided in Appendix F.

Different frontier models exhibit distinct evaluator profiles, with no
single backbone dominating all evaluation dimensions. Recent GPT models
are recall-oriented: GPT-5.4 achieves the strongest span-level recovery
and major-error detection, while GPT-4.1 provides similar recall
behavior at substantially lower cost. Claude Opus 4.6 exhibits the
strongest precision-oriented profile, leading on severity-aware
precision and direct-assessment ranking while remaining competitive on
span F1. Gemini 3.1 Pro combines ranking and precision strengths: it
achieves the strongest MQM ranking accuracy together with the highest
overall precision among span-level evaluators.

At the other end of the spectrum, Grok-4 and DeepSeek-V3.2 substantially
over-predict errors at a large precision cost, with DeepSeek-V3.2 in
particular flagging more than twice as many errors per segment as
Human\_(E). The remaining open-weight models (DeepSeek-R1, Kimi K2
variants, Qwen-235B) sit consistently below the top tier across both
span-level and ranking-based evaluation.

Overall, strong evaluator quality is multi-dimensional: different models
favor different trade-offs between recall, precision, severity
calibration, and ranking behavior. We adopt GPT-5.4 as the default
backbone in the remaining experiments. Appendix G repeats the matched
design comparison on Claude Opus 4.6 and Gemini 3 Flash to test whether
the broader design conclusions transfer beyond GPT-5.4.

### 4.4 Point-Wise vs. Side-by-Side Evaluation

Human MT evaluation is commonly conducted in an implicit side-by-side
(SbS) setting, where one annotator sees multiple translations of the
same source (Freitag et al., 2021). Our main Orbit-SC setup is
point-wise (PW): each call evaluates one source–translation pair
independently. We adapt it to SbS by placing two translations of the
same source in one call and returning a separate report for each. The
few-shot sources remain fixed, with the second translation added to each
demonstration.

Table 6 shows a clear trade-off. SbS improves Accuracy and Fluency
ranking for all three backbones and improves or matches MQM ranking, but
does not improve span $`F_{1}`$. Paired context shifts predictions
toward higher precision and lower recall. PW is therefore convenient for
evaluating an individual MT system, whereas SbS is better suited to
ranking a fixed set of systems. Reversing the presentation order
produced no statistically significant differences.

### 4.5 Multi-Call Workflows

We next study multi-call workflows as a design dimension: do additional
LLM calls improve evaluator quality? We compare three approaches: task
decomposition, iterative self-review, and fusion across multiple
evaluators (Table 7).

#### 4.5.1 Task decomposition

Inspired by M-MAD-style decomposition (Feng et al., 2025), we split
annotation into semantic and non-semantic passes. Concatenating both
outputs improves recall, particularly for major errors, but
substantially hurts precision due to overlapping and conflicting
annotations. LLM fusion recovers precision at the cost of recall. In our
setting, decomposition alone provides limited benefit over the
single-call baseline.

#### 4.5.2 Self-review

We add a second-pass reviewer that re-examines the first-pass output,
either only adding missed spans or also editing existing predictions.
Self-review consistently improves recall, especially on major errors,
but also introduces borderline predictions that reduce precision.
Allowing the reviewer to edit existing spans partially recovers this
precision loss and yields a small F1 gain over the single-call setup.

#### 4.5.3 Model-diversity fusion

Fusion across diverse evaluator backbones produces the strongest overall
gains. We refer to this multi-call configuration — fusing Claude Opus
4.6, GPT-5.4, and Gemini 3.1 Pro outputs via Claude Opus 4.6 as fuser —
as Orbit-MC. Orbit-MC matches or exceeds expert-to-expert agreement on
span F1 and major-error recovery, while maintaining strong precision on
high-severity errors.

Unlike fixed-label ensembles, these structured reports do not provide a
canonical unit for majority voting: models may identify the same error
with different span boundaries and descriptions, or assign conflicting
categories, severities, and segment scores. A deterministic rule would
therefore first require a separate span-matching policy and arbitrary
conflict-resolution choices. The semantic fuser is used precisely to
perform this reconciliation.

Fusion quality depends strongly on the fuser backbone itself. Using the
same three constituent models, GPT-5.4 as fuser falls several F1 points
below Claude, while Gemini 3.1 Pro as fuser produces the strongest MQM
ranking accuracy. Claude substantially outperforms GPT-5.4 as a fusion
model despite weaker standalone recall (Section 4.3), suggesting that
strong evaluator generation and strong evaluator aggregation are
distinct capabilities.

#### 4.5.4 Sources of diversity

Within model-diversity fusion, expanding from two to three constituent
models improves overall quality, while expanding the fusion set to five
models by adding recall-oriented backbones (Grok-4, Kimi-K2) further
increases recall but reduces precision and F1, consistent with the
over-prediction tendencies observed in Section 4.3.

Temperature and strategy diversity also improve over the single-call
baseline but produce consistently smaller gains than model diversity,
indicating that complementary evaluator profiles are more valuable than
repeated sampling from the same backbone.

Overall, multi-call workflows benefit most from genuine evaluator
diversity rather than from iteration or decomposition over a single
backbone. The multi-call configuration Orbit-MC therefore adopts
three-model fusion (Claude Opus 4.6, GPT-5.4, Gemini 3.1 Pro) via Claude
as fuser, achieving the strongest span-detection profile across all
tested configurations.

### 4.6 Cost and Deployment Trade-offs

Table 8 compares human and API costs at a common scale of 10,000
evaluated segments. We use this volume as a representative
data-collection campaign for training or adapting an automatic evaluator
rather than a one-off evaluation set. A segment is one
source–translation pair. PW is the standard Orbit-SC setup, with one
pair per call. Its SbS adaptation places two translations of the same
source in one call and returns a separate report for each. The human PW
and SbS conditions use the same visibility distinction. Thus, 300 SbS
tasks and 600 PW tasks both yield 600 segment-level reports.

Human estimates use observed aggregate labor for one annotator pass
rather than extrapolating median completion times. Scaling these totals
to 10,000 reports gives roughly 3,542 expert-hours in SbS, 1,625
assessor-hours in SbS, and 1,717 assessor-hours in PW. Monetary
estimates are rounded to the nearest \$5. The Orbit-MC total includes
three evaluators and the fuser.

At this scale, one pass by a professional expert costs about \$48,920.
An assessor pass costs \$6,500 in SbS and \$6,865 in PW. Orbit-MC costs
roughly \$1,720, corresponding to a $`4\times`$ cost reduction from the
least expensive assessor setup and a $`28\times`$ reduction from
professional expert RATE. Point-wise Orbit-SC costs \$20–\$385 depending
on the backbone, a further $`4`$–$`86\times`$ reduction relative to
Orbit-MC while retaining the strong results in Table 5. For GPT-5.4, SbS
costs roughly \$75 compared with \$85 for separate PW calls. The
corresponding quality comparison is reported in Section 4.4.

These dollar totals still understate the practical gap. Human evaluation
requires recruiting and screening annotators who are native speakers of
the target language and have C1/C2 proficiency in the source language.
For expert pools, formal training or a degree in linguistics or
translation is also preferred. Protocol training, calibration,
scheduling, and ongoing quality control add further overhead (Freitag
et al., 2021; Kocmi et al., 2024b; Popov et al., 2025). Much of this
infrastructure must be established again for each language direction,
which delays evaluation and makes procedures harder to standardize
across pools. API-based evaluation can reuse a common workflow across
directions, although model quality may still vary across languages and
domains.

## 5 Multilingual Transfer

We test whether the evaluator design choices identified on
English–Russian transfer across annotation protocols, annotation
densities, and language pairs. We evaluate Orbit-SC and Orbit-MC on
WMT23 MQM (en-de, zh-en) and WMT25 ESA (cs-de, en-it, en-zh) benchmarks
using the same inference-time configurations developed on RATE
English–Russian data. Human₂ denotes a second annotator scored against
the gold annotation.

Across both MQM and ESA benchmarks (Table 9), the main findings of our
design study transfer consistently beyond the English–Russian setting.
Orbit-SC remains competitive across all evaluated language pairs, while
Orbit-MC preserves its strong recall-oriented profile, improving
major-error recovery over Orbit-SC on en-de and remaining comparable on
zh-en.

Compact RATE-style prompting remains competitive on MQM. Replacing the
en-ru few-shot pool with direction-specific demonstrations (DS) provides
no substantial gain over Orbit-SC on either MQM direction.

Notably, the en-ru pool matches direction-specific demonstrations on
en-de span $`F_{1}`$ while improving $`R_{4+}`$ and MQM, and improves
all three metrics on zh-en. These results suggest that the advantage
comes not from Russian itself but from how the pool was constructed. The
pool covers diverse error categories and severities, with annotations
collaboratively reconciled by multiple experts.

Transfer to ESA benchmarks exhibits substantially lower precision across
all evaluators, likely reflecting the much sparser annotation density of
ESA compared to MQM and RATE. On span $`F_{1}`$, Orbit-MC provides
smaller gains than on MQM and is slightly below Orbit-SC on en-it and
en-zh, reflecting that multi-model fusion expands prediction sets in a
regime where additional spans are heavily penalized. Nevertheless,
transfer gains remain clearest on major-error recovery, where Orbit-MC
consistently exceeds Human₂ across all ESA directions.

## 6 Conclusion

We systematically study how instruction design, demonstrations, backbone
choice, and multi-call workflows shape LLM-based MT evaluation across
RATE, MQM, ESA, and multiple language pairs. Curated, collaboratively
reconciled demonstrations can match or outperform larger pools, model
families show distinct evaluator profiles, and fusion yields the
strongest overall results. Orbit instantiates these findings as single-
and multi-call evaluators. Its strongest configurations achieve
expert-level error span detection and Accuracy/Fluency ranking, while
Orbit-MC exceeds assessor agreement on MQM ranking.

## Limitations

##### Language coverage.

Our main design study is conducted on a single high-resource language
pair (English–Russian). We validate the resulting Orbit-SC and Orbit-MC
configurations on five additional WMT directions (en-de and zh-en for
MQM, and cs-de, en-it, and en-zh for ESA), but coverage of low-resource
pairs, non-Latin scripts beyond Russian and Chinese, and language
families outside Indo-European and Sino-Tibetan remains limited.
Findings on instruction language and few-shot example selection in
particular may require adaptation for typologically distant target
languages.

##### Closed-source dependencies.

Our strongest configurations (Orbit-SC with GPT-5.4, Orbit-MC fusing
Claude Opus 4.6, GPT-5.4, and Gemini 3.1 Pro) depend on closed-source,
API-only models whose weights and training procedures are not public.
This affects reproducibility (exact behaviour may shift across silent
model updates) and longevity (specific model snapshots may be
deprecated). We mitigate the reproducibility concern through the broad
cross-model comparison in Section 4.3, showing that qualitative
conclusions hold across model families.

##### Span-matching methodology.

Span-level $`F_{1}`$ depends on the choice of span-matching formulation.
We use MP with $`\tau{=}1`$ as the primary formulation because our main
question is whether the evaluator identifies the annotated error
location. MPP assigns partial credit in proportion to character overlap
and is therefore more sensitive to span-boundary agreement. Appendix E
reports MPP and LLM-based reconciliation as complementary sensitivity
analyses.

##### Severity calibration.

Appendix F shows that severity assignment is a distinct capability from
error detection, with both human experts and LLM evaluators
substantially under-rating major errors when stricter severity
thresholds are required. We report detection-oriented $`R_{4+}/P_{4+}`$
as primary metrics in the main paper because they isolate the more
fundamental question of error localization, but downstream use cases
that depend on accurate severity assignment (e.g., quality estimation
thresholds, automatic gating) require additional severity calibration
that is not the focus of this work.

##### Human–LLM collaboration study.

Our human–LLM collaboration experiments (Appendix H) are exploratory in
scope: they measure annotation revision rather than ground-truth
correctness, use only Orbit-MC as the reference configuration, and were
conducted with a limited annotator group on a single language pair.
Whether the observed shifts in annotation behavior correspond to
improved annotation quality, and how the effects generalize across
configurations and annotator pools, remains to be studied.

##### Subjectivity of MT evaluation.

Translation quality assessment remains partly subjective even among
trained experts. The agreement between our expert annotators (Table 1)
provides a human-agreement reference point for how closely any evaluator
— human or LLM-based — can be expected to match a single “ground truth”.
We treat expert agreement as the headline target rather than a hard
ceiling, and recognize that ranking differences within this band may not
reflect meaningful quality differences.

## Ethics Statement

##### Annotator compensation and consent.

RATE annotators received compensation at standard commercial translation
rates for their region, with expert annotators earning approximately
double the hourly wage of assessors, reflecting the higher
qualifications required. No personal data was collected during the
annotation process, and all source segments were screened for
potentially disturbing material prior to annotation. All annotators were
informed about how their annotations would be used — including for the
development and evaluation of LLM-based MT evaluators — prior to
beginning the tasks.

##### Human–LLM collaboration study.

The collaborative annotation study in Appendix H followed the same
compensation and consent procedures. Annotators were informed that they
would be reviewing LLM-generated reports as part of their workflow, and
that their pre- and post-hint annotations would be compared. No
identifying information about annotators is reported in our analyses.

##### Annotator feedback.

Following the annotation, we administered a feedback questionnaire. The
vast majority of annotators reported a positive experience and found the
instructions clear.

##### Use of closed-source models.

Our strongest configurations rely on closed-source LLMs accessed via
commercial APIs. We discuss reproducibility and longevity implications
of this choice in the Limitations section.

## Appendix C Annotator Pool

##### Expert annotators.

Our Expert tier consists of professional translators who are native
Russian speakers with verified C1/C2 English proficiency and substantial
industry experience. A university degree in linguistics or translation
studies was preferred. Candidates passed a multi-stage selection
process: a post-editing test that required context analysis and source
fact-checking, followed by an interview. After selection, experts
received structured training on the RATE protocol — error
categorization, severity calibration, and the Accuracy/Fluency scoring
scales — with concrete examples for each category.

##### Assessor.

Our Assessor tier consists of in-house crowd-level evaluators: native
Russian speakers with verified C1 English proficiency (screened via a
standardized proficiency test). We do _not_ require linguistics or
translation degrees or professional translation experience. Assessors
received the same RATE-protocol training materials as experts but were
not subject to the post-editing selection test.

##### Pool size and workload.

The pool comprises 35 expert translators and 82 assessors. Each segment
is annotated independently by three experts from the pool in implicit
side-by-side mode (Expert $`\times`$ 3), and by assessors in two setups:
implicit side-by-side (Human*(A-SbS)) and point-wise (Human*(A-PW)). For
evaluation we hold two expert annotations as gold and score the third
against each of them and average, giving the Human\_(E) agreement
reported in Table 1 (Section 3.3). Average workload per annotation pass
was 17 segments per expert and 22 segments per assessor. Median
annotation times were 14 minutes per segment for experts and 5 minutes
for assessors, compared to the $`\sim`$ 68 seconds per segment that
Kocmi et al. (2025) report for English–Russian WMT25 ESA annotation.

##### Training and dispute resolution.

Before annotation began, all annotators participated in a calibration
session in which a small set of pre-annotated examples was reviewed and
edge cases were discussed collaboratively. For the few-shot pool used in
Section 4.2, annotators worked in overlapping groups on subsets of
segments and reconciled disagreements through discussion to produce
consensus annotations more reliable than any single-annotator output.

## Appendix D Methods Not Included in Main Comparison

We do not include several recent MT evaluation approaches in our primary
comparison due to differences in evaluation setup and workflow design.
M-MAD (Feng et al., 2025) explores debate-style multi-agent evaluation
with substantially different orchestration strategies and computational
budgets. We draw on its decomposition idea in our multi-call ablations
(Section 4.5) but do not reproduce the full multi-agent system.
EAPrompt (Lu et al., 2024) produces error analyses through iterative
prompting. A controlled re-evaluation by Feng et al. (2025) reports
lower span-level $`F_{1}`$ than GEMBA-MQM, which is already included in
our comparison. MQM-APE (Lu et al., 2025) relies on automatic
post-editing as a validation step, requiring multiple sequential LLM
passes per segment and following a substantially different multi-stage
workflow. More broadly, our goal is not to exhaustively benchmark every
existing MT evaluation system, but to study evaluator design principles
under a controlled experimental setup.

## Appendix E Sensitivity to Span Matching

Perrella et al. (2026) recommend MPP with micro-averaging to penalize
imprecise span boundaries and avoid the preference of MP for longer
spans. Our target places more emphasis on error-identity agreement, so
we retain MP with $`\tau{=}1`$ as the primary formulation and compare it
with MPP and an LLM-based reconciliation proxy. All metrics in this
section are micro-averaged.

### E.1 Formal Definitions

We compare three matching formulations.

##### MP with $`\tau{=}1`$ (primary).

Predicted error spans are matched to gold annotations via bipartite
matching that maximizes the number of matched pairs. A predicted–gold
pair is eligible for matching iff their character overlap is $`\geq 1`$.
Each matched pair contributes a credit of $`1.0`$ to both precision
(over predicted spans) and recall (over gold spans).

##### MPP (Perrella et al., 2026).

Eligible predicted–gold pairs must overlap by at least one character.
Among eligible one-to-one matchings, MPP selects the matching that
maximizes the resulting MPP $`F_{1}`$. Credit is then proportional to
overlap. For a matched pair with $`k`$ overlapping characters, precision
credit (over the predicted span of length $`\ell_{p}`$) is
$`k/\ell_{p}`$, and recall credit (over the gold span of length
$`\ell_{g}`$) is $`k/\ell_{g}`$.

##### LLM-based reconciliation proxy.

For each translation, we present the predicted spans and gold
annotations to GPT-5.4 and instruct it to match annotations that
describe the same error, allowing character boundaries to differ. Each
matched pair contributes a credit of $`1.0`$. We treat this as an
exploratory reconciliation proxy rather than human adjudication.

### E.3 Boundary Sensitivity under MPP

The matching formulation affects exact system rankings and the
comparison between human and model agreement. Under MPP, Human\_(E) drops
by $`4.9`$ recall points and $`5.7`$ precision points. Across the three
Orbit-SC backbones, recall changes by only $`1.6`$–$`2.2`$ points, while
precision falls by $`14.3`$–$`19.5`$ points. Orbit-MC shows the same
asymmetry. This follows directly from MPP’s partial-credit rule when a
predicted span extends beyond the gold boundaries.

Boundary fidelity is relevant for some downstream uses. However, MPP can
under-credit error-identity agreement when systems and experts use
different span granularities.

### E.4 LLM Reconciliation Proxy

The LLM reconciliation proxy provides a complementary view. It places
Human*(E) at $`F*{1}{=}53.1`$, the three Orbit-SC configurations at
$`51.7`$–$`54.1`$, and Orbit-MC at $`56.4`$. These results support the
main error-identity comparison, while MPP shows that the exact
human–model ordering is sensitive to span boundaries.

GPT-5.4 is both the reconciler and one of the evaluated backbones, so
the proxy may share biases with the systems being judged. We therefore
use it as complementary evidence rather than as the primary
meta-evaluation method.

### E.5 Methodological Takeaway

We retain MP with $`\tau{=}1`$ as primary because our main question is
whether an evaluator identifies the annotated error location. MPP
provides a boundary-sensitive view, while the LLM reconciliation proxy
emphasizes error identity. The three formulations support the same broad
conclusion that Orbit is a strong evaluator, but exact system ordering
and the human–model comparison depend on the operational definition of a
span match.

## Appendix F Model Comparison: Extended Results

Table 15 extends the model comparison from Section 4.3 with per-model
span density (mean predicted spans per segment, broken down by severity)
and severity-calibrated variants of the major-error metrics.

##### Severity-aware metrics.

The main $`R_{4+}`$ metric measures recovery of gold spans with severity
$`\geq 4`$ regardless of predicted severity. The main $`P_{4+}`$ metric
measures precision for predictions assigned severity $`\geq 4`$ against
the same gold major-error spans. For $`R_{4+}^{\bullet}`$ and
$`P_{4+}^{\bullet}`$, only predictions assigned severity $`\geq 3`$ are
eligible for matching. These are severity-aware variants with a
one-level tolerance around the major threshold. The gap between
$`R_{4+}`$ and $`R_{4+}^{\bullet}`$ therefore measures how often real
major errors are detected but assigned severity $`\leq 2`$.

##### Severity calibration is a separate axis of evaluator behavior.

As shown in Table 15, the gap between $`R_{4+}`$ and
$`R_{4+}^{\bullet}`$ reveals substantial under-rating of major errors
across both human and LLM evaluators. Human*(E) itself drops $`11`$
points ($`65.3\to 54.0`$), confirming that severity assignment is
genuinely hard even for trained experts. LLM evaluators show gaps of
comparable magnitude: GPT-5.4 drops from $`74.7`$ to $`61.3`$
($`-13.4`$), Claude Opus 4.6 from $`72.2`$ to $`56.8`$ ($`-15.4`$), and
Gemini 3.1 Pro from $`67.8`$ to $`56.8`$ ($`-11.0`$). This indicates
that detection and severity assignment are partially independent
capabilities, and that the difficulty of severity calibration is not
specific to LLM evaluators — trained experts exhibit the same pattern.
We adopt the detection-oriented $`R*{4+}/P\_{4+}`$ as primary metrics in
the main paper because they isolate the comparatively more fundamental
question of whether the evaluator finds the right spans, but report
severity-calibrated variants here as a complementary diagnostic.

##### Span density patterns.

The mean spans-per-segment columns clarify the recall–precision
trade-offs visible in the span-level metrics (Table 5). Backbones with
strong precision (Gemini 3.1 Pro, Claude Opus 4.6) predict at or below
the expert non-trivial rate ($`4.39`$ and $`5.09`$ vs. $`5.28`$), while
recall-oriented backbones (GPT-5.4, Grok-4) predict above it but with
major-severity counts that remain plausible ($`1.55`$ and $`1.36`$ vs.
$`1.01`$). DeepSeek-V3.2 is the clear outlier: $`11.96`$ non-trivial
spans and $`6.12`$ major errors per segment — roughly $`6\times`$ the
expert major-error rate — explaining its severe precision collapse
($`P_{4+}=15.3`$). Kimi-K2 sits at the opposite extreme,
under-predicting both non-trivial ($`3.49`$) and major ($`0.70`$) spans,
consistent with its low overall recall ($`R=22.0`$).

## Appendix H Human–LLM Collaborative Annotation

We conduct a preliminary study of how expert annotators interact with
reports generated by the Orbit-MC multi-call configuration. On a
held-out set of 600 English–Russian segments (disjoint from the design
study data), we compare two expert groups. Human*(E1) first produced
independent annotations and then re-annotated the same segments after
reviewing Orbit-MC reports (post-hint, within-subject design).
Human*(E2) received the reports before its initial annotation
(pre-hint).

In aggregate, access to Orbit-MC reports increases annotation density
and shifts annotators toward lower overall quality scores (Table 17).
Annotators do not adopt reports uncritically: post-hint annotations
retain fewer spans than the raw report contains, and slider scores shift
toward but do not match Orbit-MC’s more severe assessments, indicating
partial rather than wholesale integration of report signals. These
results are exploratory — the study measures annotation revision rather
than ground-truth correctness.
