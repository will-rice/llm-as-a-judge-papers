# Cost-Effective Automated Judging of Natural-Language Mathematical Proofs

Benjamin Grayzel ^(†)^(†)thanks: Work begun while at the Department of
Computer Science, Dartmouth College. Affiliation: Independent Researcher
Email: [benjamingrayzel@gmail.com](mailto:)

###### Abstract

Grading natural-language mathematical proofs is a recurring cost in
evaluating math-reasoning systems, and frontier LLM judges are
expensive. We ask whether cheap open-weight models can serve as reliable
judges given a candidate proof, a ground-truth proof, and a
human-grading rubric. On a 200-instance validation sample of
IMO-GradingBench, two of three cheap judges (GPT-OSS-120B,
DeepSeek-V4-Flash) and their three-model consensus are statistically no
worse than the frontier (Claude Opus 4.7, Gemini 3.1 Pro) on agreement
with human pass/fail decisions, at $`4`$–$`100\times`$ lower cost. On
the full 1000-instance benchmark, the choice of consensus rule over the
three judges is a precision/recall dial: unanimous (all-three-pass)
rules reach the highest precision ($`0.855`$), majority vote the highest
recall ($`0.912`$); across four replicate runs the unanimous rule is
also the steadiest. No rule won outright; the dial replicated on a
held-out 600-instance split and on the independent ProofBench. In this
domain, cheap judges are competitive with the frontier at one to two
orders of magnitude lower cost, and unanimity is the right setting when
false positives are costly.

## 1 Introduction

Benchmarks for AI mathematical reasoning increasingly include problems
whose solutions are full natural-language proofs rather than short final
answers, and scoring those proofs is a bottleneck. Formal verification
with proof assistants such as Lean (de Moura and Ullrich, 2021) gives
trustworthy guarantees and has reached IMO-medal performance with
AlphaProof (Hubert et al., 2025), but until its coverage extends to the
breadth of research mathematics, most proofs will still be graded in
natural language. While the model under study changes between
experiments, the judge must be reliable and held fixed across an entire
study, so its cost is a tax on the whole research effort, and it recurs
inside self-improving systems (which need a judgment at every step of a
generate–critique–revise loop). This motivates a concrete question: _is
there a cheap judge a budget-constrained researcher can trust for proof
grading?_

LLM-as-a-judge (Zheng et al., 2023) is now the standard tool but
exhibits well-documented biases (Gu et al., 2024), and frontier models
often fail to produce valid arguments even when their final answers are
correct (Petrov et al., 2025), motivating dedicated grading benchmarks
such as the Open Proof Corpus (Dekoninck et al., 2025) and
IMO-GradingBench (Luong et al., 2025). Cost has received less attention:
Verga et al. (2024) show that small-model panels can outperform a single
large judge on QA and chatbot tasks. Two concurrent works frame our
contribution. Ma et al. (2026) reach expert-level agreement by combining
a strong reasoning backbone with reference solutions and marking
schemes; we ask whether the backbone itself must be strong and find
that, for reference-based pass/fail grading, it need not be. Naik et al.
(2026) study the closest reference-_free_ version of our question and
report cheap judges trailing the frontier by $`{\sim}10\%`$ in accuracy;
in our reference-_based_ setting the accuracy gap closes entirely,
consistent with the reference solution doing work the judge would
otherwise have to do.

We study judging, not solving, on IMO-GradingBench (Luong et al., 2025):
1,000 instances, each pairing an Olympiad problem and a reference
solution with a candidate proof and an expert human grade on the
standard 0–7 IMO scale. Our prior expectation, following Verga et al.
(2024), was that a majority-vote consensus of several cheap models would
be the safest choice. We tested this: the consensus performed well but
did not beat its strongest member. The more useful finding is that the
cheap tier as a whole is competitive: the best cheap open-weight judges
are statistically no worse than frontier baselines (Claude Opus 4.7,
Gemini 3.1 Pro) on pass/fail agreement at a cost one to two orders of
magnitude lower. The choice of consensus rule is most useful as a
precision/recall dial whose unanimous setting delivers the highest
precision and the most stable behavior across replicate runs.

## 2 Setup

### Task and metrics.

A grading instance is a tuple _(problem, ground-truth solution,
candidate solution, human score)_; a judge reads the first three and
outputs a score. The decision that matters most for downstream use is
the pass/fail boundary (a score of $`\geq 6`$ on the 0–7 IMO scale). Our
primary metric is pass-agreement: the fraction of instances where the
judge’s pass/fail decision matches the human’s. Because only
$`{\sim}30\%`$ of instances are passes, a _fail-everything_ baseline
already reaches $`0.715`$ pass-agreement on our validation sample, so we
report Cohen’s $`\kappa`$ (Cohen, 1960) alongside it (the same agreement
corrected for chance, $`0`$ for any constant classifier) and include the
baseline as a row in every table. We further report F1 at the pass
boundary, Spearman rank correlation with the human score as a secondary
ordinal measure, and per-grading cost.

### Data and sampling.

From the benchmark’s 1,000 instances (spanning 30 IMO-style problems) we
drew two disjoint random samples of 200: a _prior_ (exploratory) sample
used for exploration and for selecting the consensus trio, and a
_validation_ sample used as a clean held-out test. The trio, its
settings, and the majority rule were fixed on the prior sample alone,
before any validation call was made. Section 4 adds the cheap tier over
the complete benchmark and a check on the 600 instances untouched during
any exploration; headline results are on the validation sample unless
noted. The 200 validation instances come from only 30 problems, so we
also check every paired comparison by resampling whole problems
(Appendix G).

### Judges.

We evaluate three cheap open-weight models (GPT-OSS-120B,
DeepSeek-V4-Flash, Gemma-4-31B) and two frontier baselines (Claude Opus
4.7, Gemini 3.1 Pro). The cheap models were chosen on the prior sample
for their cost-accuracy tradeoff and offsetting calibration biases
(Gemma over-credits, DeepSeek under-credits), the intended ingredient
for a majority vote. Every judge uses the same prompt, lightly adapted
from the benchmark’s public grading rubric, and emits a score in
$`\{0,1,6,7\}`$; each model runs at the strongest reasoning
configuration it exposes (settings, providers, coverage, re-run policy,
and the ordinal statistic behind consensus-row $`\rho`$ in Appendix A).
The majority pass/fail vote of the three cheap models was the
pre-specified consensus rule; Section 4’s unanimous variants were added
later and checked on held-out data.

### The cheap tier is competitive with the frontier.

The intervals of the top five systems overlap substantially; paired
comparisons against each frontier baseline make the claim precise.
Relative to Claude Opus 4.7, the paired difference in pass-agreement is
$`+0.020`$ (90% CI $`[-0.020,+0.060]`$) for GPT-OSS-120B, $`+0.005`$
$`[-0.035,+0.045]`$ for DeepSeek-V4-Flash, and $`0.000`$
$`[-0.030,+0.035]`$ for the trio; against Gemini 3.1 Pro the same three
are $`+0.035`$ $`[-0.010,+0.080]`$, $`+0.020`$ $`[-0.020,+0.060]`$, and
$`+0.015`$ $`[-0.020,+0.055]`$. All three are therefore non-inferior to
both frontier baselines at a $`\pm 5`$-point margin (Schuirmann, 1987),
and DeepSeek and the trio are fully equivalent to Opus under TOST;
GPT-OSS fails equivalence only on the _upper_ side. Gemma is the
exception, measurably behind both ($`-0.060`$ $`[-0.095,-0.030]`$ vs
Opus, $`-0.045`$ $`[-0.080,-0.010]`$ vs Gemini). This shows the best
cheap judges are not measurably worse than the frontier; it does not
show they are better, and our sample cannot rank the leading systems
against one another. The chance-corrected column agrees, with GPT-OSS at
$`\kappa=0.716`$ and Opus at $`0.680`$, both “substantial” agreement
(Landis and Koch, 1977) against $`\kappa=0`$ for the fail-everything
baseline.

### Robustness of the comparison.

The frontier baselines were run once, and Table 1 reports the cheap
tier’s original run, which for GPT-OSS was the best of its four runs.
The “no worse than the frontier” results hold when we resample whole
problems instead of instances, and when we use each cheap system’s
average over four runs (e.g. GPT-OSS vs Opus becomes $`-0.005`$, 90% CI
$`[-0.043,+0.034]`$; the trio $`+0.019`$ $`[-0.015,+0.057]`$). They also
hold against Gemini at its default setting, which scores the same as
high reasoning at a quarter of the cost (Appendix G).

### The consensus did not beat its best member.

The majority vote matched Opus on pass-agreement but fell below its
strongest single member, GPT-OSS-120B, at roughly five times the cost:
combining a strong model with two weaker ones diluted rather than
improved the result (Section 4 asks whether a different rule over the
same trio fares better).

### The price gap is large.

GPT-OSS-120B grades at \$0.0016 per instance, about $`100\times`$
cheaper than Claude Opus 4.7 (\$0.162) and $`{\sim}22\times`$ cheaper
than Gemini 3.1 Pro at its default setting (\$0.035; $`{\sim}90\times`$
at high reasoning), while doing no worse on the pass/fail decision.
DeepSeek is $`{\sim}10\times`$ cheaper than default Gemini, and the
three-judge consensus $`{\sim}4\times`$. Running three judges, or one
judge three times, for stability costs \$0.96–\$2.13 per 200: still
$`3`$–$`7\times`$ cheaper than default Gemini and $`15`$–$`34\times`$
cheaper than Opus. All costs are what OpenRouter charged us in May 2026;
the open models are served there by third-party hosts, so their prices
may change, for example if you pin a provider. Where the frontier keeps
an edge is rank correlation with the human score (Opus $`0.715`$, Gemini
$`0.704`$, cheap judges $`0.62`$–$`0.68`$): for a graded quality signal
rather than a pass/fail gate, a frontier judge is still safer
(Appendix H). Reasoning effort, meanwhile, is a model-specific lever,
large for GPT-OSS-120B and negligible for Gemini (Appendix B).

## 4 Consensus rules are a precision/recall dial

We graded the three cheap judges over the complete 1000-instance
benchmark for a more stable estimate and to explore consensus
configurations beyond the pre-specified majority vote (Table 2). Three
observations. First, the single-model leader changes with scale:
GPT-OSS-120B led on the validation sample, but on the full benchmark
DeepSeek-V4-Flash is the strongest single judge ($`0.873`$ vs
$`0.842`$)—direct evidence that 200 instances cannot separate the
leaders (Appendix H). Second, the choice of consensus rule trades
precision against recall (Figure 3, Appendix H): the unanimous
all-three-pass rule reaches the highest pass-agreement ($`0.879`$) and
precision ($`0.855`$), majority vote is the most recall-heavy
($`0.912`$) and, via its median member score, has the highest rank
correlation of any system ($`\rho=0.765`$). That unanimity is stricter
is true by definition (every unanimous pass is also a majority pass);
what the data add is the size of the trade ($`+0.111`$ precision for
$`-0.135`$ recall) and that unanimity loses no pass-agreement for it.
The same holds for both-pass pairs (Table 9): a pair’s strictness is
driven by its most conservative member, so any pairing that includes the
under-crediting DeepSeek inherits high precision. Third, the top
configurations are too close to call: all-three-pass is measurably ahead
of GPT-OSS and Gemma, but its lead over DeepSeek ($`+0.006`$, 95% CI
$`[-0.008,+0.021]`$) and majority vote ($`+0.016`$ $`[-0.011,+0.044]`$)
is within noise.

### A held-out check of the unanimous rule.

The unanimous rules were noticed during analysis, so we checked them on
the 600 instances untouched during all exploration, predicting that
all-three-pass would win outright. It did not: all-three-pass scored
$`0.873`$ pass-agreement, a statistical tie with the other consensus
configurations, which land in a $`0.868`$–$`0.878`$ band (Appendix D).
What _did_ replicate is the dial itself: unanimity again delivered the
optimal precision ($`0.861`$ vs the majority vote’s $`0.776`$) at the
cost of recall. This was further replicated on the independent
ProofBench benchmark (Appendix E). We therefore recommend not
all-three-pass as uniquely best but unanimity as the high-precision
setting when false positives are costly, and majority vote when recall
or a graded signal matters.

### Run-to-run stability.

Re-running the cheap tier three more times on validation (Appendix C)
shows single cheap judges moving by $`{\sim}2`$ points (std) between
runs (GPT-OSS’s headline $`0.875`$ is the top of a $`0.83`$–$`0.88`$
range). Part of this is ordinary sampling randomness, and part may come
from calls being routed among a shifting pool of third-party providers.
The consensus rules damp this: all-three-pass has both the highest mean
($`0.902`$) and the smallest spread (std $`0.009`$ vs
$`0.022`$–$`0.024`$ for the volatile singles). We did not re-run the
frontier baselines, so we do not know their spread; using the cheap
tier’s four-run averages leaves Section 3’s main result unchanged.

## 5 Limitations

Sample size and intervals. Even on the full benchmark the leading
systems’ intervals overlap and we cannot resolve a single best judge;
the frontier baselines were run on the 200-instance validation sample
only (due to budget constraints) and only once, so the full-benchmark
comparison is cheap-tier-only and we do not know how much the frontier
varies between runs. Agreement is not verification. These judges
approximate human grades but cannot certify that a proof is correct.
Even the strictest rule passes some proofs the humans failed (roughly
one in seven of its passes). Output scale. Judges can only answer 0, 1,
6, or 7, so they cannot give the partial credit (2–5) that humans often
do, limiting rank correlation. Budget-constrained search. We could not
afford a broad sweep, most notably GPT-5.5 Pro; a small blind GPT-5.5
adjudication of the judge–human disagreements is in Appendix F.
Contamination. GPT-OSS-120B predates IMO-GradingBench by three months,
so it cannot have trained on the benchmark’s graded instances; grading
against a provided reference is in any case a distinct task from having
encountered the problem. Rule selection. The unanimous rules were
identified during analysis; the held-out check above and an independent
replication on ProofBench (Appendix E) both show the rule _class_
replicating while rankings within the band do not. Scope. These results
speak to judging, not solving, on IMO-style mathematics with a
ground-truth reference and a human grade; we claim no transfer to
reference-free verification.

## 6 Conclusion

For grading AI-generated natural-language proofs on a budget, cheap
open-weight judges are a credible choice: the best of them do as well as
frontier judges on pass/fail agreement at $`1`$–$`10\%`$ of the cost.
Neither the intuition that a majority vote would be best nor our logged
prediction that the unanimous variant would win outright was borne out.
What survives our checks is a precision/recall dial: unanimity buys
precision and stability, while majority buys recall and the best ordinal
signal. We stop short of crowning a single model, since the leader
shifts between samples and benchmarks.

## Appendix A Methodology details

### Judge prompt and scoring buckets.

Every judge uses the same prompt and is instructed to emit a score in
$`\{0,1,6,7\}`$ (incorrect / partial / almost / correct); our parser
accepts any integer 0–7, and a handful of off-bucket scores (4 across
all runs, under 0.1%) occurred and are scored as parsed. This
four-bucket scheme follows the public grading prompt released with
IMO-GradingBench (Luong et al., 2025), which we lightly adapt so that
our judges are scored under an established, externally defined rubric
rather than one of our own design. Pass/fail and all confusion-matrix
metrics use the raw human score, so a human-4 graded as 6 by a judge is
correctly counted as a false positive. The benchmark also ships a
per-problem marking scheme; we do not provide it to the judge. The exact
prompt is included in the supplementary material.

### Sampling.

The two disjoint 200-instance samples were drawn by uniform selection
without replacement: the prior sample with seed 42, and the validation
sample with seed 7 from the remaining 800 instances. The held-out check
of Appendix D uses the 600 instances in neither sample.

### Reasoning settings.

For each model we use the strongest reasoning configuration it exposes.
These settings are not normalized and are not directly comparable across
providers: GPT-OSS-120B runs at effort xhigh, Gemma-4-31B and Gemini 3.1
Pro with reasoning activated (listed as high), while Claude Opus 4.7
(adaptive thinking) and DeepSeek-V4-Flash (default) self-regulate their
reasoning, so we report them at their default.

### Providers, coverage, and re-runs.

The cheap open-weight judges are served through OpenRouter by a shifting
pool of third-party providers; we did not pin a provider, so a single
judge’s pass-agreement can move by a few points between runs
(Appendix C). For a reproducible number, pin a provider and log the
served-provider field. The frontier baselines are first-party and
single-provider; we ran them once and did not measure their run-to-run
spread. Under $`2\%`$ of judge calls failed to return a parseable score
on the first attempt (transient rate limits, or reasoning that exceeded
the 32k-token output ceiling without concluding); we re-ran these and
never substituted fabricated scores. Final coverage is 1000/1000 for
each cheap judge and 200/200 for each frontier baseline.

### Consensus ordinal statistic.

For consensus rows we report Spearman $`\rho`$ of the natural ordinal
statistic (the median member score for majority vote, the minimum for
unanimous rules) whose threshold at $`{\geq}6`$ reproduces the rule’s
pass/fail decision. (The _mean_ of member scores would produce
intermediate values that no single judge can emit under the
$`\{0,1,6,7\}`$ bucket scheme, making its correlation incomparable to
single-judge correlations; the min/median statistics do not have that
defect.)

### Calibration biases.

The trio was chosen on the prior sample alone, where the mean signed
score error (judge $`-`$ human) is $`+0.545`$ for Gemma-4-31B
(over-credits), $`-0.790`$ for DeepSeek-V4-Flash (under-credits), and
$`+0.170`$ for GPT-OSS-120B (near-calibrated). Pooling the prior and
validation samples ($`n=400`$) gives $`+0.51`$, $`-0.79`$, and
$`+0.02`$; on the validation half alone GPT-OSS sits slightly negative
($`-0.13`$). The two large biases are stable across our replicate runs
(Gemma $`+0.34`$ to $`+0.46`$, DeepSeek $`-0.72`$ to $`-0.87`$), so the
trio’s offsetting diversity is a property of the models rather than of
any single run; GPT-OSS stays near zero throughout ($`-0.08`$ to
$`-0.03`$).

## Appendix B Reasoning effort is a model-specific lever

To check whether reasoning _budget_, rather than model identity, drives
judge quality, we ran Gemini 3.1 Pro at both its default setting and
high reasoning on the validation sample (Table 3).

| Setting | Reason. tok.       | Agree | F1    | $`\rho`$ | Cost    |
| ------- | ------------------ | ----- | ----- | -------- | ------- |
| Default | $`{\sim}2{,}400`$  | 0.840 | 0.771 | 0.714    | \$7.07  |
| High    | $`{\sim}10{,}400`$ | 0.840 | 0.761 | 0.704    | \$28.61 |

Table 3: Gemini 3.1 Pro: default vs. high reasoning ($`n=200`$). “Agree”
is pass-agreement, $`\rho`$ is Spearman, and cost is per 200 gradings.

Raising Gemini’s reasoning roughly fourfold left its pass-agreement
unchanged ($`0.840`$ in both cases; a paired bootstrap finds no
detectable difference, and ten individual decisions flipped but canceled
out), while quadrupling its cost. By contrast, reasoning effort matters
a great deal for GPT-OSS-120B: on the exploratory sample its
pass-agreement rises from $`0.72`$ at minimal effort to $`0.84`$ at
xhigh. Claude Opus 4.7 uses adaptive thinking and does not accept a
manual reasoning budget, so its reported result already reflects
whatever thinking it elects to do. The conclusion is that reasoning
effort is a model-specific lever, not a universal one: large for
GPT-OSS-120B, negligible for Gemini.

Part of this variance is ordinary sampling randomness, which any LLM
judge would show. Another possible contributor is how the models are
served: each call is routed to one of several third-party providers.
Provider dispersion tracks the spread across our three judges: Gemma is
served almost entirely ($`{\sim}93\%`$) by a single provider and has the
smallest spread (std $`0.011`$, edging out even the majority vote; we do
not claim consensus always reduces variance), while DeepSeek is spread
over ten providers with none serving more than a quarter of calls and
has the largest ($`0.024`$). With only three judges this pattern is
suggestive, not conclusive: we log the provider on every call but did
not run a controlled comparison with providers pinned, so we cannot
separate the two causes. For the recall-biased judges, unanimity over a
single model’s own three runs filters the occasional spurious passes
that run-to-run noise produces, lifting GPT-OSS from a single-run mean
of $`0.851`$ to $`0.888`$ and Gemma from $`0.810`$ to $`0.849`$;
DeepSeek, already balanced, does not surpass its best single run. In
effect, running one cheap model three times under a unanimity rule
recovers much of the multi-model consensus benefit.

judge again lands in Landis–Koch “substantial” territory ($`\kappa`$

$`0.784`$ raw agreement from the skewed base rate alone. Second, the
dial replicates: unanimity buys precision over majority vote ($`0.887`$
vs $`0.786`$, $`+0.101`$) at the cost of recall ($`0.585`$ vs
$`0.819`$); on IMO-GradingBench the same trade was
$`+0.111`$/$`-0.135`$. Third, the single-judge ranking fully reverses:
Gemma goes from worst to best ($`\kappa`$ $`0.604\to 0.730`$) and
DeepSeek from best to worst ($`0.717\to 0.609`$), and
all-three-pass—dragged by DeepSeek’s recall collapse—is not the best
rule here (the GPT-OSS+Gemma pair and the majority vote lead on

dial, not any fixed member or configuration. Caveats: single run (no
replicate spread); judge Spearman $`\rho`$ is a floor rather than an
estimate here, since $`122/435`$ expert grades fall in $`2`$–$`5`$,
values the $`\{0,1,6,7\}`$ scheme cannot emit; and we checked for
self-preference (DeepSeek-V4-Flash grading DeepSeek-R1 proofs): it is
conservative on all three generators but least so on its own sibling
(judge-minus-expert pass rate $`-0.028`$, against $`-0.048`$ on
Gemini-2.5-Pro and $`-0.069`$ on o3), a small gap in the direction of
self-preference that a sample this size cannot resolve.

## Appendix F Adjudicating judge–human disagreements

The all-three-pass rule disagrees with the human grader on 121 of 1000
instances (45 false positives, 76 false negatives). To probe whether
this residue reflects judge error or instance ambiguity, we had a
stronger model than any judge in our study (GPT-5.5 at xhigh reasoning)
blindly re-grade a seed-fixed sample of 40 disputed instances (25 FP, 15
FN) under the same prompt, with no access to the human grade or the
judges’ scores. We report whose side of the pass/fail boundary it lands
on.

| Disputed set                                | $`n`$ | Sides with judges | Sides with human |
| ------------------------------------------- | ----- | ----------------- | ---------------- |
| False positives (judges pass, human fails)  | 25    | 10 (40%)          | 15 (60%)         |
| False negatives (judges fail, human passes) | 15    | 8 (53%)           | 7 (47%)          |
| Combined                                    | 40    | 18 (45%)          | 22 (55%)         |

Table 7: Blind GPT-5.5 adjudication of sampled all-three-pass–vs–human
disagreements.

Two observations. First, the adjudicator does _not_ vindicate the cheap
consensus: it backs the human label slightly more often on both sets.
Another model disagreeing with a human does not by itself make the human
label ambiguous; we take the 45% rate only as a sign that these
disagreements are not mostly clear-cut judge mistakes. The adjudicator’s
justifications typically reconstruct the argument, accept its broad
strokes, and then turn on one specific technical call (e.g. whether
citing a classical theorem without proof is admissible at IMO
standard)—exactly the judgments a human grader makes, which is why a
stronger model does not settle them decisively. Second, much of the
false-positive set is a scale artifact: 32 of the 45 FPs carry a human
grade of 4 or 5, values the $`\{0,1,6,7\}`$ output scheme cannot emit,
so a judge reading a proof as substantially-right-but-gapped must round
to 1 or 6, and 6 crosses the pass threshold. The adjudicator makes this
visible: on the FPs where it sided with the human it emitted 1 or 0,
never 4 or 5 (mean absolute gap to the human score 2.9 points), so
“siding with the human” means agreeing on the threshold while
disagreeing sharply on partial credit. Caveats: $`n=40`$; the
adjudicator ran inside an agentic CLI harness rather than a controlled
API call, so these are indicative observations, not benchmark numbers
(one degenerate generation was re-run; details in the supplementary
material).
