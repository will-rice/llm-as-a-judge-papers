# Same Rule, Different Verdicts: Rubric Language and Clause Structure in LLM Judges for Korean Support Dialogues

Sangdo Han Affiliation: CEO Business School
Email: [sangdohan@ceobizschool.kr](mailto:)    Moonsoo Kim
^(†)^(†)thanks: Corresponding author. Affiliation: CEO Business School
Email: [mskim@ceobizschool.kr](mailto:)

###### Abstract

Services that operate in Korean often write their evaluation rubrics in
Korean and hand them to an LLM judge. We test whether a judge’s verdict
depends on the language of the rubric and on how a rubric item is
phrased. We write 12 criteria for support dialogues in four clause
structures (affirmative, negated, conditional, exception) such that two
pairs of structures have the same meaning, and we give each item in
Korean and in English. Six low-cost models from three providers, plus
one reference model, judge 60 translated ESConv dialogues and 144
constructed boundary cases under every combination (51,744 requests
across the seven models). The analysis plan was fixed and committed
before data collection. Cross-provider agreement is lower with Korean
rubrics than with English rubrics (Cohen’s $`\kappa`$ 0.687 vs. 0.745;
difference $`-0.058`$, 95% CI $`[-0.072,-0.045]`$). Disagreement
concentrates in negated, conditional, and exception items (odds ratio
5.6 relative to affirmative items). A single model changes its verdict
between two items with the same meaning in 11–15% of cases. Two
hypotheses are not supported: models from the same provider do not agree
more than models from different providers, and adding paired examples
does not reduce the language gap. We measure consistency only; no human
labels are used, and all materials were drafted by an LLM.

## 1 Introduction

Zheng et al. (2023) found that strong LLMs judging open-ended chat
responses agree with human preferences about as well as humans agree
with each other, which makes an LLM judge an attractive substitute for
human evaluation of dialogue systems. In non-English deployments the
rubric is usually written in the service language, because the people
who own the rubric read and maintain it in that language. Whether this
choice changes the verdicts is an empirical question with a practical
cost: if two judges read the same Korean item differently, a rubric
score reflects the choice of judge as much as the dialogue.

Prior work has shown that multilingual judges are inconsistent across
languages (Fu and Liu, 2025), that English instructions can give more
consistent judgments than translated ones (Zubiaga et al., 2026), and
that the language of a judge prompt interacts with the judge model
(Mahmood et al., 2026). These studies vary the language. They do not
control how an item is phrased. Rubrics, however, are full of negations,
conditions, and exceptions, and these are the constructions in which a
small difference in reading changes the verdict.

We control both factors. Each criterion is written in four structures,
and the structures form two pairs with the same meaning. This gives a
test that needs no reference labels: if one model gives different
verdicts to two items that mean the same thing, at least one verdict is
wrong. We ask four questions, fixed before data collection
(Section 3.4):

- •
  H1. Cross-provider agreement is lower with Korean rubrics than with
  English rubrics.
- •
  H2. Disagreement is concentrated in negated, conditional, and
  exception items.
- •
  H3. Agreement within a provider is higher than agreement across
  providers.
- •
  H4. Paired examples reduce the language gap.

H1 and H2 are supported. H3 and H4 are not. We report all four.

## 2 Related Work

#### Multilingual LLM judges.

Fu and Liu (2025) measure the consistency of judge verdicts across 25
languages on parallel data and report low agreement. Zubiaga et al.
(2026) compare translated and English rubrics for fine-tuned judges in
English, Spanish, and Basque. Mahmood et al. (2026) localize a judge
prompt into five languages for requirement-level judgments of code
agents and report that judge rankings change with language and that
inter-judge agreement is low in every language. Related efforts build
multilingual meta-evaluation benchmarks for judges (Son et al., 2025),
train evaluators that score non-English responses against English
references (Doddapaneni et al., 2025), and transfer judges across
languages by decomposing evaluation into shared criteria (Sheth et al.,
2026). Our H1 and H3 extend these observations to Korean and to support
dialogues. To our knowledge, none of these studies controls the clause
structure of the rubric items or uses meaning-equivalent item pairs.

#### Rubric wording.

Bagaria et al. (2026) show that a classifier given only the text of an
English rubric item, without the response, predicts a judge’s verdict
well above chance, and that negation words in an item are associated
with negative verdicts. They also reverse the meaning of rubric criteria
and find that the judge often keeps its verdict. That design changes the
meaning and asks whether the verdict follows. Ours keeps the meaning
fixed, changes only the structure, and asks whether the verdict stays.
The two designs are complementary.

#### Negation and prompt wording.

Language models perform poorly on negation benchmarks (Truong et al.,
2023), and in few-shot settings their accuracy changes substantially
with formatting choices that leave the meaning of a prompt unchanged
(Sclar et al., 2024). Rubric items with negations, conditions, and
exceptions combine both risks. Our structure contrasts measure them in
the setting where a judge must apply such an item to a dialogue.

## 3 Method

### 3.1 Rubric

We define 12 criteria for the helper’s behaviour in a support dialogue
(for example, asking about the client’s situation before giving the
first piece of advice). Each criterion is written as four items:

- •
  POS: an affirmative requirement.
- •
  NEG: a negated sentence with the same satisfaction conditions as POS.
- •
  COND: the requirement under a condition (“if the client has not asked
  for advice, …”).
- •
  EXC: the requirement followed by an exception (“…This does not apply
  if the client has asked for advice.”).

POS and NEG mean the same thing; COND and EXC mean the same thing. Each
item exists in Korean and in English, giving 96 items. The judge
instructions are also written in both languages and are in the language
of the rubric. The instructions define the three verdicts (met,
violated, not applicable). For the example condition, each item comes
with one satisfying and one violating example; the four structures of a
criterion share the same example text.

All items, examples, and instructions were drafted by Claude and were
not reviewed by a human before data collection. Equivalence between the
Korean and English items was checked by back-translation with two models
from different providers (Claude Sonnet 5.5 and GPT-5.4 nano); the
comparison itself was also done by Claude. Items were revised twice on
this basis and all 96 were kept. Three items were mistranslated by one
back-translation model only and are listed in the appendix.

### 3.2 Data

#### Set A: natural dialogues.

We sample 60 dialogues (1,721 utterances, 21–40 utterances each, 13
topics) from ESConv (Liu et al., 2021) with a fixed seed and translate
them into Korean with Claude Sonnet 5.5. An LLM spot-check of 12
dialogues (376 utterances) marked 5 utterances as errors (1.3%). No
human checked the translations.

#### Set B: boundary cases.

For each criterion we construct 12 short dialogues in Korean with
English counterparts (144 cases). Cases are minimal pairs: within a
criterion the helper’s turns are fixed and one client sentence changes.
The four categories are: no exception situation; the client explicitly
denies the exception situation; the client explicitly states it; the
client states it indirectly. For the last category (36 cases) a literal
reading and the reading intended by the item writer give different
verdicts on COND and EXC items. These labels come from the item writer
and are not human gold labels.

### 3.3 Judges and Procedure

The confirmatory judges are six low-cost models, two from each of three
providers: Claude Sonnet 5.5 and Claude Haiku 4.5; GPT-5.6 Luna and
GPT-5.4 nano; Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite and Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite. Claude
Opus 5.5 is run as a reference model and is excluded from all tests.
Reasoning is disabled or set to the lowest available level (Appendix C);
it cannot be disabled for the reference model. Default temperature is
used. The same prompt text is sent to every model, with no
provider-specific structured-output feature.

A Set A request contains one Korean dialogue and the 12 items of one
structure in one rubric language. A Set B request contains one case and
one item. Set B is run with and without examples, with Korean and
English cases, with a verdict-only baseline (no rationale), and with two
extra repetitions on 36 cases. Each model receives 7,392 requests; the
seven models receive 51,744. Malformed outputs are retried once and then
counted as missing (765 retries in total). Data were collected on 6–7
October 2026 through the providers’ batch interfaces.

Of 88,704 item-level verdicts, 1,219 (1.4%) are missing. 1,199 of these
are from the reference model, almost all because the response reached
the output token limit; its reasoning cannot be disabled and counts
toward that limit (Appendix E). The six confirmatory models have 20
missing verdicts out of 76,032 (0.03%).

### 3.4 Measures and Analysis Plan

Agreement between two models is Cohen’s $`\kappa`$ (Cohen, 1960) over
the three verdicts. There are 15 pairs of confirmatory models: 3 within
a provider and 12 across providers. A _structure flip_ is a case in
which one model gives different verdicts to the POS and NEG items, or to
the COND and EXC items, for the same unit under otherwise identical
conditions. A _language flip_ is the same for the Korean and English
versions of one item.

H1 is the difference in mean cross-provider $`\kappa`$ between Korean
and English rubrics on Korean units without examples (Sets A and B),
with a cluster bootstrap over units stratified by set (10,000
resamples). H2 and H4 are contrasts in a mixed-effects logistic
regression of pairwise disagreement on rubric language, structure, pair
type, and examples, with random intercepts for unit, criterion, and
model pair. H3 is the bootstrap difference between mean within-provider
and mean cross-provider $`\kappa`$. H2–H4 are Holm-corrected (Holm,
1979). The regression is fitted with lme4 (Bates et al., 2015) and the
contrasts with emmeans (Lenth and Piaskowski, 2026); the full model
converged without simplification.

The hypotheses, estimators, tests, exclusion rules, and model settings
were fixed in a document committed and pushed to a public repository 12
minutes before the first request was submitted. The plan was not
registered with a registry. One operational rule was added after
collection began: a request that failed with a transient provider error
was resubmitted once (one request). A planning simulation gave a minimum
detectable $`\kappa`$ difference of 0.03–0.11 at 80% power, depending on
the assumed within-unit error correlation.

## 4 Results

### 4.1 Rubric Language (H1)

Mean cross-provider $`\kappa`$ is 0.687 (95% CI $`[0.667,0.705]`$) with
Korean rubrics and 0.745 ($`[0.726,0.763]`$) with English rubrics. The
difference is $`-0.058`$ ($`[-0.072,-0.045]`$; none of 10,000 bootstrap
replicates is non-negative). H1 is supported. The difference has the
same sign in Set A ($`-0.046`$, $`[-0.061,-0.031]`$), in Set B
($`-0.132`$, $`[-0.163,-0.103]`$), and when “not applicable” is merged
with “met” ($`-0.057`$, $`[-0.073,-0.042]`$).

### 4.2 Clause Structure (H2)

The odds of disagreement for negated, conditional, and exception items,
averaged, are higher than for affirmative items (log-odds contrast 1.72,
SE 0.08, $`z=21.8`$, Holm-adjusted $`p<10^{-100}`$; odds ratio 5.6). H2
is supported. Table 1 and Figure 1 show the descriptive pattern. In Set
B the largest gap between languages is for negated items: 0.51 with
Korean rubrics against 0.78 with English rubrics.

### 4.3 Provider (H3)

Mean within-provider $`\kappa`$ exceeds mean cross-provider $`\kappa`$
by 0.005 ($`[-0.001,0.012]`$, Holm-adjusted $`p=0.23`$). H3 is not
supported. Two models from the same provider agree about as much as two
models from different providers (Korean rubrics: 0.697 within, 0.687
across; English rubrics: 0.745 and 0.745). In Set B alone the difference
is 0.021 ($`[0.004,0.039]`$); this is a sensitivity analysis and does
not change the conclusion.

### 4.4 Paired Examples (H4)

The language gap in disagreement does not shrink when examples are added
(log-odds contrast $`-0.08`$, SE 0.09, Holm-adjusted $`p=0.39`$). H4 is
not supported. Examples raise agreement on negated items in both
languages (Set B: Korean 0.51 to 0.56, English 0.78 to 0.85) and leave
the gap in place.

### 4.5 Flips Within a Model

Across the six models, Korean units, and no examples, a model gives
different verdicts to POS and NEG items in 14.1% of cases with Korean
rubrics and 12.1% with English rubrics; for COND and EXC items the rates
are 14.9% and 11.0%. Models differ widely. In Set B the structure-flip
rate with Korean rubrics ranges from 3.8% (Claude Sonnet 5.5) to 24.3%
(GPT-5.4 nano). Language flips occur in 10.8% of cases for affirmative
items and in 15.0–15.7% for the other three structures. Table 2 gives
the per-model values.

### 4.6 Literal and Intended Readings

On the 36 cases where the client states the exception situation
indirectly, the share of verdicts matching the literal reading ranges
from 43% to 92% across models. GPT-5.4 nano follows the literal reading
in about 90% of verdicts in both languages; the two Gemini models are
near 50%. Claude Sonnet 5.5 follows the literal reading more often with
Korean rubrics (78%) than with English rubrics (65%).

### 4.7 Self-Consistency and Baselines

Fleiss’ $`\kappa`$ over three repetitions of the same request ranges
from 0.74 (GPT-5.4 nano) to 0.97 (Claude Sonnet 5.5); five of six models
are at 0.85 or above. Run-to-run variation therefore accounts for part
of the cross-model disagreement but not most of it.

Without a rationale, mean cross-provider $`\kappa`$ on the matched
conditions is 0.55 (Korean) and 0.71 (English); with a rationale written
before the verdict it is 0.64 and 0.78. Asking for a rationale raises
agreement in both languages and does not close the gap. With English
cases instead of Korean cases the language difference is larger
($`-0.198`$, $`[-0.238,-0.159]`$).

### 4.8 Exploratory Analyses

The analyses in this subsection were not part of the fixed plan and
involve no significance tests.

#### Criteria.

The drop for negated items is not spread evenly across criteria
(Table 3). With Korean rubrics, cross-provider $`\kappa`$ on negated
items is near zero for the closing-summary criterion ($`-0.05`$; English
0.58) and 0.11 for the referral criterion (English 0.92), and 0.41–0.45
for three further criteria. For the other seven criteria the differences
on negated items are smaller (absolute differences 0.08–0.25) and go in
both directions; Korean agreement is higher for four of them. Each value
rests on 12 cases per criterion and is unstable; we report them to show
where the effect comes from, not as estimates.

#### A flip.

For the closing-summary criterion, 25 model–case combinations change
verdict between POS and NEG under the Korean rubric but not under the
English rubric. In 23 of them the Korean NEG verdict is the opposite of
the other three verdicts. The first in case order is Claude Haiku 4.5 on
a case where the counselor’s last turn summarizes the conversation.
Under both English items and the Korean POS item the verdict is _met_.
Under the Korean NEG item (“the counselor does not finish the final turn
without summarizing”) the verdict is _violated_, with the rationale that
the counselor _did_ summarize in the final turn. The model reads the
evidence correctly and inverts the rule.

#### Excluding Claude judges.

Because the materials were drafted by Claude, we recompute the H1
contrast with the four non-Claude judges only (4 cross-provider pairs;
same data and bootstrap procedure, 1,000 resamples). Mean cross-provider
$`\kappa`$ is 0.645 with Korean rubrics and 0.731 with English rubrics,
a difference of $`-0.085`$ ($`[-0.104,-0.067]`$), larger than with all
six judges. The language effect is therefore not carried by the Claude
judges.

#### Reference model.

Claude Opus 5.5, run with adaptive reasoning, changes its verdict
between POS and NEG items in 3.5% of Set B Korean cases with Korean
rubrics (0% with English rubrics) and between COND and EXC items in 0%
(2.2%), far below the confirmatory models. Its agreement with the six
confirmatory models, pooled over the four structures, ranges from
$`\kappa=0.47`$ (GPT-5.4 nano) to 0.93 (Claude Sonnet 5.5) with Korean
rubrics. Because it differs from the confirmatory models in both size
and reasoning setting, and because its missing responses are not random
(Appendix E), we do not attribute its consistency to either factor.

## 5 Discussion

The language of the rubric matters, and it matters most where the rubric
is syntactically complex. For affirmative items the two languages are
close. For the same requirement written as a negation, agreement with
Korean rubrics drops far more than with English rubrics. A practical
reading is that a Korean rubric should state requirements affirmatively
where possible, and that negated or conditional items deserve a
consistency check across judges before they are used.

The structure-flip rate gives a lower bound on error that does not
depend on reference labels. When a model flips on a meaning-equivalent
pair, one of the two verdicts is wrong regardless of which reading is
correct.

The study was motivated by an informal observation that judges from
different providers disagreed more than judges from the same provider.
Under controlled conditions we do not find this. Differences between
individual models and between item structures are large; a provider
effect beyond these is not detectable in our data. Paired examples, a
common remedy, did not reduce the language gap here.

The flip example in Section 4.8 suggests one mechanism: the model
identified the relevant behaviour in the dialogue and then applied the
Korean negated rule with the wrong polarity. A judge can write a correct
rationale and still return the wrong verdict, so reading rationales
alone would not catch this error.

## Limitations

No human labels. We measure consistency, not accuracy. We cannot say
which verdict is right, and we cannot compare the models’ language gap
with a human one. LLM-authored materials. The items, examples,
instructions, boundary cases, dialogue translations, back-translation
comparison, and translation spot-check were all produced by Claude and
were not reviewed by a human before data collection. Two of the six
judges are Claude models. Claude Sonnet 5.5 has the lowest
structure-flip rate with Korean rubrics and the lowest language-flip
rate (Table 2); we cannot separate model capability from an advantage on
materials written by a related model. The H1 effect does not depend on
the Claude judges (Section 4.8). Scope. Six low-cost models, one
language pair, one domain. No model from a Korean provider is included.
The reference model ran with a different reasoning setting and has 9.5%
missing verdicts, 34% in the verdict-only baseline, where the output
limit is smallest. Translation. Set A dialogues are translations;
suggestions phrased as statements in English were sometimes rendered as
questions in Korean. The rubric-language contrast holds the dialogue
fixed, so this does not affect H1, but it may affect absolute verdicts
on question-related criteria. Constructed cases. Set B cases are minimal
pairs built from shared templates and are not independent samples of
natural dialogue. Plan. The analysis plan was fixed in a public commit
before collection but was not registered with a registry.

## Ethical Considerations

Consistency is not accuracy, and a consistent judge is not evidence that
a support dialogue is safe or helpful; our results should not be used to
certify counseling systems. ESConv was collected from crowdworkers in
help-seeker and supporter roles with approval from an institutional
review board, and workers were informed about data use (Liu et al.,
2021). Most conversations concerned the help-seekers’ own current
experiences, so the dialogues and our translations contain personal
disclosures. We use ESConv under its CC BY-NC 4.0 license, release the
translations under the same non-commercial terms, use them only for this
research, and make no attempt to identify participants. No new human
participants were involved.

## Use of LLMs

Claude was used to draft all experimental materials, to translate and
spot-check dialogues, to compare back-translations, to write analysis
code (with a coding agent), and to draft this manuscript. The authors
are responsible for the content.
