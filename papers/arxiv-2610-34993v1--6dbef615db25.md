# Reason or Recite: Complementary Specialists with an LLM Judge for Traffic Anomaly Reasoning

Mayur Kulkarni Affiliation: Stellarview AI, Pune, India
E-mail <mayur.k@stellarview.ai>

###### Abstract

Road anomalies such as collisions, violations, and sudden hazards demand
more from video understanding than detection: a practical system must
verify what happened, describe the scene, and explain the cause. We find
these competences pull in opposite directions for vision-language
models: closed-form questions reward precise, evidence-grounded
reasoning, while open-ended answers are scored by similarity to
reference text, where matching the annotation style matters as much as
reasoning correctly. We present Reason-or-Recite (RoR), a two-specialist
framework for the Traffic Anomaly Reasoning track of the 2026 AI City
Challenge. RoR (1) rewrites the official chain-of-thought annotations
into a structured, temporally grounded schema and LoRA-tunes a 9B
vision-language model on the enhanced traces; (2) fine-tunes a
4-bit-quantized 27B model on answers alone, absorbing the annotation
style; and (3) selects between the two candidates per question with a
training-free LLM judge. RoR ranks first on the in-domain leaderboard
with mean score 0.6788.

## 1 Introduction

Understanding anomalous events in transportation goes well beyond
flagging that something unusual happened: a practical system must verify
whether a specific event occurred, describe the scene and actors,
localize the event in time, and explain _why_ it happened. Track 3 of
the 2026 AI City Challenge \[37\] operationalizes this via the Traffic
Anomaly Reasoning (TAR) benchmark: a unified video-language model must
answer ten heterogeneous task types, nine of them scored, over CCTV
traffic footage, ranging from binary event verification and
multiple-choice QA to open-ended causal analysis, scene description, and
video summarization.

Two properties of the benchmark shape our solution. First, the task
types demand _different competences_: closed-form questions (binary and
multiple-choice) reward precise, evidence-grounded reasoning, while the
seven open-ended tasks are scored with BERTScore \[55\] against a single
reference answer. Second, similarity to a single reference rewards
matching that reference’s register and content-selection policy as much
as answering correctly, and the training annotations, machine-generated
by a VLM auto-labeling pipeline (Sec. 3.2), are the only available
sample of the style being scored against: a model that reasons _better_
than its supervision can paradoxically score _worse_.

We therefore decouple the two competences. For evidence-grounded
reasoning, we rewrite the official chain-of-thought annotations into a
structured, evidence-grounded schema (\<scene\>, \<query\>, \<evidence\>
with timestamps, \<reason\>, \<conclusion\>) using an off-the-shelf LLM,
and LoRA-tune \[17\] a Qwen 3.5 9B VLM on the enhanced traces (Secs. 4.2
and 4.3). For open-ended tasks, we fine-tune a larger Qwen 3.6 27B model
with QLoRA \[7\] on the _answers only_, letting it absorb the training
annotation distribution directly (Sec. 4.4). At inference, a frozen
Qwen 3.6 27B _judge_ selects the better candidate per question, with no
additional training (Sec. 4.5).

Our contributions are:

- •
  Structured reasoning enhancement (TAR-RE): an LLM-driven re-annotation
  of the TAR reasoning traces into an explicit, temporally grounded
  schema, substantially improving closed-form QA after fine-tuning.
- •
  Answer-distribution specialization for reference-scored generation: an
  answer-only QLoRA fine-tune of a larger model that absorbs the
  auto-labeled annotation distribution directly and outperforms
  reasoning-tuned models on the BERTScore-scored open-ended tasks,
  showing that under single-reference scoring, matching the annotation
  distribution can outweigh gains in reasoning quality.
- •
  Evidence-gated per-question arbitration: a training-free LLM judge
  that selects between the specialists per question; unconstrained, it
  scores below the stronger specialist due to position and family bias,
  while our conservative design gains over both.
- •
  First place: 1st on the Track 3 in-domain (TAR) leaderboard with mean
  score 0.6788, including perfect binary event verification.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Vision-Language Models for Video Understanding

Open multimodal LLMs have matured rapidly into capable video
reasoners \[1, 40, 3, 25, 56, 58\], with recent backbones built for
long, temporally rich video \[43\]: Qwen3-VL interleaves textual
timestamps with video tokens over a 256K context \[2\]. On the language
side, Qwen3 \[47\] integrates a fast non-thinking mode and an explicit
thinking mode into a single model, a reasoning/answering duality our
two-specialist design mirrors at the system level. We build on this
line: the reasoning specialist applies LoRA \[17\] to Qwen 3.5 9B \[32\]
and the answer specialist QLoRA \[7\] to Qwen 3.6 27B \[33\], its
architecturally identical, further post-trained successor. A
complementary line grounds video reasoning in time, via timestamp-bound
features, supervised event boundaries, timestamped causal traces, or
temporally indexed evidence banks \[34, 18, 14, 44, 10\]; rather than
encoding time in the architecture or the retrieval stage, our structured
traces make timestamped evidence explicit in the supervision itself.

### 2.2 From Anomaly Detection to Reasoning Supervision

Traffic and surveillance anomaly analysis began as _detection_: weakly
supervised methods score video snippets from video-level labels \[35,
39\], supported by traffic-specific benchmarks spanning dashcam and CCTV
views \[11, 50, 26, 46, 5\]. Such systems localize anomalies but cannot
explain them, so a second wave reframes anomaly analysis as _language
understanding_ via multimodal LLMs \[8, 36, 54\], prompted foundation
models \[52\], or verbalized learning \[51\]; most recently,
VAD-R1 \[19\] and VAU-R1 \[59\] make the model _reason_ before
answering, optimizing the reasoning with GRPO-style reinforcement
learning. How that reasoning should be supervised is a question of its
own: chain-of-thought prompting improves answer accuracy \[45\], the
_structure_ of the trace matters \[4, 49\], distillation trains directly
on rationales \[16, 53, 27\], with recent evidence that _rewriting_
traces to fit the student is what makes it succeed \[22\], while
reinforcement learning abandons trace supervision altogether \[6, 12\].
Auto-generated supervision, finally, carries the generator’s stylistic
fingerprint \[30\], an effect central to our setting (Sec. 3.2). We
improve the _supervision_ rather than the optimizer: restructuring weak
auto-labeled traces into an evidence-grounded schema is far cheaper than
RL rollouts and remains compatible with lightweight LoRA fine-tuning.

### 2.3 Output Selection and LLM-as-Judge

Reliability can be improved by choosing among candidate outputs rather
than trusting a single decode: self-consistency marginalizes over
sampled reasoning paths \[42\], LLM-Blender ranks and fuses outputs from
multiple models \[20\], strong LLMs have proven reliable automated
judges of response quality \[57\], and routers such as RouteLLM \[29\]
instead pick a model _before_ inference to trade off cost and quality.
Our judge differs on both axes: training-free, it arbitrates _after_
generation between two heterogeneous fine-tuned specialists, per
question.

### 2.4 VLM Solutions in the AI City Challenge

Successive AI City Challenges \[41, 38\] have pushed VLMs into
fine-grained traffic understanding: CityLLaVA \[9\] established
bounding-box visual prompting and best-view selection, later extended
with domain-aware expert priors and QLoRA tuning \[15\]; other strong
entries decompose captions into spatial and temporal segments \[28\] or
combine spatially guided fine-tuning with caption refinement \[31\].
Closest to our design, Kyem *et al*. \[23\] route statically by task
type between two specialists, and Kachhadiya *et al*. \[21\] dispatch
each question type to the agent with the best validation accuracy; both
rely on a precomputed routing table, whereas our judge arbitrates per
question; our annotation enhancement restructures reasoning traces
rather than decomposing captions.

## 3 Task and Dataset

### 3.1 The TAR Benchmark

The TAR training set comprises 44,040 annotations over 3,670 CCTV videos
(965 anomalous, 2,705 normal; $`{\sim}26.1`$ hours) drawn from eight
public datasets \[37\], including UCF-Crime \[35\], TAD \[26, 46\],
SO-TAD \[5\], VAD-R1 \[19\], and Accident-Bench \[13\]. Each annotation
attaches a question, its gold answer, and a chain-of-thought reasoning
trace to a video. The ten task types fall into three groups: _Basic_
(event verification (binary), event verification with explanation,
multiple-choice QA, multiple-choice QA with explanation, and open-ended
QA); _Scene_ (scene description and video summarization); and _Temporal_
(temporal localization, causal linkage, and event description). The
in-domain test set contains 960 human-verified annotations over 80 clips
trimmed from 17 public YouTube videos. Binary and multiple-choice
answers are scored by exact accuracy (regex extraction), all open-ended
tasks by BERTScore F1 \[55\] (roberta-large, rescaled); the leaderboard
mean is the unweighted average over nine task types (temporal
localization was excluded from scoring by the organizers).

### 3.2 How the Annotations Were Made, and Why It Matters

The 44,040 training annotations are machine-generated by a three-stage
VLM auto-labeling pipeline \[37\]: Gemini 3.1 Pro produces a caption and
a structured event description for each video, and Gemma-4 converts
these into multi-task Q&A pairs with chain-of-thought reasoning. For 910
of the 3,670 videos, pre-existing NVIDIA human annotations served as
supplementary context; the test references, in contrast, are
human-verified \[37\]. Before training anything, we examined the
training annotations, with programmatic checks across the full set and
manual inspection of a small sample of videos; the four defect classes
this surfaced (Fig. 1, quantified in Tab. 1) shaped every design
decision in RoR.

Figure 1: Four defect classes of the official TAR annotations, with
verbatim training examples; each is detailed in Sec. 3.2. Panel (c)

#### (a) The reasoning cites sources the model cannot see.

Reasoning distilled from a labeling pipeline inherits the pipeline’s
inputs: many traces justify an answer not from the video but from the
captions and annotator notes the teacher models were prompted with
(Fig. 1a). Conservative phrase matching (“ground truth”, “the provided
description”, _etc_.) flags 10.1% of scene-description reasoning and
$`{\sim}1\%`$ overall, a lower bound since only explicit wording is
detectable; fine-tuning on such traces teaches a model to appeal to an
authority it will never have.

#### (b) Timestamps are everywhere, in no consistent format.

Temporal grounding permeates the supervision: 66.1% of all annotations
contain at least one timestamp, most inside the reasoning itself
(Tab. 1), in chaotic notation: four conventions coexist, often within a
single item, 911 timestamps use a colon as a decimal separator, and 113
gold answers mix two conventions inside one JSON object (Fig. 1b).

#### (c) Some annotations contradict the video.

Manual inspection of sampled clips surfaced annotations whose premise or
timing is simply wrong (Fig. 1c), including an entire question family
premised on a collision that never occurs: all task types derive from
the same event description, so one upstream hallucination propagates
into every task for that video. We deliberately do not relabel, since
gold outcomes anchor our regeneration (Sec. 4.2), but such noise bounds
what any method can extract from this training distribution.

#### (d) The reasoning asserts without inspecting the timeline.

Excluding scene description, whose global character needs no timestamps,
a trace anchors a median of two observations to a timestamp, and 33.1%
anchor none. The verification case is sharpest: when a binary question’s
gold answer is _No_, establishing that absence requires scanning the
whole video, yet 46.1% of “No” traces cite no timestamp (Fig. 1d),
teaching a model to output verdicts about a timeline it never inspects.

Beyond these four classes, the traces are uniformly shallow: the mean
trace length, computed per task type, ranges from 109 words on the
shortest task to 244 on the longest, and the traces are largely a
restatement of the answer they should derive (token-level ROUGE-L \[24\]
between the two reaches 0.33; Tab. 1). Defects (a)–(d) and this
shallowness motivate regenerating the reasoning specialist’s supervision
(Sec. 4.2). The same provenance cuts the other way, too: these
machine-generated answers are the only available sample of the register
the open-ended tasks are scored against, the property the answer
specialist exploits (Sec. 4.4).

## 4 Method

### 4.1 Overview

RoR turns the diagnosis of Sec. 3 into a system in three parts: TAR-RE
(Sec. 4.2) repairs the training supervision; two specialists are then
fine-tuned, one per scoring regime: the reasoning specialist on the
regenerated TAR-RE traces (Sec. 4.3), the answer specialist on the gold
answers alone, with the reasoning stripped entirely (Sec. 4.4); and at
inference both specialists answer every test question independently, and
a frozen, training-free judge selects between the two candidates per
question (Sec. 4.5).

#### MCQ with explanation is answered by construction.

Each MCQ-OE test item restates an MCQ item on the same video: the same
stem and candidate options, re-shuffled and occasionally lightly
re-worded. The multiple-choice inference therefore already contains the
open-ended answer, and we generate nothing for MCQ-OE: the option text
of the final MCQ prediction is matched to the corresponding option of
the MCQ-OE twin, and that option’s wording is submitted verbatim: a
benchmark-specific shortcut, orthogonal to the method. Under
single-reference BERTScore (Sec. 3.2), reproducing the correct option’s
exact wording is the strongest submission available whenever the
underlying MCQ prediction is right.

### 4.2 Structured Reasoning Enhancement (TAR-RE)

TAR-RE (_TAR Reasoning Enhanced_) repairs the supervision defects of
Sec. 3.2 in two steps: normalize time, then regenerate the reasoning.

#### Unified timestamps.

We first canonicalize every timestamp in every field to a single textual
form, \<X.Y seconds\>, parsing all the conventions catalogued in
Sec. 3.2 (Fig. 2, bottom). Decimal seconds need no base-60 arithmetic,
and the format matches the textual timestamps Qwen3-VL interleaves with
video tokens \[2\]: the supervision speaks the backbone’s native
representation of video time.

#### Regenerating the reasoning.

We then use Qwen 3.5 9B in thinking mode to rewrite every training
trace. For each item, the model receives the video together with the
task name, the question, the gold answer, and the original reasoning; it
deliberates in its native thinking channel and must emit the five-part
trace of Fig. 2 (scene, query, timestamped evidence, reason,
conclusion), separating what is _visible_ (evidence) from what is
_inferred_ (reason), under the per-section depth floors shown there and
total floors of 220 words for closed-form and 320 words for descriptive
tasks: brevity, not verbosity, is the failure mode of the official
traces. Two constraints keep the rewrite honest: the gold _outcome_ is
frozen (the Yes/No polarity, option letter, or timestamp values must
match the official answer, so regeneration cannot silently relabel the
data, including, deliberately, the erroneous labels of Sec. 3.2), and
the gold _wording_ is forbidden (a validator rejects any trace that
misses a structural tag, misses the depth floors, or contains the gold
answer verbatim; since the LLM samples at non-zero temperature, rejected
traces are simply regenerated until they pass).

#### Why these five sections.

The schema adapts the perception-to-cognition trace structure of
VAD-R1 \[19\] to the multi-task setting of TAR: global before local,
perception before cognition, visible fact separated from inference.
\<query\> pins down what to look for, since TAR spans ten task types
where VAD-R1 answers one fixed question; \<evidence\> ties each
observation to a timestamp; and \<conclusion\> paraphrases the derived
outcome, so the answer that follows the think block restates a result
rather than leaps to one.

Table 1 summarizes the effect: the traces become far longer yet _less_
similar to their answers, with ROUGE-L overlap roughly halved on every
open-ended task. Over 99% use the unified timestamp format exclusively,
the median number of time-anchored observations per trace rises from two
to 16–30, and the share of traces citing no moment at all falls from
33.1% to 0.2% (scene description excluded). These statistics measure
structure, not factual grounding: defects (a), (b), and (d) of Sec. 3.2
are removed by construction, but nothing here certifies that every cited
observation is correct; the downstream effect on accuracy is measured in
Sec. 5.3.

#### A joint target for the Scene-group tasks.

Scene description and video summarization are the two tasks whose
prompts carry no distinguishing content. For the answer specialist
(Sec. 4.4), rather than train them as separate items, we merge each
video’s pair into a single coarse-to-fine target: the scene description
first, the summarization second, delimited as
\<scene\>…\</scene\> \<summary\>…\</summary\>, from which the two
answers are sliced per task at inference; emitting the global scene
before the event narrative forces the model to commit to coarse
structure before fine-grained detail. The reasoning specialist, by
contrast, trains on all ten tasks in the single think-then-answer format
of Sec. 4.3.

### 4.3 Reasoning Specialist: 9B LoRA on TAR-RE

The reasoning specialist, a Qwen 3.5 9B VLM adapted to the TAR-RE traces
with LoRA \[17\], exists to _reason before it answers_: unlike the
answer specialist, it is supervised to reproduce the full trace, not
merely the outcome.

#### Training target: think, then answer.

We exploit Qwen 3.5’s native thinking channel. For every task type, the
target places the complete five-part TAR-RE trace of Sec. 4.2 inside the
model’s think block, and the gold outcome immediately after it:

```math
\texttt{<think>}\;\langle\text{scene}\rangle\ldots\langle\text{/conclusion}\rangle\;\texttt{</think>}\;\{\text{answer}\}.
```

The question and video tokens are masked from the loss; supervision
covers trace and answer alike, and all ten task types share this one
format. The model deliberates in the channel the backbone was designed
for, and answer extraction is a single deterministic step: we decode
greedily, split on \</think\>, and read the text that follows, so
closed-form outputs reach the exact-match scorer without task-specific
parsing.

#### Adaptation.

We fine-tune on the $`80\%`$ TAR-RE training split and keep the
checkpoint with the lowest validation loss; the adapter placement
(shared with the answer specialist), optimizer, schedule, and video
sampling are in Sec. 5.1.

### 4.4 Answer Specialist: 27B QLoRA, Answer-Only SFT

The answer specialist is a Qwen 3.6 27B VLM fine-tuned with QLoRA \[7\],
and it exists to win the seven open-ended tasks. Since these are scored
against references whose register only the training answers sample
(Sec. 3.2), we treat them as distribution matching rather than reasoning
and supervise on the answer alone: no trace, no schema, no deliberation.
With no reasoning scaffold to lean on, the model must carry the register
in its weights, a capacity question on which the 27B backbone \[33\]
outperforms the 9B (Sec. 5.3). Training uses the backbone’s non-thinking
mode with the chat template’s empty think block masked from the loss
(Sec. 5.1), and targets the merged coarse-to-fine Scene-group answer
(Sec. 4.2). The result is the “recite” half of RoR: the model routinely
emits near-paraphrases of references it has never seen (the splits are
video-disjoint; Sec. 5.1), and Fig. 3 traces the specialists’ open-ended
gap to register and content selection rather than perception.

#### Where it excels, and where it does not.

Stronger on the open-ended tasks, the model is weaker on the closed-form
ones, where it asserts while the reasoning specialist verifies: on
validation items whose verdict hinges on inspecting the video, it
recites the salient prior (a collision implies a red light was run)
where the reasoning specialist cites timestamped \<evidence\> and
returns the correct verdict. BERTScore barely separates such a false
verdict from a true one ($`0.57`$ vs. $`0.61`$), but the exact-match
tasks punish it outright; this per-question complementarity is what the
judge (Sec. 4.5) exists to exploit.

### 4.5 Judge-Guided Selection

For every test question (MCQ-OE excepted; Sec. 4.1), a frozen,
out-of-the-box Qwen 3.6 27B receives the video frames, the question, and
both candidate answers and returns which one to submit: no fine-tuning
and no precomputed routing table. The prompt casts the judge as a
_conservative verifier_ rather than a free selector, a design dictated
by its measured position bias and family self-preference (Sec. 5.3): the
candidate expected to win, the output of the specialist with the higher
validation score on the question’s task type, is presented last and is
kept unless clearly contradicted by the video, and a brief per-task
instruction states what separates a correct answer from an incorrect
one. A deterministic pre-filter settles uncontested items, so the judge
adjudicates only where the candidates genuinely disagree.

## 5 Experiments

### 5.1 Implementation Details

#### Hardware and quantization.

All training runs on a single GPU: the 9B reasoning specialist on one
NVIDIA RTX 5090 (32 GB), the 27B answer specialist on one NVIDIA
RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell (96 GB), with the 27B’s frozen base quantized to
4-bit NormalFloat while bf16 LoRA adapters train; the vision tower is
exempt and stays in bf16, since the answer specialist’s value depends on
perception.

#### Training stack.

At the time of writing, no widely used fine-tuning framework offers
stable support for video inputs to the Qwen 3.5/3.6 series, so we
implement our own training and evaluation pipeline on top of
transformers, TRL, PEFT, and bitsandbytes, keeping prompt construction
byte-identical between training and inference. A chunked loss computes
the cross-entropy in slices, never materializing the full logits tensor
over the large vocabulary; we release the pipeline with our code.

#### Fine-tuning recipe.

Both specialists share one recipe. Their backbones are hybrids \[32\]
interleaving Gated DeltaNet linear attention \[48\] with full softmax
attention in a fixed 3:1 pattern, with a SwiGLU MLP in every layer;
rank-64 LoRA adapters ($`\alpha=128`$, dropout $`0.05`$, no bias) attach
to every linear projection of all three block types: the full-attention
$`q`$/$`k`$/$`v`$/$`o`$ maps, the Gated DeltaNet query/key/value streams
with their decay, update, and output gates, and the SwiGLU gate/up/down
maps, twelve module types in all; the mixer’s non-matrix parameters, the
vision tower, and the token embeddings stay frozen. Training uses
maximum sequence length $`5120`$, a fixed $`32`$ frames per video at a
per-frame budget of $`131{,}072`$ pixels (floor $`3{,}136`$), AdamW with
a peak learning rate of $`2\times 10^{-4}`$ on a cosine schedule with
$`3\%`$ warmup and no weight decay, a per-device batch size of $`1`$
with gradient accumulation $`16`$, bf16 precision, gradient
checkpointing, and FlashAttention-2; the 9B trains for four epochs, the
27B for two. The 27B otherwise differs only as Sec. 4 dictates, training
the same adapters over its NF4-quantized base and keeping them unmerged
at inference. All inference, including the judge’s, uses greedy
decoding.

#### Data splits and hygiene.

The TAR-RE data is split $`80/10/10`$ into training, validation, and
internal test sets by _video_: all annotations of a video share a split,
so no validation or internal-test video appears in training under any
task type, and the assignment is stratified by source dataset (seed
$`42`$), with uniform task weighting. Both specialists train on the
$`80\%`$ split; the validation split drives every development-time
decision (checkpoint selection, the judge’s prompt and pre-filter; the
judge’s weights stay frozen); the internal test split is never trained
on, drives no development-time decision, and is reserved for the
ablations of Sec. 5.3, whose numbers therefore estimate uncontaminated
generalization.

### 5.2 Main Results

Tab. 2 reports the final in-domain (TAR) leaderboard. RoR ranks first
with a mean score of 0.6788. The win comes from breadth rather than a
single task: our submission attains the best score on four of the nine
scored task types (perfect binary event verification (BCQ, $`100\%`$),

description) and remains within $`0.025`$ of the per-column best on
every other task. The leaderboard scores the combined submission; the
per-column pattern matches the division of labor seen on validation

#### Reasoning supervision.

Table 3 isolates the value of the TAR-RE traces on the 9B backbone.
Trace supervision decides the closed-form tasks: multiple choice reaches
$`100\%`$ and binary verification also gains. The picture inverts on
BCQ-OE, which the answer-only arm wins by $`5.1`$ BERTScore points,
while open QA and scene description sit within two points. Reasoning
supervision pays where answers are scored exactly and costs where
similarity to the reference register is scored, the division of labor
the two specialists instantiate.

#### Selection strategy.

Table 4 isolates the selection mechanism on the internal test split.
Because the answer specialist wins every free-text task on average on
validation, a static per-task router collapses to the 27B-only row, so
$`0.4958`$ is the ceiling of any routing table. Judge-guided selection
scores $`0.5043`$; gains over the 27B can arise only where the judge
selected the reasoning specialist’s answer, so this margin is realized
per-question flip recovery, not routing. The prompt is what makes the
recovery positive: a neutral “pick the better answer” rubric scores
$`0.4887`$, below the 27B alone, overturning good defaults on position
and verbosity more often than content earns correct overrides, so the
$`0.0156`$ between the two rubrics is purely the value of constraining
the judge. An oracle reaches $`0.5347`$; the judge recovers roughly a
fifth of that gap, the remainder being the price of guarding against its
own biases, quantified below.

#### Complementarity, not routing.

The oracle’s headroom is located in _flips_: items whose better answer
comes from the specialist that loses that task on average, precisely the
answers static task-to-model routing \[23, 21\] discards by
construction. On the internal test split, flips are 41% of BCQ-OE items
(worth $`+0.060`$ per flipped item under oracle selection), 49% of
causal linkage ($`+0.035`$), 37% of temporal description ($`+0.027`$),
24% of open-ended QA ($`+0.024`$), and 13% of scene description
($`+0.008`$); only video summarization, at 0%, would be fully served by
a routing table. The recoverable score concentrates in the
reasoning-heavy tasks, hence arbitration per question rather than per
task.

#### Judge behavior.

Three measurements shape how the judge is deployed. First, position
bias \[57\]: under the neutral rubric the judge favors the
last-presented candidate on 65–79% of items regardless of author, and
only 51.6% of its verdicts survive swapping the order (chance level). A
judge this position-driven cannot be granted free choice; the
winner-last ordering of Sec. 4.5 aligns this prior with the
validation-favored candidate, so an override must be earned by visual
evidence rather than by slot. Second, self-preference \[30\]: averaged
over both orders, the neutral judge selects the same-family 27B
candidate on 42.8% of items against the oracle’s 37.8%. Third, who can
judge at all: a 9B sibling returns the last-presented candidate on every
item, so the frozen 27B is the smallest family member with any content
sensitivity, and neither specialist can take the role, since judging a
comparison containing one’s own answer forfeits independence.

### 5.4 What Did Not Work

Tab. 5 lists our main negative results; three lessons stand out.

#### A single reference cannot be paraphrased toward.

Every post-hoc transform that reworded a generated answer regressed: LLM
rewriting, cleanup, fusion, and every truncation. The metric rewards
token-level proximity to one specific reference, and a paraphrase made
without that reference in hand moves away from it on average; proximity
must instead be trained into the weights: hence the answer specialist’s
design, and why the judge selects rather than edits. The only edits that
survived injected verifiable content extracted with Gemini 3.1 Pro and
left surrounding text untouched: transcribing the location overlay
burned into each test frame (read from a native-resolution crop) into
the scene descriptions ($`+0.004`$ LB), and anchoring open-ended answers
to event windows Gemini localized from cropped clips, checked against
sibling-task facts ($`+0.008`$ LB).

#### The remaining headroom points to perception, not capacity.

On scene description, grafting in the references’ vehicle sentences by
oracle gains $`+0.06`$, so headroom exists. But every deployable route
to it traded a small recall gain for a larger precision loss, and every
capacity lever (vision-tower LoRA, higher rank, more frames, more
pixels) tied or regressed: the binding constraint is the precision of
what the model asserts it saw, and only fine-tuning improved it.

#### Local validation is not a leaderboard proxy.

Our best-ever validation configuration for scene description scored
$`0.077`$ _below_ the shipped model on the leaderboard. The two sets are
drawn differently: the organizer test set is dominated by short,
collision-heavy clips (median length $`\sim`$10 s), whereas our local
split follows the released-data distribution. A configuration tuned on
the latter (few frames, very high resolution) reads and transcribes
storefront signage in the sign-dense test domain, collapsing precision.
After this inversion we trusted validation only for the component it
directly measures and verified configurations on the evaluation server,
as permitted.

## 6 Conclusion

We presented RoR, the winning entry to the Traffic Anomaly Reasoning
track of the 2026 AI City Challenge: an annotation audit, reasoning
supervision regenerated into a structured, timestamp-grounded schema
(TAR-RE), two complementary specialists trained on it, and a
training-free LLM judge arbitrating per question, ranking first with
mean score 0.6788. Our analysis shows unconstrained multimodal judging
is unreliable; position-bias control is what makes arbitration
profitable. The broader lesson: when a benchmark’s supervision is
auto-labeled and its metric scores similarity to a single reference,
reasoning quality and reference matching are separable objectives, and
conflating them in one model leaves accuracy on the table.
