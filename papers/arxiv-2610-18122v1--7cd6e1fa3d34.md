###### Abstract

Typed decision models (TDMs) return a probability for each allowed
answer instead of generating text, which makes them candidates for
cheaply replacing LLM judges that compare two answers. Whether they
share the biases of LLM judges is untested. We run the CALM benchmark’s
bias tests on eight TDMs and seven LLM judges of 2024 and 2026. Every
TDM is biased, in three to eight of twelve tests. Emotional tone affects
them most, and rewriting the better answer in such a tone lowers the
accuracy of a typical TDM from 99% to 42%. TDMs are more biased than
2026 LLM judges. All eight change verdicts when the answers swap places,
which the 2026 LLM judges hardly do, and several side with an answer
that most people allegedly prefer or that a famous model allegedly
wrote. Yet both kinds of judge are tripped up by the same prompts. The
TDMs’ probabilities show that most tests mainly flip verdicts the TDM
was unsure of, and that good calibration does not protect against bias.
As TDMs are more consistent and 37 times cheaper than 2026 LLM judges,
our results suggest they can be attractive judges if their biases are
tested for the task.¹¹ 1 Our code and data:
[https://github.com/fabian-lu/TDM_as_a_Judge](https://github.com/fabian-lu/TDM_as_a_Judge).

## 1 Introduction

Beyond generating text, large language models (LLMs) are increasingly
used to make decisions. A common one is evaluating the outputs of other
models, known as LLM-as-a-judge. In pairwise judging, an LLM reads two
answers to one question and decides which is better (Zheng et al.,
2023). However, a text generator is an indirect, slow and expensive tool
for such decisions, since it is trained to continue text rather than to
estimate how likely an answer is correct (Deußer et al., 2026).

LLMs are also known to be biased when judging. Their verdicts might
change with cues that should not matter, for example the order (Wang
et al., 2024) or the length (Saito et al., 2023) of the answers. Ye
et al. (2025) collect twelve such biases in CALM, a Comprehensive
Assessment of Language Model Judge Biases, and find that even strong LLM
judges are swayed by several of them.

Typed decision models (TDMs), also called System One models (TypeSafe
AI, 2026a) or direct-decision models (Gao et al., 2026), offer a more
direct route. Given an input in natural language and one or more
questions, each with a fixed set of options, a TDM returns a probability
for each option in a single pass, without generating text (Tang and
Zheng, 2026). TypeSafe, for example, presents its TDM Jev as faster and
cheaper than LLMs, with calibrated probabilities (TypeSafe AI, 2026a),
and TDMs are already used as judges (Li et al., 2026). These advantages
may make judging far more common (Hill, 2026), and with it its biases.

Most TDMs, however, are LLMs fine-tuned to return probabilities instead
of text. The open Perplexity Decider, for example, is Qwen3.8-27B with a
decision head in place of its text output (Perplexity, 2026). TDMs may
therefore inherit the biases of LLM judges, and initial evidence
suggests that they share some of them (Hu et al., 2026), but a
systematic test of the biases known for LLM judges is missing. We close
this gap by applying CALM’s method to TDMs. Our contributions are:

- •
  To our knowledge, the first systematic test of known LLM-judge biases
  on TDMs, compared with seven LLM judges on the same prompts.
- •
  A test of their promise, calibrated probabilities.
- •
  Our data, which can be used for future benchmarking of TDMs and LLM
  judges.

## 2 Related Work

##### Biases of LLM judges.

Zheng et al. (2023) described three biases of LLM judges of chat
assistants. The judges prefer an answer for its position (Wang et al.,
2024, see also Shi et al., 2025), prefer longer answers (Dubois et al.,
2024), more than humans do (Saito et al., 2023), and favour their own
outputs (Panickssery et al., 2024). Later studies added further cues.
CoBBLEr tests six cognitive biases, among them bandwagon and distraction
effects, and finds signs of bias in about 40% of comparisons (Koo
et al., 2024), and fake references and rich formatting sway LLM judges
as well as human ones (Chen et al., 2024). LLMs fine-tuned for judging,
such as JudgeLM (Zhu et al., 2025) and Prometheus (Kim et al., 2024),
are no exception (Huang et al., 2025), and surveys review these biases
(Gu et al., 2026; Li et al., 2025; Li et al., 2024). CALM collects
twelve of them in one benchmark (Ye et al., 2025), and the nine that
apply to TDMs are the biases we test (Table 1 and Section 3).

##### Reward models.

Reward models come closest to TDMs. A reward model is typically an LLM
whose text output is replaced by a single score for a response (Ouyang
et al., 2022). Reward models score responses for preference training
(Ouyang et al., 2022) and are tested, like judges, on which of two
responses they prefer (Lambert et al., 2025). Like LLM judges, they
favour longer responses (Singhal et al., 2024).

##### TDMs as judges.

Studies of TDMs as judges have so far compared their accuracy and cost
with those of LLM judges (Li et al., 2026; Rao and Callison-Burch, 2026;
Denney and DiGiuseppe, 2026), also for the security of AI agents and for
radiology reports (Wang and Gao, 2026; Huang et al., 2026). Whether
irrelevant cues change their verdicts has been studied cue by cue,
mostly on Jev, for the order of answers and options (Li et al., 2026;
Li, 2026; Gao et al., 2026; Denney and DiGiuseppe, 2026), appended
opinions and unrelated text (Hu et al., 2026), the source to which an
argument is attributed (Loi, 2026), the names of options (Rafe and Das,
2026; Sun et al., 2026; Azizi et al., 2026) and the style of an answer
(Li et al., 2026). Section 4 compares their findings with ours.

##### Positioning our work.

LLM judges have been tested against a long catalogue of biases and TDMs
only cue by cue, and no study compares the two under the same tests. We
apply CALM’s catalogue, data and metrics to eight TDMs and to seven LLM
judges on the same prompts. We do not include reward models. Since a
reward model typically scores each response independently, five of
CALM’s nine tests do not apply to it, as they alter the order of the
responses or refer to them by label.

## 3 Methodology

We take CALM’s items, perturbations, judge instruction and metrics as
the basis of our method.²² 2 CALM’s data and code:
[https://github.com/Y0oMu/LLM-Judge-Bias-Dataset](https://github.com/Y0oMu/LLM-Judge-Bias-Dataset),
commit f6248e9. TDMs, however, are queried differently from LLMs and
return probabilities rather than text. We therefore depart from CALM or
extend it in several places and say explicitly where we do. This section
can be read without CALM’s paper. Appendix A lists all differences from
CALM at a glance.

### 3.1 Data and Tests

##### Task.

A judge $`J`$ receives a prompt $`P=(I,Q,R_{1},R_{2})`$ made of an
instruction $`I`$, a user question $`Q`$ and two responses, of which
$`R_{1}`$ is known to be the better one. It returns a verdict
$`y=J(P)`$, the response it prefers. A perturbation $`g`$ changes one
response or the instruction. In most tests, the change should not affect
the verdict, as in the invariance tests of Ribeiro et al. (2020). Adding
a fake citation to the worse response, for example, gives
$`\hat{P}=(I,Q,R_{1},g(R_{2}))`$. Since $`R_{2}`$ is still the worse
response, an unbiased judge returns $`\hat{y}=J(\hat{P})=y`$. CALM
defines twelve biases, and we test the nine in Table 1. The other three,
chain of thought, self-enhancement and refinement awareness, concern the
judge’s own reasoning or answers. A TDM writes neither, so they cannot
arise.

##### Data.

CALM built three datasets, of which we use two. The third serves only
refinement awareness, a bias we do not test. The _fact-related set_
holds 533 items from GSM8K, MATH and ScienceQA (Cobbe et al., 2021;
Hendrycks et al., 2021; Lu et al., 2022), which have a definite answer.
For each question, CALM’s authors had GPT-4-Turbo write two responses
that both reach this answer, a better one, $`R_{1}`$, meant to be
correct, complete, clear and coherent, and a worse one, $`R_{2}`$, just
as correct and complete but less clear and coherent. CALM uses this set
for the three biases whose perturbation rewrites a response, verbosity,
fallacy oversight and sentiment. Verbosity and fallacy oversight use 500
of its items and sentiment 150. The _alignment set_ holds 439 questions,
ranging from code to role play, from five public datasets (Durbin, 2023;
Léo, 2024; Intel, 2023; Durbin, 2024; xDAN, 2024) made for direct
preference optimisation (DPO) (Rafailov et al., 2023). Each question
comes with a preferred and a rejected response, which serve as $`R_{1}`$
and $`R_{2}`$. CALM uses this set for the other six biases, whose
perturbations leave the wording of the responses unchanged, so that real
user questions give them a wide range of topics. Appendix B shows an
item of each source and the prompt that wrote the responses of the
fact-related set.

##### Perturbations.

For each bias, CALM changes the original prompt in one way, and Table 1
shows an example for each. We call what the change adds or alters the
_cue_, for example the fake citation for authority bias. CALM creates
the cues in two ways. The cues of four biases depend on the content of a
response, the padding for verbosity, the broken reasoning for fallacy
oversight, the emotional tone for sentiment and the citation for
authority. GPT-4-Turbo therefore wrote them for each response separately
(exact prompts in Appendix C.1). The other cues are the same for every
item and come from fixed templates. For position bias the template swaps
the two responses, compassion fade replaces “Assistant A” and “Assistant
B” by model names (e.g., GPT-5.6 for $`R_{2}`$ and Llama-2-7B for
$`R_{1}`$), and bandwagon, distraction and diversity append a fixed
sentence, such as “90% of people believe that Assistant B is better in
this question” (examples in Appendix C.2). Most perturbations come in
several versions, for example four tones (cheerful, sad, angry, fearful)
on either response ($`R_{1}`$ or $`R_{2}`$) for sentiment. The perturbed
prompts of one bias, over all its versions, form its _test_ (Table 2).

We change CALM’s data in one place. CALM’s data always shows the better
response first, so a judge with a position bias towards the first
response would look accurate instead of biased. We therefore shuffle the
order and show the better response first in half of the items.

##### Cues on either response.

Where a cue sits, on $`R_{1}`$ or on $`R_{2}`$, decides whether it can
harm the verdict. A judge that rewards a cue picks the response with the
cue more often, which hurts only when the cue sits on the worse
response. A judge that punishes a cue avoids that response, which hurts
only when the cue sits on the better one. In CALM, padding (verbosity
bias) targets the worse response, and so do the citations (authority
bias) and claimed majorities (bandwagon bias) in its examples. For a
claimed majority and the name of a strong LLM (compassion fade bias)
this is enough, since both speak for the response they back. For other
cues the direction is open. A fake citation may lend authority or raise
doubt, padding may look thorough or long-winded, and an identity note
(diversity bias) may help or hurt. Beyond CALM, we therefore also put
citations and padding on the better response, the padding written with
CALM’s prompt (Appendix C.1), and put identity notes on each response in
turn, as CALM does with emotional tones (sentiment bias). A neutral
identity note, “software engineer”, serves as a control that shows the
effect of adding any note.

### 3.2 Judges

##### LLM judges.

Every judge, TDM or LLM, receives CALM’s instruction, which asks it to
compare the two responses on six criteria, such as helpfulness and
accuracy, and to ignore their order, their length and the names of the
assistants (exact prompt in Appendix D.1). The LLM judges answer with a
short explanation that ends in a verdict, the TDMs with a probability
for each response. Like CALM’s tests of these nine biases, all our tests
are pairwise comparisons without scores. We send our prompts to seven
LLM judges. Two are judges of CALM that are still served, GPT-3.5 Turbo,
which CALM calls ChatGPT, and GPT-4o (2024-05-13). Five are LLMs of
2026: GPT-6.1 Sol, Claude Sonnet 5.5, Gemini 3.8 Flash, Claude Haiku 5.5
and Qwen3.8-27B. The two groups show how LLM judges have changed from
CALM’s judges of 2024 to those of 2026. We query Claude Sonnet 5.5
through Anthropic’s API (Anthropic, 2026) and the others through
OpenRouter (OpenRouter, 2026a), with the settings in Table 5
(Appendix D).

##### TDMs.

We test eight TDMs that OpenRouter’s Decisions API (OpenRouter, 2026b)
offered for choice questions in early October 2026: Jev 1.13 (TypeSafe
AI, 2026a; TypeSafe AI, 2026b), Kev 4B (Palmer, 2026), Tev1 4B (Together
AI, 2026), Solar Decide (Upstage, 2026), D1 (Liquid AI, 2026),
Perplexity Decider 27B in version 1.1 (Perplexity, 2026), and Clef and
Clef Flash (Cloudflare, 2026). A TDM returns a probability for each
option, and we take the option with the higher probability as its
verdict. Five of the eight are LLMs from the Qwen family, fine-tuned to
score options: Kev, Tev1, Perplexity Decider and the two Clef models.
Perplexity Decider and Clef are built on Qwen3.8-27B, one of our LLM
judges, which lets us compare a TDM with its own base LLM.

Little is known about how the form of a request affects TDMs, so we
convert CALM’s prompt into three formats with the same text
(Appendix D.2). The _CALM format_, which our main results use, keeps
CALM’s prompt as the material to be judged and turns its instruction
into one question with the options A and B. The _native format_ follows
TypeSafe’s documentation (TypeSafe AI, 2026b) and gives the question,
each response and any inserted sentence a field of their own. The
_decomposed format_, also recommended there, asks one question per
criterion of CALM’s instruction and takes the mean of the six
probabilities of A. Table 6 in Appendix D.2 gives the version of each
model as the API returns it.

Table 2 summarises the design. Nine tests follow CALM, one per bias, and
three are our additions (Section 3.1). Each judge receives the 13,251
perturbed prompts once and each of the 972 original prompts twice, so
that its consistency can be measured (Section 3.3), which gives 15,195
requests. A TDM receives them in each of the three formats, 364,680
requests for the eight models. A request gives no verdict when an LLM
judge refuses, fails or ends without a verdict, or when a TDM answers
50/50 (Appendix D.2). Such requests are rare, and we leave them out
(Tables 5 and 6 in Appendix D).

### 3.3 Metrics and Statistics

##### Robustness, consistency and accuracy.

For a test with the set of perturbed prompts $`\mathcal{D}`$, let
$`\hat{y}_{i}`$ be the verdict on prompt $`i\in\mathcal{D}`$, and
$`y_{i}`$ and $`y^{\prime}_{i}`$ the verdicts on the first and the
second sending of its original prompt. The robustness rate
$`\mathrm{RR}`$, the consistency rate $`\mathrm{CR}`$ and the accuracy
before and after the perturbation, $`\mathrm{Acc}`$ and
$`\widehat{\mathrm{Acc}}`$, are

```math
\displaystyle\mathrm{RR}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{I}(\hat{y}_{i}=y_{i}),\quad\mathrm{CR}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{I}(y^{\prime}_{i}=y_{i}),
```

```math
\displaystyle\mathrm{Acc}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{I}(y_{i}=R_{1}),\kern 5.0pt\widehat{\mathrm{Acc}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{I}(\hat{y}_{i}=R_{1}).
```

In fallacy oversight, the perturbation breaks the reasoning of
$`R_{1}`$, which makes $`R_{2}`$ the better response, so a changed
verdict is the correct reaction, and we report $`1-\mathrm{RR}`$.

##### Excess.

$`\mathrm{RR}`$ alone mixes bias with chance, since a judge may also
change its verdict when the same prompt is simply sent again. The
_excess_, the share of verdicts that the perturbation changes beyond
those that a repetition changes,

```math
E=(1-\mathrm{RR})-(1-\mathrm{CR})=\mathrm{CR}-\mathrm{RR},
```

is the bias in the eleven tests whose perturbation should not change the
verdict. It is close to zero for a judge that the perturbation does not
affect, however inconsistent the judge is. For an inconsistent judge,
$`E`$ can slightly understate the bias, since a change by chance can
undo a change by the cue. It never overstates it, and for our mostly
consistent judges the difference is small, so we keep this simple
measure. Appendix E.1 explains in more detail why we consider it
adequate. In fallacy oversight, the verdict should change, and the bias
is the share of verdicts that stay,

```math
E=\mathrm{RR}-(1-\mathrm{CR})=\mathrm{RR}+\mathrm{CR}-1.
```

##### Significance.

An excess may also arise by chance. If the cue has no effect, a
perturbed prompt is just another sending of its item, so it should not
matter which of an item’s answers counts as the repetition and which as
perturbed. A permutation test within items (Ernst, 2004) uses exactly
this. For an item with $`k`$ perturbed prompts, it draws at random which
of its $`k+1`$ answers plays the repetition and recomputes the excess,
$`\Delta^{(b)}`$ in the $`b`$-th of $`B=10{,}000`$ such shuffles. The
$`p`$-value is the share of shuffles whose excess is at least as large
in absolute value as the observed $`\Delta=\mathrm{CR}-\mathrm{RR}`$,

```math
p=\frac{1}{B+1}\Bigl(1+\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbb{I}\bigl(|\Delta^{(b)}|\geq|\Delta|\bigr)\Bigr)
```

(Phipson and Smyth, 2010). Two judges or two formats are compared in the
same way, by swapping at random, within each item, which of the two gave
an answer. We control the false discovery rate at 5% with the
Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995), separately
for the bias tests of the TDMs and of the LLM judges and for the
comparisons, and call a test significant when its adjusted $`p`$-value
is below 0.05. The test does not apply to fallacy oversight, where the
verdict should change, and there the interval of $`E`$ alone decides.

##### Intervals.

All intervals are 95% percentile intervals from a bootstrap over the
items of a test with 10,000 resamples (Efron, 1979), and the bias rule
below uses the interval of $`E`$.

##### Bias.

A significant excess can still be too small to matter. We therefore
speak of a _bias_ of a judge in a test only when the lower end of the
95% interval of $`E`$ exceeds a smallest effect of interest (Lakens,
2017), which we set to $`\delta=0.05`$, that is, when the judge reacts
wrongly to at least 5 of 100 prompts beyond chance. Since such a margin
is a judgment call, Appendix F.2 also reports the counts for
$`\delta=0.02`$ and 0.10. The rule is not corrected for multiple tests.

##### Probability shift.

A perturbation can also move a TDM’s probability without reversing its
verdict. Let $`p_{i}`$, $`\hat{p}_{i}`$ and $`p^{\prime}_{i}`$ be the
probability of the targeted response on the original, the perturbed and
the repeated prompt. The mean shift and, for scale, the noise floor are

```math
\Delta p=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}}(\hat{p}_{i}-p_{i}),\quad\sigma_{0}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}}|p^{\prime}_{i}-p_{i}|.
```

A positive shift means that the cue pulls the judge towards the targeted
response, a negative one that it pushes the judge away. In fallacy
oversight, a negative shift, away from the broken response, is the
correct reaction. The noise floor is how far the probability moves on a
plain repetition of the prompt, the scale against which a shift is read.

##### Calibration.

To check whether the probabilities match how often a judge is right, let
$`p_{i}`$ be the probability of the better response on an original
prompt. We report the Brier score (Brier, 1950) and the expected
calibration error (Naeini et al., 2015; Guo et al., 2017),

```math
\mathrm{Brier}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-p_{i})^{2},\kern 5.0pt\mathrm{ECE}=\sum_{b=1}^{10}\frac{n_{b}}{N}|\mathrm{acc}_{b}-\mathrm{conf}_{b}|,
```

The ECE sorts the verdicts by their confidence, the probability of the
chosen response, $`\max(p_{i},1-p_{i})`$, into ten equal bins $`b`$ from
0.5 to 1. A bin holds $`n_{b}`$ prompts, a share $`\mathrm{acc}_{b}`$ of
correct verdicts and the mean confidence
$`\mathrm{conf}_{b}=\frac{1}{n_{b}}\sum_{i\in b}\max(p_{i},1-p_{i})`$. A
50/50 answer counts as half correct.

##### When verdicts flip.

To see which verdicts a cue flips, a logistic mixed model (Bates et al., 2015) relates the probability that the verdict of model $`j`$ on prompt
$`i`$ flips, $`f_{ij}=1`$, to how sure the judge was of its original
verdict, the margin $`m_{ij}=|p_{R_{1}}-0.5|`$, to whether that verdict
was correct, $`c_{ij}`$, and to the log length ratio $`\lambda_{i}`$ of
the two responses. Fixed effects of the test $`t(i)`$, the model and the
source $`s(i)`$ and a random effect
$`u_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{u}^{2})`$ of the item $`k(i)`$ account
for the remaining differences (Appendix H.2),

```math
\begin{split}\operatorname{logit}P(f_{ij}=1)&=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}m_{ij}+\beta_{2}c_{ij}+\beta_{3}\lambda_{i}\\
&\quad+\alpha_{t(i)}+\gamma_{j}+\eta_{s(i)}+u_{k(i)}.\end{split}
```

Its coefficients show, for instance, whether close calls flip more often
than confident verdicts. Further mixed models ask whether TDMs and LLM
judges flip on the same prompts (Appendix G), how the request format
changes the odds of a flip (Appendix K) and how the variance of the
shift divides between test, model and item (Appendix H.2).

## 4 Results and Discussion

This section presents and discusses the findings we consider most
important, and the appendices referred to throughout give the details.
Unless stated otherwise, the numbers for the TDMs refer to the CALM
format, summaries over judges are medians, and summaries over tests are
means.

### 4.1 TDMs Are Biased

Every TDM is biased, and which test is run matters more than which TDM
is tested. Each of the eight TDMs is biased in three (Solar) to eight
(Kev) of the twelve tests (Figure 1). Sentiment and position bias all
eight, sentiment most strongly, while five tests bias at most one. The
rows of Figure 1 thus differ far more than its columns, and in a mixed
model of the probability shift on each prompt, the test explains 25% of
the variance and the model only 1.4% (Appendix H.2). The versions of a
test differ as well, as a claimed majority of 90% sways every TDM more
than one of 60% (Appendix F.5). The ranking of the tests barely changes
for the margins $`\delta=0.02`$ and 0.10 or in the native and the
decomposed format, and sentiment remains a bias in all eight TDMs
throughout (Appendix F.2).

Earlier studies tested some of these cues on TDMs, mostly on Jev and
outside judging, and their results agree with ours. Swapping two answers
flips 3.7 to 11.1% of Jev’s verdicts (Li et al., 2026; Denney and
DiGiuseppe, 2026), close to its excess of 8 here. Open TDMs proved more
sensitive to order (Li, 2026; Gao et al., 2026), and here all TDMs
except Perplexity Decider show a larger position bias than Jev, up to 24
(Tev1). An observer’s opinion appended to the input flips 12.1% of Jev’s
decisions and two unrelated sentences around the question 4.6% (Hu
et al., 2026), much as bandwagon is a bias for Jev here and distraction
for no TDM. Jev does not prefer the longer of two answers, but a more
elaborately written rejected answer lowers its accuracy by 8.8 points
(Li et al., 2026), which fits sentiment being the strongest bias here.

Two findings call for interpretation. First, the position bias persists
although every prompt instructs the judge to ignore the order of the
responses, so it has to be removed by the design of the request, for
example by asking in both orders (Section 4.4). Second, identity notes
change no more verdicts than the neutral note (median excess 3 and 4),
and the six identity groups do not differ (Appendix F.5). What little
diversity changes comes from adding a note, not from the named identity.

For the choice of a TDM, two points follow. Since none is the least
biased in every test, the best model depends on the bias that matters
for the task (Appendix L). Solar has the fewest biases, but not because
it is the most robust. It changes about one in ten verdicts on a
repeated alignment prompt (Table 3), and although the excess corrects
for this, the noise widens its intervals, so that seven of its tests
remain unclear. Its noise thus hides whether it is biased, and a judge
that changes its verdicts by chance is a poor judge in itself.

### 4.2 Bias Compared with LLM Judges

Compared with the LLM judges, the TDMs are more biased than those of
2026 and lie between the two of 2024 (Figure 1), and this order holds
for the margins $`\delta=0.02`$ and 0.10 (Appendix F.2). Sentiment and
authority (on better) affect the TDMs and the 2026 LLM judges alike.
Position, bandwagon and compassion fade are biases in eight, five and
three TDMs but in no 2026 LLM judge, and even Jev, with one of the
smallest position biases among the TDMs (8), exceeds every 2026 LLM
judge (at most 6). Verbosity (on better), in contrast, is a bias in all
five 2026 LLM judges but in only three TDMs. The 2026 LLM judges differ
from those of 2024 mainly in how they treat the presentation of a
response. They hardly react to position (at most 6, against 37 and 21)
and punish padding, which GPT-3.5 Turbo rewards, while their reactions
to sentiment and authority have not become weaker. Despite these
differences, the two kinds of judge flip on the same prompts. In a
logistic mixed model, the prompt effects of the TDMs and the 2026 LLM
judges correlate at 0.95, and the prompt accounts for about 40% of the
variation in flips (Appendix G). This agrees with rubric grading, where
about 96% of LLM verdicts repeat Jev’s wrong answer on its most
confident errors (Rao and Callison-Burch, 2026). The step from an LLM to
a TDM can still add a bias. Under bandwagon, Qwen3.8-27B changes 6 of
100 verdicts beyond chance, and Perplexity Decider and Clef, which are
built on it, 26 and 27 (adjusted $`p<0.001`$).

Position is the best-known bias of LLM judges (Zheng et al., 2023; Wang
et al., 2024), and it has almost disappeared from those of 2026. The
TDMs show it in full. Our design cannot say whether the fine-tuning for
decisions, the typed request, the missing explanation or the read-out of
the verdict brings it back, nor what causes the bandwagon bias above.
Since both kinds flip on the same prompts, whether a verdict flips
depends largely on the prompt, not only on the judge. Under sentiment,
the prompt accounts for 90% of the variation in the flips of the LLM
judges, so some rewrites flip almost every judge and others almost none.
Either these items are hard for any model used as a judge, or their
rewrites change more than the cue, and only human judgments of the same
items could tell the two apart. The robustness rate alone, as CALM
reports it, is not enough. By it, the median TDM would look more robust
than the median 2026 LLM judge in eight of eleven tests, by the excess
in only three, since the TDMs almost never change a verdict by chance
(Table 3, Appendix F.1).

The probabilities show how the TDMs react to a cue. A TDM rewards a cue
when it raises the probability of the response the cue targets (for
position, the one in second place), and punishes it when it lowers it.
Both are biases, but rewarding harms only when the cue sits on the worse
response, and punishing only when it sits on the better one. Figure 2a
shows which cues the TDMs reward and which they punish. These shifts are
read against a noise floor, the shift on a repetition of the prompt, of
only 0.003 for the median TDM (about 0.10 for Solar, Appendix F.3).

Most shifts do not flip the verdict. Under authority, position,
bandwagon and sentiment, the median TDM keeps its verdict while its
probability moves by more than 0.10 on 19 to 24% of the prompts, about
as often as the verdict flips (Figure 2b). Figure 2c shows the flipped
verdicts by how sure the judge was on the original prompt. In all tests
but two, pooled in the figure, 30% of the verdicts given with a
probability below 0.6 flip, against 3% of those given with more than
0.9, and in the logistic mixed model each further 0.1 of confidence
halves the odds of a flip (odds ratio 0.47, 95% CI 0.45 to 0.48,
Appendix H). Sentiment and authority (on better), shown separately, flip
even confident verdicts.

Calibration differs widely between the TDMs and between the two sets
(Table 3). On the alignment set, the expected calibration error (ECE)
ranges from 0.07 (D1) to 0.25 (Solar), whose verdicts are 90% sure on
average but right in only 67% of cases, and better calibration does not
go with less bias (Appendix I).

Under authority, the TDMs punish the citation, the reverse of the trust
in citations that CALM describes. Since our citations are invented, and
some implausible, only real citations could show whether turning away
from them is wrong.

Two consequences follow for practice. First, a TDM’s own probability is
a useful warning sign, since cues mostly tip close calls, which supports
the cascade of Li et al. (2026) that passes uncertain verdicts to a
second judge. It does not warn, however, of a tone or a citation on the
better response, which flip even confident verdicts. Second, hidden
shifts matter wherever the probability is used as a score, for instance
as a reward in training, since a cue that never flips a verdict still
moves the score.

### 4.4 TDMs in Practice

On the original prompts, the TDMs are about as accurate as the 2026 LLM
judges, slightly less so on the alignment set, and the cues lower the
accuracy of both, on the fact-related set somewhat more for the LLM
judges (Acc and $`\widehat{\mathrm{Acc}}`$ in Table 3). Where a punished
cue sits decides whether it harms (Appendix F.4). Under authority, the
accuracy of the median TDM rises from 0.66 to 0.81 when the citation is
on the worse response and falls to 0.41 when it is on the better one.
The rise is no merit, since the verdict then follows the citation rather
than the quality of the responses.

The TDMs are more consistent, and six of them repeat more than 99.7% of
their verdicts when an alignment prompt is sent again (Table 3). The
request format matters little. The mean excess of a TDM differs by at
most 4.1 points between the three formats, although the format alone
changes about 5% of the verdicts (Table 3, Appendix K).

The TDMs are also far cheaper and faster. At the median, a 2026 LLM
judge costs 37 times as much as a TDM and takes six times as long
(Table 3), mostly for the explanation of 90 to 540 tokens that CALM’s
prompt asks for. Asking a TDM in both orders and averaging its two
probabilities removes the position bias by construction, since each
response sits once in each place. It doubles the cost and changed
accuracy by $`-0.2`$ to $`+3.8`$ points (Appendix M).

Li et al. (2026) found a similar trade-off. As a pairwise judge, Jev
comes within three points of GPT-6 wherever a verdict can be read off
the text, but falls behind on maths and code.

CALM places most cues on the worse response, where a punished cue looks
like robustness or even accuracy, so benchmarks should place each cue
where it can do harm, or on both responses. The low price of the TDMs
does not buy freedom from bias, but it makes the checks of Appendix N
cheap.

## 5 Conclusion

We tested whether TDMs, used as judges, show the biases catalogued for
LLM judges, with CALM’s data, prompts and metrics and with seven LLM
judges on the same prompts. They do. Each of the eight TDMs is biased in
three to eight of the twelve tests, and sentiment and position bias all
of them. In bias, the TDMs lie between the two LLM judges of 2024 and
above those of 2026. At the same time, they cost far less than LLM
judges, and six of the eight repeat almost every verdict, which makes
them a practical choice for pairwise judgments, provided their biases
are checked for the task. For readers who consider replacing an LLM
judge by a TDM, Appendix N gives a short guide based on our findings.

## Limitations

All our tests are pairwise comparisons, so judges that score single
answers, which TDMs also support, remain untested. Every item also has a
reference, a correct answer in the fact-related set and a dataset label
in the alignment set. Many uses of a judge have none, for example rating
whether an explanation is helpful to a lay reader, where the judge
stands in for a human’s perception. Such judgments may depend more on
tone and style, and our results may not carry over to them.

We rely on CALM’s items and rewrites and add no human verification
beyond the checks reported by CALM. The rewrites were written by
GPT-4-Turbo and may change more than the intended property. In the
fact-related set the two responses differ in clarity and coherence,
which an emotional rewrite also affects, so only rewrites that hold
clarity fixed could show how much of the sentiment bias is due to tone.
Our citations are fake, as in CALM, and some are implausible, so whether
punishing them is a bias or sound judgment, and whether the judges also
punish real sources, needs a test with real citations. The preference
datasets behind the alignment set were made for training, and some
rejected responses may be as good as the chosen ones. This affects
accuracy and calibration, but not the robustness rate or the shift,
which compare a judge with itself. Since all items are public, the LLMs
and the TDMs built on them may have seen some of them during training.

Three of our LLM judges accept no temperature and run at their
provider’s default, and two require hidden reasoning, which we set to
low effort. Other settings, or a prompt that asks only for the verdict,
might change their biases. The flips we report describe how a judge’s
output changes with its input, not why. The training data of the models
are not public and only some of their sizes are known, so we cannot
attribute differences between models to either. Which option a TDM
returns on a 50/50 answer is not documented, so we infer it from the
answers, and since Jev rounds its probabilities to two decimals, some of
its 50/50 answers may be close calls.

Finally, TDMs are new. The versions we test were released at most a few
weeks before our runs, and new models and versions appear in quick
succession, so our results describe the models of October 2026. The
tests should be repeated on later models, which our released code and
data allow for a few dollars per model.

## Ethical Considerations

The diversity test names six groups that face discrimination, in CALM’s
wording: homosexual people, Black people, women, people living with HIV,
refugees and Muslims. We keep CALM’s labels and template unchanged so
that our numbers stay comparable with CALM’s, although some labels, such
as “Homosexual” and “Muslim believers”, are not how these groups usually
describe themselves. The test gives an AI assistant an identity in a
single added sentence, which is far from how identity shows in real
inputs, for instance through names, dialect or topic. That identity
notes change verdicts no more than a neutral note therefore does not
show that TDMs treat these groups fairly. It shows only that this
explicit and artificial cue has no effect beyond that of any added note.
We make no claims about the groups themselves, and our tests should not
be used to certify a judge as fair.

The five template tests change the judge prompt of Appendix D. The

#### C.2.1 Position

The two responses swap places.

![](arxiv-2610-18122v1--7cd6e1fa3d34.figures/inline-20.svg)

#### C.2.2 Compassion Fade

Model names replace “Assistant A” and “Assistant B” everywhere in the
prompt, including the verdict instruction. CALM does not say which names
it used. For each item we draw, with a fixed seed, a strong current
model for the worse response (GPT-5.6, Claude Opus 5.5, Gemini 3.1 Pro)
and an older, weaker one for the better response (Llama-2-7B, GPT-3.5,
Mistral-7B), so that a judge that trusts reputation is pulled towards
the worse response.

![](arxiv-2610-18122v1--7cd6e1fa3d34.figures/inline-21.svg)

#### C.2.3 Bandwagon

A sentence after the responses claims that 60, 70, 80 or 90% of people
prefer one assistant. CALM does not say which assistant it names. As in
CALM’s case study, we always name the one with the worse response, since
only then can the sentence pull the verdict away from the better
response.

![](arxiv-2610-18122v1--7cd6e1fa3d34.figures/inline-22.svg)

#### C.2.4 Distraction

A sentence after the responses describes what one assistant likes to
eat. As in CALM, it names each assistant in turn.

![](arxiv-2610-18122v1--7cd6e1fa3d34.figures/inline-23.svg)

#### C.2.5 Diversity

A note after the responses names the identity of one assistant, one of
the six groups Homosexual, Black, Female, HIV Positive, Refugees and
Muslim believers. CALM does not say which assistant the note names. We
name each in turn, because a judge could favour or penalise a group, and
only a note on the better response can reveal a penalty. A seventh note,
“Software engineer”, names a group that should not matter and shows the
effect of adding any note at all.

![](arxiv-2610-18122v1--7cd6e1fa3d34.figures/inline-24.svg)

##### Answers of 50/50.

A TDM also returns the option it chooses. When both options have the
same probability, three models always return A, the option shown first
(Table D.2). Counting this choice would give an undecided model a
preference for the first position. We therefore count an answer with
$`|p_{A}-0.5|<10^{-5}`$ as no verdict, with a small tolerance because
the API rounds probabilities, for example to 0.499994. A perturbed
prompt is left out of $`\mathrm{RR}`$ and $`\mathrm{CR}`$ if any of its
three answers is 50/50, but its probabilities still count for the shift.
The rule changes robustness rates by at most 0.03 and one test decision,
the small verbosity effect of Tev1 in the CALM format, which becomes
significant. Since a perturbation can push a verdict to 50/50, leaving
these answers out slightly understates the bias of Tev1 and D1, which
give most of them.

## Appendix E Details on Metrics and Statistics

### E.1 The Excess Never Overstates a Bias

The excess $`E=\mathrm{CR}-\mathrm{RR}`$ (Section 3.3) is meant to
measure how often the cue alone changes a verdict, without the changes
that a judge makes by chance. This section shows that it can understate
this share but never overstate it, and by how much.

##### Two causes of a change.

Compare the verdict on a prompt with the verdict on the first sending of
its original. Two things can change it. The cue changes it with
probability $`q`$, the share we want to measure. Chance changes it with
probability $`r`$, as on a plain repetition, so $`r=1-\mathrm{CR}`$.
With two possible verdicts, a verdict that is changed twice is back
where it started. A perturbed verdict therefore differs from the
original only if exactly one of the two acts:

|     |        |                |                      |
| --- | ------ | -------------- | -------------------- |
| Cue | Chance | Probability    | Verdict              |
| no  | no     | $`(1-q)(1-r)`$ | same                 |
| yes | no     | $`q\,(1-r)`$   | changed              |
| no  | yes    | $`(1-q)\,r`$   | changed              |
| yes | yes    | $`q\,r`$       | same (changed twice) |

Table 8: The four cases for a perturbed prompt if the cue and chance act
independently: does each of them change the verdict?

##### Derivation.

On a perturbed prompt, the verdict changes in the two middle rows of
Table E.1, and on a repetition only by chance,

```math
1-\mathrm{RR}=q\,(1-r)+(1-q)\,r,\qquad 1-\mathrm{CR}=r.
```

The excess is the difference,

```math
\displaystyle E \displaystyle=(1-\mathrm{RR})-(1-\mathrm{CR})=q-2qr
```

```math
\displaystyle=q\,(1-2r)=q\,(2\mathrm{CR}-1).
```

For example, take a judge that changes 10 of 100 verdicts by chance
($`\mathrm{CR}=0.90`$) and a cue that changes 20 of 100. On the
perturbed prompts, chance also hits about 2 of the 20 changed verdicts
and turns them back, and it changes about 8 of the other 80. We see
$`18+8=26`$ changes against 10 on the repetition, so $`E=0.16`$ instead
of $`q=0.20`$, which is $`0.20\times 0.80`$.

##### The bound holds without independence.

Let $`s`$ be the share of prompts in which both the cue and chance
change the verdict. Then $`1-\mathrm{RR}=q+r-2s`$ and $`E=q-2s\leq q`$,
whatever the relation between the two. Independence only fixes $`s=qr`$.
If chance and the cue act on the same prompts more often, for instance
both on close calls (Section 4.3), $`s`$ is larger and $`E`$ is further
below $`q`$. The excess therefore never overstates the effect of the
cue, as long as chance changes verdicts on the perturbed prompts as
often as on a repetition. For a judge that never changes its verdict by
chance ($`\mathrm{CR}=1`$), $`E=q`$.

##### Why we keep $`E=\mathrm{CR}-\mathrm{RR}`$.

Under independence, the effect of the cue could be estimated as
$`q=(\mathrm{CR}-\mathrm{RR})/(2\mathrm{CR}-1)`$. We do not use this as
our measure, for three reasons. It rests on the independence of chance
and cue, which probably does not hold, so it can overcorrect. Dividing
by $`2\mathrm{CR}-1`$ enlarges the noise of the least consistent judges.
And for most of our judges it makes little difference. Six of the eight
TDMs repeat more than 99.7% of their verdicts, so their factor
$`2\mathrm{CR}-1`$ is almost 1. Only for the least consistent judges,
Solar and some LLM judges with $`\mathrm{CR}\approx 0.91`$, is $`E`$
about 18% below $`q`$. $`E`$ is thus a cautious measure, which may miss
a small bias in an inconsistent judge but does not report one that is
not there.

##### Fallacy oversight.

Here a change is the correct reaction, and the bias is the share $`b`$
of broken responses that the judge does not react to. By the same four
cases, a verdict stays the same if the judge does not react and chance
does not act, or if both act, so $`\mathrm{RR}=b\,(1-r)+(1-b)\,r`$. The
excess for fallacy oversight is then

```math
E=\mathrm{RR}-(1-\mathrm{CR})=\mathrm{RR}+\mathrm{CR}-1=b\,(2\mathrm{CR}-1),
```

with the same factor and the same conclusion.

## Appendix G TDMs and LLM Judges

TDMs and 2026 LLM judges are about as similar to each other as the
members of each group. Over the twelve tests, the excess profiles of two
TDMs correlate on average with $`\rho=0.69`$, those of two 2026 LLM
judges with 0.77, and those of a TDM and a 2026 LLM judge with 0.62
(Spearman). On the original prompts, two TDMs agree on 89% of the
verdicts, two 2026 LLM judges on 90%, and a TDM and a 2026 LLM judge on
87%.

The two kinds of judge also flip on the same prompts. For a pair of
judges and a test, we take the prompts on which the verdict of the first
judge flips and ask how often the verdict of the second flips on them,
compared with the prompts on which the first verdict stays (Table 17).
For a TDM and a 2026 LLM judge, the second verdict flips on 27% of the
prompts on which the first flips and on 10% of the others, as for two
TDMs (26 against 10%) and almost as for two 2026 LLM judges (30 against
10%). The overlap is largest under sentiment, where a flip of one judge
goes with a flip of the other in three of four cases, and smallest under
distraction and diversity (10 against 6%). Two TDMs share clearly more
flips with each other than with an LLM judge only under position and
bandwagon (32 against 23% and 26 against 21%), the two tests under which
the TDMs are far more biased than the 2026 LLM judges. Flips of the two
kinds thus cluster on the same prompts about as much as those of two
TDMs, so part of what a test measures lies in its prompts rather than in
the judge.

A logistic mixed model puts this into one number and separates the
prompt from the judge and the test. For judge $`j`$ and prompt $`i`$ of
test $`t(i)`$, fitted with lme4 on the eleven tests other than fallacy
oversight, it is

```math
\displaystyle\operatorname{logit}P(f_{ij}=1)=\alpha_{j\,t(i)}+a_{j}u_{i}+b_{j}v_{i},
```

```math
\displaystyle(u_{i},v_{i})\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\Sigma),\quad\operatorname{corr}(u_{i},v_{i})=r,
```

with one intercept $`\alpha`$ per judge and test, and $`a_{j}=1`$ for a
TDM and $`b_{j}=1`$ for a 2026 LLM judge, 0 otherwise. Thus $`u_{i}`$
and $`v_{i}`$ are the effects of prompt $`i`$ on the TDMs and on the
2026 LLM judges, and the share of the prompt is
$`\sigma_{u}^{2}/(\sigma_{u}^{2}+\pi^{2}/3)`$ on the logit scale. The
prompt accounts for much of whether a verdict flips, 38% of the
remaining variance on the logit scale for the TDMs and 42% for the 2026
LLM judges, and the prompt effects of the two kinds correlate with
$`r=0.95`$. Per test, $`r`$ lies between 0.83 under authority (on
better) and 1 (Table 17), and the share of the prompt between 13%
(distraction, TDMs) and 90% (sentiment, LLM judges). Four of the eleven
per-test fits are singular, with $`r`$ at its upper limit of 1. With an
additional random effect for the item, the item effects of the two kinds
correlate with $`r=0.74`$ and the prompt effects with 1. All mixed
models are fitted with lme4 1.1.37 in R 4.5.2, the logistic ones with
the fast approximation nAGQ = 0. As a benchmark, we split the TDMs and
the 2026 LLM judges into two halves each, five times at random, and fit
the same model for every pair of halves. Two halves of the same kind
correlate with $`r=1.00`$, and a half of each kind with 0.996 on
average. In most of these fits the correlation reaches its upper limit
of 1, which means the data cannot tell the prompt effects of the two
groups apart, so the small differences between these values say nothing
about the kinds. The main correlation of 0.95, however, shows that the
prompts that make TDMs flip are the ones that make the 2026 LLM judges
flip.

CALM found that its LLM judges favour citations. Among our judges only
GPT-3.5 Turbo still does, GPT-4o already punishes them, and the 2026 LLM
judges punish them as the TDMs do. Jev reacts under authority as
strongly as the median 2026 LLM judge (excess 12). Under verbosity (on
better), the accuracy of the 2026 LLM judges falls from 0.97 to 0.85 at
the median and that of the TDMs from 0.99 to 0.94, while that of GPT-3.5
Turbo rises from 0.86 to 0.95. Under position, Clef’s excess is larger
than that of its base LLM, Qwen3.8-27B (14 against 6), but only because
Qwen also changes verdicts by chance. Under the swap, both change about
as many verdicts (13.9 and 14.8%, paired test, adjusted $`p=0.74`$).

A closer architecture also does not make a model behave more like an LLM
judge. Over the twelve tests, we correlate the excess of each TDM with
the median excess of the five 2026 LLM judges (Spearman). Tev1, which
keeps the language-model head of its LLM and answers with one letter, is
the least similar model (0.38), followed by Clef Flash (0.45) and Solar
(0.49). Jev (0.87), Perplexity (0.81) and Clef (0.80) are the most
similar. With eight models and twelve tests, this is only a first
indication.

### H.2 The Logistic Mixed Model

Section 3.3 gives the logistic mixed model of flips, with the margin in
steps of 0.1. We fit it on the CALM format without fallacy oversight. We
set the test and the model ourselves. Every model judges every item, so
the differences between models are not confounded with the items. A
test, however, uses the items of one dataset only, so only tests on the
same dataset are compared on the same items. A comparison across the two
datasets, such as sentiment against the neutral note, also reflects the
difference between the two sets of items. The margin, the correctness,
the length and the source are properties of the items, and for them we
can only report associations. A linear mixed model splits the variance
of the shift of prompt $`i`$ for model $`j`$ in format $`f`$ into
crossed random effects,

```math
\hat{p}_{ijf}-p_{ijf}=\mu+a_{t}+b_{j}+c_{f}+(ab)_{tj}+(ac)_{tf}\\
+(bc)_{jf}+d_{s}+e_{k}+(ae)_{tk}+(be)_{jk}+\varepsilon_{ijf},
```

with the test $`t=t(i)`$, the source $`s=s(i)`$ and the item $`k=k(i)`$,
each term normal with mean zero and a variance of its own, fitted by
REML.

### K.3 What the Formats Show

Following the vendor’s advice does not help on balance. Splitting the
judgment into criteria lowers the odds of a flip at the same confidence,
perhaps because six answers average out part of each answer’s reaction,
but it also makes the TDMs less sure, and the cue acts on every
criterion alike, so splitting does not isolate it. A judge such as Clef
Flash, whose verdict changes on 9% of the prompts when identical text is
moved into fields although it repeats every verdict, is sensitive to
presentation as such. Other studies report the same sensitivity. Two
interfaces of Jev that ask for the same event disagree on a third of
decisions (Chen and Li, 2026), and open TDMs follow how their options
are written more than what they define (Azizi et al., 2026). Attaching
the names yes and no to the opposite definitions changes half or more of
Jev’s answers in decision gates (Rafe and Das, 2026), and swapping the
names flips about a third of Jev’s yes and no answers (Sun et al.,
2026). Jev, whose vendor wrote the guidance, is the least sensitive.

## Appendix L Differences between TDMs

No model is the most robust in every test (Figure 1, Table 9). Jev
resists verbosity, diversity and compassion fade but is biased under
bandwagon ($`\mathrm{RR}`$ 0.78). Under bandwagon, Clef and Perplexity
are swayed most (0.73 and 0.74) and Tev1 and D1 hardly (0.96 and 0.93),
but these two are biased under compassion fade. Perplexity and Jev have
the smallest position bias (excess 8 each). In the mixed model of
Appendix H.2, the odds of a flip are 3.1 times as high for Solar as for
Jev and 2.2 times for Clef, about half for Kev and Tev1, and 0.62 times
for D1. The models also fill the same interface differently. On a 50/50
answer, three models always return the first option (Appendix D.2), and
their calibration differs widely (Appendix I).

Which model to use depends on the bias that matters for the task, as
CALM found for LLM judges. Where the order of answers cannot be
balanced, a model with a small position bias such as Jev is preferable,
in line with reports that Jev is far less sensitive to option order than
the open model Laya (Li, 2026). Where claims of popularity may appear in
the inputs, Tev1 or D1 are preferable. We cannot attribute the
differences to size or training, since only some sizes are known and no
training data are public. Even models built on the same LLM family
differ clearly, such as Tev1 and Perplexity under bandwagon, although
they also differ in size (4B against 27B). How the step from a base LLM
to a TDM shapes these biases is a question for further study
(Section 4.2).

The different handling of ties and the different calibration show that
one interface hides quite different systems. A confidence of 0.8 does
not mean the same for two models, and a threshold tuned on one cannot be
carried over to another.

## Appendix N A Guide to Replacing an LLM Judge with a TDM

This guide summarises what our results mean for anyone who uses an LLM
judge for pairwise comparisons, as in CALM, and considers a TDM instead.
It rests on the eight TDMs and seven LLM judges we tested in October
2026 and on CALM’s fairly simple tests, so it is a starting point rather
than a guarantee.

A TDM is a good choice where cost, speed and consistency matter. At the
median, it costs about 37 times less than a 2026 LLM judge and answers
about six times as fast, and six of the eight TDMs repeat more than
99.7% of their verdicts on the alignment set, against 91 to 98% for the
2026 LLM judges (Section 4.4). A TDM also returns a probability with its
verdict, which our LLM judges, answering in text, do not. On the
fact-related prompts it is about as accurate as a 2026 LLM judge (92 to
100% against 94 to 98%), and on the alignment prompts the best TDMs come
close (75 to 78% against 72 to 80%). Under sentiment and authority (on
better), the two kinds of judge are about equally biased, and under
verbosity (on better) the 2026 LLM judges are more biased (Section 4.2),
so for these cues a switch adds no bias.

An LLM judge remains the safer choice where the inputs carry cues that
the TDMs follow and the 2026 LLM judges do not. A claimed majority
biases five of the eight TDMs and the name of a well-known model three,
but neither biases any 2026 LLM judge. Where inputs contain such cues,
for example votes, ratings or the names of the systems that wrote the
responses, and the cues cannot be removed, a switch adds bias. An LLM
judge is also needed where the verdict must be explained, since a TDM
gives no explanation. Finally, our tests are simple. For judgments that
require checking a derivation, scoring a single answer or reading long
inputs, we have no evidence that a TDM can replace an LLM judge.

For a switch, we recommend the following.

1.  1.  Ask in both orders. Every TDM we tested has a position bias, which
        no 2026 LLM judge has. Judging each pair in both orders and
        averaging the two probabilities removes it. This doubles the cost
        and changed the accuracy of our TDMs by $`-0.2`$ to $`+3.8`$ points
        (Appendix M).
2.  2.  Remove cues the task does not need. Strip claimed majorities,
        popularity counts and the names of models from the input before
        judging.
3.  3.  Do not expect a TDM to handle tone and citations better. Both kinds
        of judge punish an emotional tone and a fake citation even on the
        better response. Under authority (on better), the accuracy of the
        median TDM falls from 0.66 to 0.41, and under a tone on the better
        response from 0.99 to 0.42 (Appendix F.4).
4.  4.  Treat close calls as uncertain. Outside sentiment and authority (on
        better), three in five flipped verdicts come from verdicts given
        with a probability below 0.7, which are about a quarter of all
        verdicts (Figure 2c, Appendix H). Such verdicts can be passed on to
        a second judge, as in the cascade of Li et al. (2026). A high
        probability does not protect against a tone or a citation on the
        better response, which flip about half and 14% of the verdicts given
        with more than 0.9.
5.  5.  Use the probabilities with care. Calibration differs widely between
        the TDMs and does not go with less bias (Appendix I). A cue can also
        move the probability by more than 0.10 without flipping the verdict,
        for the median TDM on 19 to 24% of the prompts under authority,
        position, bandwagon and sentiment. Where the probability serves as a
        score, for example as a reward in training, check its calibration on
        the task’s own data and test the cues that matter there.
6.  6.  Choose the model and the request format for the task. No TDM is the
        least biased in every test, so pick the one whose weak spots matter
        least for the inputs (Appendix L). The request format changes the
        bias of most models little, but moving the same text into another
        format changes 4.5 to 5.5% of the verdicts of the median model, so
        keep one format fixed (Appendix K).
7.  7.  Read the interface. Check what the model returns on a 50/50 answer
        and how it defines its confidence. Three of our models always return
        the first option on a 50/50 answer (Appendix L).
8.  8.  Test again after an update. TDMs are new and change quickly. With
        our released code, a full bias test of one TDM in the CALM or native
        format costs US\$0.26 to 3.11 (Appendix J).

Whoever tests a judge, TDM or LLM, should look beyond the robustness
rate. It mixes bias with chance and favours consistent judges
(Section 4.2), so the consistency rate and the excess should be reported
with it, and for a TDM the shift of its probability.
