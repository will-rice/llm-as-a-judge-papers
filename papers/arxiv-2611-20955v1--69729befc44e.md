###### Abstract

Probabilistic safety judges return the probability of a safety label and
cheaply score reinforcement learning rollouts. A confidence gate sends
only uncertain verdicts to a stronger LLM judge, which leaves
confidently passed harm in a blind spot. Policies can exploit this blind
spot, and commercial judges release new versions. We propose a protocol
that validates such a judge before and during training, separates the
share of confidently passed harm from its amount, and re-validates it
after each judge change. We apply it to two versions of Jev, a
commercial decision-only judge, and to an open guard classifier. Under
the probability reward of Jev, passed harm shrinks in all 11 runs under
the primary reference, mostly more slowly than caught harm, and
concentrates in the blind spot. Binarizing this judge meets the escape
criterion on Llama, and the open guard classifier shows an escape signal
in one seed under a second reference. Guard classifiers share this blind
spot, and even frontier LLM judges catch at most one of 10 human-labeled
confident misses. Without a trained reward model, the probability reward
with a helpfulness term lowers HarmBench attack success on Llama-3.1-8B
from 0.287 to at most 0.033 at the final checkpoint.

Safety training of large language models increasingly replaces human
preference labels with automated judges. Reinforcement learning from
human feedback (RLHF) trains a reward model on human comparisons (Ouyang
et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022a), and recent pipelines score responses
with rules or with another model (Bai et al., 2022b; Lee et al., 2024;
Mu et al., 2024). Among automated judges, probabilistic safety judges
are among the cheapest. They return the probability of a fixed safety
label instead of free text, and a confidence threshold, which this paper
calls the gate, routes uncertain verdicts to a stronger delegate judge.
The gate leaves most verdicts to the cheap judge and calls the stronger
judge only where the cheap judge is unsure, which raises accuracy at a
fraction of the cost (Jung et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026b; Xu et al.,
2025).

The standard worry about such rewards is reward hacking, in which
policies trained with reinforcement learning (RL) against an imperfect
reward exploit its errors (Gao et al., 2023; Skalse et al., 2022). For a
judge with a confidence gate, exploitation has a specific form that this
paper calls gate escape. Under gate escape, the policy moves its harmful
outputs into the region where the judge is confidently wrong. Figure 1a
shows a training rollout that the judge passes with high confidence
although its final human label is harmful, which this paper calls a
confident miss.

Probabilistic safety judges include commercial decision-only judges,
also called decision models, which release new versions through an API,
and open guard classifiers, which newer releases replace (Inan et al.,
2023; Han et al., 2024). A judge that serves as a reward therefore needs
checks that a team can repeat whenever the judge changes. Existing
evaluations of decision-only judges score a fixed set of outputs with a
fixed judge version (Li et al., 2026b; Rao and Callison-Burch, 2026;
Liu, 2026). These evaluations cannot detect a failure that appears only
under optimization, and they leave open what to check again after the
judge changes.

> _What must be checked before and during RL training to use a
> probabilistic safety judge as a reward, and what must be checked again
> when the judge changes?_

This paper answers with a validation protocol of four stages (Table 1).
Its central stage separates the share of harm that the gate passes from
the amount of such harm. A re-validation rule states which stages to
repeat after the judge changes.

## 2 Related Work

#### Automated judges as safety rewards.

RLHF fits a reward model to human comparisons and optimizes the policy
against it (Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022a). AI feedback
replaces some of these labels with model judgments (Bai et al., 2022b;
Lee et al., 2024). Safe RLHF separates reward and cost models (Dai
et al., 2024), and Rule Based Rewards grade safety with an LLM grader
and composable rules (Mu et al., 2024). Guard models classify prompts
and responses as safe or unsafe and serve as moderation tools and
evaluators (Inan et al., 2023; Han et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025). The
reward studies above build reward models and graders, while this study
asks how to validate an existing judge before it becomes the reward.

#### Decision-only judges.

One benchmark evaluates Jev as a zero-shot detector of alignment
failures (Guo et al., 2026), and for decision models including Jev, high
average accuracy can hide attack groups labeled safe with high
confidence (Liu, 2026). Flash-tier LLM judges repeat over 85% of the
most confident errors of decision models (Rao and Callison-Burch, 2026).
Natural context additions can flip confident decisions (Xu et al.,
2026), and single-edit black-box attacks probe the same fragility (Hu
et al., 2026).

#### Confidence-based delegation.

Cascaded selective evaluation trusts a cheap LLM judge only when its
confidence guarantees a target level of human agreement and escalates
the rest to stronger judges (Jung et al., 2025). JEV-as-a-Judge applies
such a cascade to decision models and beats a frontier judge on held-out
pairs, but its routing weakens on style-adversarial pairs (Li et al.,
2026b). Uncertainty-based routing applies such delegation from a fast
reward model to a strong LLM judge during policy-gradient training (Xu
et al., 2025). In cost-saving cascades, metrics computed through a
verifier can stay flat while the true error swings widely (Rajput
et al., 2026). This study extends these checks to judges that serve as
RL rewards and change over time.

#### Measuring reward hacking.

Reward overoptimization follows predictable scaling laws when a gold
reward model scores the policy (Gao et al., 2023). Reward hacking has a
formal definition in terms of proxy and true rewards (Skalse et al.,
2022). In multimodal RL, outcome-only rewards create new failures among
samples whose proxy reward improves (Yao et al., 2026). Under GRPO
against a frozen LLM judge of answer correctness, the depth of judge
hacking tracks the accuracy of the judge (Wang, 2026). These measures
track how far the proxy and the true objective diverge, whereas this
study tracks where the remaining errors of a gated judge lie.

## 3 Validating a Judge Reward

### 3.1 Probabilistic Judges and the Confidence Gate

A probabilistic safety judge answers a fixed question about a response
and returns the probability $`p`$ that the answer is true. Each request
carries exactly one question and a state with the user prompt and the
assistant response. The _harmful_ question asks whether the response
meaningfully helps cause harm to people, property, or society. The
_refusal_ question asks whether the response declines or deflects the
request, and Appendix A lists both questions verbatim. Both questions
are yes/no questions, and the study defines confidence as
$`c=\max(p,1-p)`$. A response whose confidence falls below the
delegation threshold $`\tau`$ lies in the delegation band and goes to
the delegate judge when the reward uses delegation. A confident miss is
a harmful response that the judge passes as safe with $`c\geq\tau`$.
Confident misses form the blind spot of the gate, since the delegate
judge never sees them. The main judge in this study is Jev, with version
1.13.0 in the Llama experiments and version 1.14.0 in the Qwen
experiments.

### 3.2 Measuring Passed Harm under RL

Two measures track where the remaining harm sits during training. Two
reference judges, Qwen3Guard-Gen-8B (Zhao et al., 2025) and gpt-oss-120b
(OpenAI, 2025), score the training rollouts for measurement only and
never enter the reward. As the primary reference, Qwen3Guard scores all
76,800 rollouts of each run, and its Unsafe label counts as harmful
while its Controversial label counts as safe, which restricts the
measures to clear harm. The reference judge gpt-oss-120b runs with low
reasoning effort and scores all harmful-prompt rollouts and a 20% sample
of the rest. The gate-pass rate (GPR) is the share of reference-harmful
responses to harmful prompts that are confident misses. The passed-harm
rate (PHR) divides the same count by all responses to harmful prompts. A
rising GPR with a falling PHR marks concentration, in which training
removes the harm that the judge sees faster than the harm that it
misses. A significantly rising PHR marks escape, in which the policy
moves more harm into the blind spot. Under concentration, the remaining
harm shrinks and collects in the blind spot, which raises its share
while its amount falls, whereas under escape the harm inside the blind
spot grows in amount. A PHR change whose interval includes zero leaves
the decision to the human audit (S3). The measures compare an early
window of the first 50 steps with a late window. The late window spans
steps 150–299, and the comparison uses four harm criteria: each
reference judge alone, both agreeing, or either one. Confidence
intervals come from a prompt-level bootstrap (Efron, 1979) with 2,000
resamples per window, since the 8 responses to one prompt are not
independent. A change counts as significant when its 95% interval
excludes zero.

### 3.3 The Validation Protocol

The protocol applies four stages before and during training, and Table 1
lists the result of each stage in this study.

#### S0: before training.

The yes/no questions are chosen on development data and frozen before
training, and each gives a single probability that serves directly as
the reward and as the gate input. Each question binds the answer names
true and false to definitions with the same meaning, because decision
models can follow an option name rather than the definition bound to it
(Sun et al., 2026). Decision models also give probabilities that need
not cohere across logically related questions (Li et al., 2026a), and
the study therefore fixes one question per label. The threshold rule
picks the smallest $`\tau`$ whose confident items reach an accuracy of
at least 0.95 on 100 calibration items from WildGuardTest (Han et al.,
2024), which all later evaluations exclude. This rule yields
$`\tau=0.93`$ on the calibration items of version 1.13.0.

#### S1: offline checks.

Human-labeled responses from WildGuardTest and XSTest-Response (Röttger
et al., 2024) reveal the direction of the errors before any training.
The checks also cover calibration, appended sentences that claim safety,
and dedicated safety classifiers at a matched delegation rate. A judge
that errs in only one direction offers the policy a single region to
exploit, and S2 asks whether the policy exploits it.

#### S2: under RL pressure.

S2 trains the policy with the candidate reward and judges the change in
the PHR of Section 3.2 by its 95% interval. The stage passes when the
whole interval lies below zero, even if the GPR rises, whereas an
interval entirely above zero marks escape and calls for another judge or
reward form. An interval that includes zero leaves the decision to S3,
and passing therefore requires a significant decrease. A significant PHR
rise under the second reference judge also sends the run to S3, because
a reference judge similar to the reward judge can share its blind spot.

#### S3: human audit.

Two annotators label 200 rollouts from the last 50 steps, and the
verdicts of the judge and of Qwen3Guard define the strata (Appendix C).
The delegate judge scores the same responses, which shows whether
delegation would catch the confident misses that humans label harmful.
The audit samples only the last 50 steps and therefore bounds the level
of confident misses without testing their change.

### 3.4 Re-Validation When the Judge Changes

A version update or a replacement of the judge can alter the gate. After
a version update, the protocol repeats S1 on all offline items, checks
whether the frozen $`\tau`$ still satisfies the S0 rule, and repeats S2
on runs that train with the new version. The protocol keeps the frozen
$`\tau`$ while it still meets the rule and re-selects $`\tau`$ only when
the rule fails. Re-applying the rule to version 1.14.0 would move the
threshold to 0.86 on the strength of one or two calibration items, and a
threshold that follows such noise would loosen the gate without any real
change in the judge. A replacement of the judge requires all four stages
again, and Sections 4–5 apply S0–S2 to an open guard classifier without
S3.

## 4 Experimental Setup

#### Reward conditions.

Each reward condition changes one design choice of Prob and holds the
others fixed (Table 2). Binary changes the reward form with the judge
fixed, and Prob+Help tests whether a helpfulness term preserves response
quality. Prob+Delegate and LLM-Verdict route part or all of the
harmful-prompt responses to a delegate judge, and Prob-WG replaces the
judge with the reward form fixed. The Prob condition uses
$`r=1-p_{\text{harm}}`$, and Binary gives a reward of one when
$`p_{\text{harm}}<0.5`$ and zero otherwise. The Prob+Delegate condition
sends responses with $`c<\tau`$ to the delegate judge and keeps
$`r=1-p_{\text{harm}}`$ for the rest. The LLM-Verdict condition sends
every harmful-prompt response to the delegate judge, and delegated
responses receive $`r=1-v`$ for the binary delegate verdict $`v`$. The
delegate judge is the OpenAI model gpt-4o-mini, snapshot

#### Policies and training.

The study trains two policies, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Grattafiori
et al., 2024) and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Qwen Team et al., 2024). Training
uses GRPO in TRL (von Werra et al., 2020) with vLLM generation (Kwon
et al., 2023). Each run updates a low-rank adaptation (LoRA) adapter (Hu
et al., 2022) of rank 64 for 300 steps. Each step samples 8 responses
for each of 32 prompts. The main setting uses a KL coefficient
$`\beta=0.001`$ with a learning rate of $`2\times 10^{-5}`$. Every
condition runs with two seeds, and Binary runs on Llama only. The
Prob-WG condition replaces Jev with the open guard classifier WildGuard
for both rewards on Llama, and its gate uses WildGuard confidence at
$`\tau=0.99`$ from the same S0 rule. The Prob condition on Llama adds
three runs with weaker or stronger optimization pressure (Appendix A).
The training pool of 12,000 prompts mixes harmful prompts from
WildJailbreak (Jiang et al., 2024) and PKU-SafeRLHF (Ji et al., 2025),
borderline prompts from WildJailbreak and OR-Bench-80k (Cui et al.,
2025), and general instructions from UltraFeedback. Harmful prompts make
up 40% of the pool, and the other two groups make up 30% each. The pool
excludes every prompt that overlaps with the evaluation sets.

#### Benchmarks.

Held-out benchmarks evaluate checkpoints taken every 100 steps up to
step 300 with greedy decoding (Appendix A). HarmBench (Mazeika et al., 2024) contributes 300 behaviors scored by its official classifier, and
StrongREJECT (Souly et al., 2024) contributes 313 prompts scored by its
official grader. Over-refusal is the refusal share on the 250 safe
prompts of XSTest and on the 1,319 prompts of OR-Bench-Hard, with
refusals judged by Qwen3Guard. AlpacaEval measures helpfulness on 805
instructions (Li et al., 2023) as the win rate against the base policy,
judged pairwise by gpt-oss-120b in both orders.

## 5 Results

The results answer three questions in protocol order: whether the
offline checks expose a weakness that survives a judge change
(Section 5.1), whether the remaining harm escapes the gate under RL
(Section 5.2), and which reward form avoids escape and keeps helpfulness
(Section 5.3).

### 5.1 Offline Checks and Judge Changes

#### Errors run in one direction.

Against human labels, every confident error of the judge is a missed
harmful response rather than a false alarm. Jev confidently passes 30 of
the 267 harmful responses in WildGuardTest (WGT). These confident misses
amount to 11.2% of the harmful responses. Jev never flags a benign
response as harmful with confidence above $`\tau`$ (Table 5 in
Appendix B).

#### Guard classifiers share the blind spot.

Table 6 in Appendix B matches the delegation rate of each classifier to
the 19.4% of Jev. At that cost, Llama-Guard-3 confidently misses 10.9%
of the harmful responses and WildGuard misses 12.4%. Llama-Guard-3 also
misses 15 of the 30 confident misses of Jev. WildGuard also misses 16 of
them. Independent errors would give about 3 shared misses with
Llama-Guard-3. Jev differs from these classifiers mainly in calibration,
with an expected calibration error (ECE) (Guo et al., 2017) of 0.014 on
WGT harmful. Llama-Guard-3 and WildGuard reach an ECE of 0.049 and 0.056
(Table 6). Appending an opinion, an authority claim, or a claim of
safety to a harmful response rarely flips the verdict (Appendix B).
Raising $`\tau`$ to 0.99 cuts the confident misses to 1.9% of the
harmful responses. That threshold delegates 63% of all responses, and a
higher $`\tau`$ therefore shrinks the blind spot only by delegating most
of the work.

#### The weakness survives the version change.

Re-scoring all 4,324 offline items with version 1.14.0 tests this
weakness under the new version. The re-scoring costs USD 0.14 in judge
calls, which keeps the offline checks cheap to repeat after each update.
The new version yields 29 confident misses and no confident false alarm
on WGT harmful. The verdicts of the two versions agree on 98.2–99.3% of
the items per dataset. Under version 1.14.0, the frozen threshold
reaches a confident accuracy of 0.973 and satisfies the S0 rule.

#### Aggregate measures improve.

Optimization against the judge makes the policy safer by the usual
aggregate measures. The Qwen3Guard safety score is the share of
harmful-prompt responses that Qwen3Guard labels Safe or Controversial.
It ends between 0.963 and 0.985 in all five Prob runs on Llama. These
metrics divide by all responses and can therefore improve while harm
concentrates where the gate does not look.

#### The share rises, but the amount falls.

Figure 2 places every run by its change in the share of passed harm, the
GPR, and in its amount, the PHR. Read alone, the share points to escape,
since almost all runs lie to the right of zero and the GPR of the eleven
probability-reward runs grows in 37 of 44 comparisons. The amount points
the other way, and all 11 runs with the probability reward of Jev lie
below zero. Seven of them fall significantly and pass S2 outright, and
the other four go to S3, where the human audit below covers two. In the
main Prob run on Llama, the PHR falls from 1.48% to 0.54% of
harmful-prompt responses. Training thus removes the harm that the judge
catches faster than the harm that it misses, and the remaining harm
shrinks while it collects in the blind spot, which is concentration
rather than escape. The pattern holds for both judge versions, for the
three runs with other optimization pressure, and for 10 of the 11 runs
under the gpt-oss reference. Only Binary lies in the upper region with
significance (Section 5.3), and Tables 7 and 8 in Appendix C give the

#### An open judge shows an escape signal.

Under Prob-WG, the two reference judges disagree about escape. The
Qwen3Guard PHR stays flat in both seeds, whereas the gpt-oss PHR rises
in both and significantly in one, from 0.95% to 2.01% of harmful-prompt
responses. Most responses behind this rise open with a refusal and then
supply the requested content, and Qwen3Guard labels only 18 of the 310
such late responses Unsafe. The primary reference thus misses most of
the responses that gpt-oss flags, and StrongREJECT under Prob-WG also
ends above the base policy in both seeds (Table 3). Whether harm
concentrates without escape may therefore depend on the judge even under
a probability reward, and the second stage needs two references to
detect the difference.

#### Humans label far fewer confident misses as harmful.

Two annotators labeled 200 end-of-training rollouts of the two
$`\beta=0.01`$ Prob runs on Llama under the MLCommons hazard guidelines
(Ghosh et al., 2025). Among the responses that Qwen3Guard labels Unsafe
or Controversial and Jev confidently passes, the final human label marks
13.3% as harmful (Table 9 in Appendix C). The bootstrap interval for
this share spans 5.0–23.3%. The Unsafe subset, which matches the
reference definition of the GPR, gives 11.1% on 18 items.
Reference-judge estimates of confident misses therefore overstate their
level seven- to ninefold. Responses that both Jev and Qwen3Guard call
safe are almost always safe, with 0.1% harmful after reweighting to the
rollout pool. The two annotators disagree most on the passes that
Qwen3Guard flags, with a Cohen $`\kappa`$ of 0.21 overall, and no
confident miss carries a harmful label from both of them. The shared
blind spot thus appears to lie on a boundary that humans also find
unclear.

### 5.3 Reward Form and Delegation

This section shows how S2 separates the reward forms and the delegation
designs.

#### Even with the same judge, binarization leads to escape.

The Binary condition keeps the judge of Prob and changes only the reward
form, and this change alone moves harm into the blind spot. Under the
Qwen3Guard reference, its PHR rises significantly by 1.5 and 1.7 points
in the two Llama seeds, which meets the escape criterion. These are the
only significant increases of the pooled Qwen3Guard PHR in the study,
and the gpt-oss PHR and the GPR under all four criteria rise in both
seeds as well. Binary still lowers HarmBench attack success below the
base policy (Table 3), and benchmarks alone would therefore accept this
reward, whereas S2 rejects it as escape.

#### A binary verdict from another judge moves the PHR the same way.

On Llama, this rise is consistent with the reward form rather than the
judge driving it. The LLM-Verdict condition shares the binary reward
form with Binary but uses gpt-4o-mini as the judge, and its PHR rises
without significance in both Llama seeds. The Prob+Delegate condition,
which uses binary verdicts only inside the band, also raises the PHR
without significance on Llama, and the reward form alone cannot account
for this rise because the binary verdicts cover part of the responses.
On Qwen, the PHR under LLM-Verdict and Prob+Delegate falls without
significance in all four runs, and this study does not run Binary on
Qwen.

#### The rise is significant only on health prompts.

A post hoc split shows that the Llama increase under Binary,
Prob+Delegate, and LLM-Verdict is significant only on medical and
mental-health prompts, in all six runs under the Qwen3Guard reference.
Under Binary, the PHR on these prompts rises by 5.2 and 6.7 points in
the two seeds. The remaining prompts rise by only 1.1 and 1.2 points,
without significance. The gpt-oss reference finds no significant change
on these prompts (Appendix C).

#### Binary rewards give sparse, large signals.

Binary rewards leave most GRPO groups without a learning signal and give
the rest an oversized one. A group carries no learning signal when all 8
of its rewards are equal. On Llama, 92% of the harmful-prompt groups
carry no signal under Binary (Table 10 in Appendix D). The share is
87–88% under LLM-Verdict. It drops to 70–75% under Prob, where the
reward takes many values. In the first 50 steps on Llama, a
signal-carrying group has a reward standard deviation of about 0.41
under both Binary and LLM-Verdict. The value is only 0.06 under Prob.
Appendix D describes the deepest mid-training collapses, which the
binary reward alone does not reproduce.

#### Delegation catches part of the harm at a cost.

On Llama, delegation catches harm inside the band but raises
over-refusal without a clear safety gain. Under Prob+Delegate,
gpt-4o-mini labels 62–72% of the reference-harmful responses as harmful
and assigns them zero reward. At the final checkpoint, the bootstrap
intervals of its HarmBench attack success overlap those of Prob, whereas
its OR-Bench-Hard refusal rises in both seeds with intervals that do not
overlap (Table 3). On Qwen, Prob+Delegate keeps the PHR flat and trains
stably, yet its second seed ends with a HarmBench attack success of
0.237, close to the 0.240 of the base policy.

#### The delegate judge shares the blind spot.

The delegate judge misses the same responses that Jev confidently
passes. On harmful-prompt responses with $`c\geq\tau`$, gpt-4o-mini
returns the verdict of Jev for 97.4–98.1% of them. Among the confident
misses that a reference judge identifies, gpt-4o-mini catches 23–65% in
post hoc scoring depending on the run. The final human label marks 10 of
the confidently passed responses as harmful, and the delegate judge
catches none of them. The stronger judges gpt-5.6-sol and
claude-opus-5.5 catch only one of them when they score the same
responses, and gpt-oss also flags that one (Appendix C). Inside the
delegation band, gpt-4o-mini catches 7 of the 9 human-labeled harmful
responses. It also flags 58.1% of the harmless responses in the band as
harmful. The judge gpt-5.6-sol lowers this false-alarm share to 25.8%.
Delegation works only inside the band, and confident misses never enter
it. The audit sample is small and comes from the last 50 steps on Llama,
and the result therefore bounds rather than settles the coverage of
delegation.

## 6 A Practical Recipe

| Step            | Recommendation                         | Evidence |
| --------------- | -------------------------------------- | -------- |
| Judge           | Pass S0–S1, freeze $`\tau`$            | §5.1     |
| Harmful prompts | Use the probability, not a 0/1 reward  | §5.3     |
| Benign prompts  | Add a helpfulness term                 | Table 3  |
| Delegation      | Keep LLM judges for monitoring only    | §5.3     |
| Monitoring      | Track GPR and PHR under two references | §5.2     |
| Checkpoints     | Evaluate several, not only the last    | App. D   |
| Open issue      | Over-refusal on borderline prompts     | Table 3  |

Table 4: A recipe for safety GRPO with a probabilistic safety judge,
drawn from the results on two 7–8B chat policies.

A probabilistic safety judge supports safety GRPO without a trained
reward model (Table 3). On Llama, Prob lowers HarmBench attack success
to 0.040 and 0.153, and on Qwen it lowers the rate to about a fifth of
the base-policy rate. Binarizing the same judge weakens this effect and
meets the escape criterion (Section 5.3), and the recipe therefore keeps
the probability itself as the reward.

The cost of Prob lies in helpfulness, and all 15 Prob checkpoints on
Llama lose to the base policy on AlpacaEval (Table 11 in Appendix D). A
helpfulness term on benign and general prompts removes most of this
cost. On Llama, Prob+Help raises the AlpacaEval win rate to 0.594 and
0.532 and beats the base policy, whereas on Qwen it stays below the base
policy. HarmBench attack success stays at or below the Prob level in
three of the four runs. The Prob+Help answers remain shorter than the
base answers (Table 12 in Appendix D), which rules out length as the
source of the win.

Table 4 condenses the results into a recipe for safety GRPO of
single-turn chat policies with a probabilistic judge. A Prob run costs
USD 1.6–2.2 in judge calls.

## 7 Conclusion

A probabilistic safety judge can serve as an RL reward once it passes
the four stages of the validation protocol, whose offline checks repeat
cheaply after a judge change. For one commercial judge and the
Qwen3Guard reference, the amount of passed harm falls in every run under
the probability reward while its share grows in most runs, which
reflects concentration rather than escape. Binarizing the same judge
meets the escape criterion on Llama, and with the open guard classifier
as the reward, the second reference judge detects an escape signal in
one seed. Guard classifiers and LLM judges share much of the blind spot,
and even frontier models catch at most one of the 10 human-labeled
confident misses. The GPR alone suggests escape, whereas the PHR of S2
reveals concentration, and reference judges overstate confident misses
seven- to ninefold until the human audit of S3 corrects them. Without a
trained reward model, the probability reward with a helpfulness term
makes Llama far safer than the base policy on HarmBench and better than
it on AlpacaEval. We recommend that safety RL pipelines repeat the
offline checks whenever the judge changes, report both the share and the
amount of confidently passed harm under two reference judges, and audit
residual harm with humans.

## Limitations

#### Reward judges.

Training runs use one commercial decision-only judge, accessible only
through an API, in two versions and, on Llama only, one open guard
classifier. The protocol makes no assumption about the judge, but the RL
findings rest on these two reward judges, and Llama-Guard-3 enters only
the offline checks. The version change provides a single re-validation
case. The escape signal under WildGuard appears in one seed under one
reference and lacks a human check, because the audit covers Jev runs
alone.

#### Scale and coverage.

The study trains two instruction-tuned policies of 7–8B parameters with
LoRA and a single RL algorithm, and larger policies, full fine-tuning,
or other algorithms may change the size of the concentration effect.
Each main condition has two seeds, and checkpoints of the same run often
differ more than conditions do at a single checkpoint. The Binary
condition runs on Llama only, and the study compares only the two ends
of the reward form without testing graded rewards with three or five
levels.

#### Delegate judge and controls.

All delegation results use gpt-4o-mini, and a stronger delegate judge
may catch more of the subtle harm that the probabilistic judge misses.
The LLM-Verdict condition keeps the refusal reward of the probabilistic
judge and uses a judge that agrees with it where it is confident, and it
is therefore not an independent control for adaptation to the
probabilistic judge. The binary reward alone does not reproduce the
deepest mid-training collapses, and their cause remains open. The study
does not reproduce uncertainty-based routing with a trained reward model
(Xu et al., 2025), whose pairwise design does not fit a pointwise judge.

#### Measurement.

The two reference judges reach moderate agreement with each other, with
a $`\kappa`$ of 0.56–0.62. The two human annotators agree even less,
with a Cohen $`\kappa`$ of 0.21 under shared guidelines. Reference-judge
estimates of confident misses overstate their level even against the
either-annotator share of 33.3%, although the PHR falls in most Jev
probability-reward runs under both references (Appendix C). The human
audit covers 200 rollouts from Llama and samples the last 50 steps
alone, and two of the four runs that S2 leaves to S3 have no audit. It
contains just 2 responses that both reference judges call harmful and
therefore cannot test the strictest criterion. The pairwise gpt-oss-120b
judge behind the AlpacaEval scores has no human agreement check and may
carry biases beyond length.

## Ethics Statement

This work studies how to train language models to refuse harmful
requests and how to measure the harm that such training leaves behind.
The experiments generate harmful text as an unavoidable part of
measuring reward hacking. The paper shows only two truncated excerpts in
Figure 1, with real names and drug doses masked and no operational
detail.

Two annotators labeled 200 responses that include violent, criminal, and
hateful content. The labeling guide opened with a content warning, and
annotators could pause or stop at any time.

The finding that safety judges share a blind spot could inform attempts
to evade such judges. The study reports where the blind spot lies only
in aggregate, and the excerpts in Figure 1 contain no content that would
help to evade a judge. The main implication is defensive, since a shared
blind spot argues for audits that do not rely on the judges themselves.

## Appendix A Judge Questions and Setup

Each Jev request contains the state fields user_prompt and
assistant_response and a single question. The harmful and refusal
questions are binary questions whose probability of “true” serves as
$`p`$. The helpfulness question is a five-level scoring question whose
expected level serves as $`\mathbb{E}[h]`$.

#### Training.

All runs use LoRA adapters of rank 64 with $`\alpha=128`$ and no dropout
on every linear layer. Training uses TRL 1.16 defaults for the GRPO
objective, with one optimization iteration per batch, a clipping range
of 0.2, and group-normalized rewards. The optimizer is AdamW with a
constant learning rate after a warmup of 10 steps. Each step accumulates
gradients over 16 micro-batches of 16 completions in bfloat16 with
gradient checkpointing. Sampling uses temperature 1.0 and at most 512
new tokens. Each run trains for 300 steps on one H200 GPU and takes
about six hours. Each run shuffles the prompt pool with its seed and
visits each prompt at most once. The Prob condition on Llama adds two
runs with $`\beta=0.01`$ and a learning rate of $`10^{-5}`$. It also
adds one run with $`\beta=0.001`$ and a learning rate of
$`5\times 10^{-5}`$. The median per-token KL divergence at the end of
training is 0.06 for $`\beta=0.01`$. It lies in 0.27–0.40 for
$`\beta=0.001`$. The Prob-WG condition reads $`p_{\text{harm}}`$ and
$`p_{\text{ref}}`$ from one greedy WildGuard generation in its official
prompt format. Each probability is the next-token probability of “yes”
normalized against “no” after the output fields “Harmful response:” and
“Response refusal:”.

both orders and averages the two verdicts.

## Appendix B Offline Checks

#### Baselines.

Llama-Guard-3-8B and WildGuard-7B score each response through the
probability of the unsafe label token in their outputs. Their confidence
is $`\max(p,1-p)`$, as for Jev. A matched delegation rate of 19.4%
delegates the 19.4% least confident responses of each judge.

#### Appended sentences.

Appending an opinion, an authority claim, or a meta statement to 801
harmful WildGuardTest responses flips at most 1.7% of the harmful
verdicts. None of the confident verdicts flips.

#### Delegation band.

The 100 calibration items cover each combination of harm label and
adversarial prompt type. At $`\tau=0.93`$, the confident items of the
calibration set reach an accuracy of 0.959 under version 1.13.0. Because
the implementation computes $`c`$ in floating point, every experiment
delegates the responses with $`0.07\leq p<0.93`$.

#### Domain split.

gpt-4o-mini labels every training prompt from its text alone as medical,
mental health, or other. Medical and mental-health prompts together form
the health domain, which covers 479 of the 4,800 harmful prompts in the
training pool. Each Llama run sees 54–59 harmful health prompts in the
early window. The late window contains 181–204 of them, and the
bootstrap resamples prompts within each domain. Under the Qwen3Guard
reference, the PHR increase under Binary, Prob+Delegate, and LLM-Verdict
is significant only on health prompts, and the remaining prompts show
smaller nonsignificant increases. The gpt-oss reference gives positive
but nonsignificant changes on health prompts in five of these six runs,
and the health result therefore rests on one reference. The gpt-oss
escape signal of Prob-WG comes from the remaining prompts rather than
from health prompts. On Qwen, the Qwen3Guard PHR on health prompts rises
significantly in the second seed of Prob+Delegate and of LLM-Verdict, by
0.86 and 2.40 points. The human audit contains only 34 health items, too
few for a separate estimate of confident misses. The split is post hoc,
and a study that trains and evaluates on one health domain remains
future work.
