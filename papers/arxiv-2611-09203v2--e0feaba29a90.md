# Copies Are Not Confirmations: How News Duplication Fools LLM Judges and Continual Learners

Abdelkader Metales Affiliation: caaise GmbH
Email: [metales@caaise.com](mailto:)    Miriam Grabenheinrich

###### Abstract

A language model that keeps learning from the news sees the same claim
many times, but many reports are copies of one source: wire stories,
content shared within a media group, syndication and press releases. We
ask whether LLM judges and continual learners treat such copies as
independent confirmations. In 200,000 English articles from CC-News, a
near-duplicate detector identifies copy links to another outlet for
18.9% of the articles, a detector-based lower bound. In a controlled
experiment with claims about invented people, three identical reprints
instead of one report raised the lies accepted by Llama 3.2 from 0.06 to
0.71 and by phi4 from 0.21 to 0.90; Gemini 2.5 Flash already believed a
single report, and Gemini 2.5 Pro followed provenance information most
consistently. In a simulated continual-learning stream, showing the
judge one report per detected copy chain cut the copied lies accepted by
0.10-0.24 across four judges, at a mean cost of at most 4.2 points of
accepted truths. Text similarity links none of the copies reworded by
three LLMs. An entity-novelty test, the share of a report’s named
entities absent from an earlier look-alike, separates these rewrites
from 100 author-labelled independent reports of the same event (AUC
0.98-0.99), statistically indistinguishable from TF-IDF and the best
tested embedding model, but recognises only 17% of natural same-source
derivatives. With both detectors run on 15,084 real articles carrying
inserted claims, one report per chain cut the lies accepted by phi4 from
0.57-0.67 to 0.09 (copies), 0.14 (rewrites) and 0.34 (rewrites with
invented details), and by Llama 3.2 from 0.56-0.62 to 0.07, 0.09 and
0.25, at a cost of 2.4 and 10 points of accepted truths. Counting
reports or chains without a judge is unreliable, and colluding sources
that do not copy each other remain an open problem.

## 1 Introduction

Models that update themselves from incoming text must decide which
claims to believe. In our earlier work, a continual learner was safe
only to the extent that a judge (a verifier that approves each claim)
was accurate, and a learner that selected claims by surprise absorbed
nearly every lie (Metales and Grabenheinrich, 2026). News adds a further
problem. The same claim reaches a reader many times, but often through
copies: a wire story reprinted by dozens of papers, articles shared
across the titles of one owner, syndicated content and press releases.
Journalists treat such reprints as one source, not many. A learner or
judge that treats every publication as independent evidence may
therefore be swayed by claims republished from a single source.

Repetition is known to shift what language models believe: repeated
counterfactual claims in continued pre-training flip a large share of
beliefs (Churina et al., 2025), and repeated low-credibility information
in the prompt reverses models’ source preferences (Schuster et al.,
2026). Neither line of work separates copies from independent sources,
and neither tests a defence inside a continual learner.

Terms. A _report_ is one article stating a claim. A _copy_ repeats an
earlier report: a verbatim _reprint_, an _edited copy_ (credit or lines
removed) or a _rewrite_ (same content in new wording, here produced by
an LLM). A _chain_ is a group of reports our detector links as copies;
it is a duplicate cluster, not a verified provenance lineage.
_Independent reports_ gather their information separately; _collusion_
means distinct outlets that coordinate on the same false claim without
copying one another: they look independent but are not. We keep four
properties apart: textual similarity, which our detectors measure;
information dependence, whether one report derives from another, which
they estimate; source independence, separate information gathering; and
corroboration, whether the claim is true. This paper establishes neither
of the last two.

We ask four questions:

- •
  RQ1 How common are copies in real news, and what kinds of dependence
  do they reflect? (Section 3)
- •
  RQ2 Do LLM judges treat copies as independent confirmations, and does
  telling them otherwise help? (Section 4)
- •
  RQ3 In a continual learner fed by a stream of reports, do rules that
  count reports or independent chains keep lies out? (Section 5)
- •
  RQ4 Does removing copies before the judge sees the evidence reduce the
  lies learned, and against which attacks? (Sections 5 and 7)

The paper studies whether repeated publications are treated as
independent evidence by LLM judges and learning pipelines, and whether
filtering by dependence changes that behaviour. Our contributions fall
into four groups. (1) _Measurement_: cross-outlet copying in 200,000
news articles, with a dependence taxonomy drawn from newsroom practice
and a measurement of the copy detector itself (precision in a labelled
sample, recall on edited and LLM-reworded copies). (2) _Judges_: a
controlled experiment with four LLM judges on their sensitivity to
repeated reports, outlet diversity and supplied provenance information.
(3) _Continual learning_: a simulated stream with three attacks in which
showing the judge one report per detected chain reduces the lies
accepted from copy networks, validated on 15,084 real articles carrying
inserted claims with the real detectors and two local judges. (4)
_Content-based dependence signals_: an entity-novelty test that
recognises most copies reworded by three LLMs, compared with text and
embedding similarity against other reports of the same event and an
adaptive rewriter; it recognises few natural same-source derivatives.
Collusion remains unsolved. The source corpus is public; code, prompts,
annotations and results are available at
[github.com/Metalesaek/copies-experiment](https://github.com/Metalesaek/copies-experiment);
article texts are not redistributed and are rebuilt from CC-News by the
scripts.

## 2 Related work

Repetition and belief in LLMs. Churina et al. (2025) continually
pre-train Qwen 2.5 models on corpora with increasing shares of
counterfactual claims and find that belief flips rise sharply with the
poison ratio; they do not compare copies with independent sources and
test no defence. Schuster et al. (2026) show that, in context, models
prefer institutional sources and that repeating information from a
low-credibility source can reverse that preference; their mitigation
changes the model’s weights. We study the same weakness at the point
where a learner decides what to learn, and test a defence that changes
the evidence, not the model.

Continual learning from claims. Our earlier paper (Metales and
Grabenheinrich, 2026) gates each learning update with surprise, a judge,
a regression test and the model’s prior, and finds that the learner
inherits its judge’s errors; it proposes corroboration across
independent chains as future work. This paper tests that proposal.

News reuse and source criticism. Wire copy, syndication and
press-release journalism (“churnalism”) are well documented in
journalism studies; newsroom verification norms such as the two-source
rule count independent sources, not publications. WCEP (Gholipour
Ghalandari et al., 2020) collects many articles per news event for
summarisation. Schlichtkrull (2024) gives models background on outlets
for source-critical reasoning. We use the independence of reports,
rather than the reputation of outlets, as the signal.

Text reuse in journalism. The METER corpus measured how British
newspapers reuse Press Association copy, from verbatim reprints to heavy
rewriting (Clough et al., 2002). In a study of UK national newspapers,
most press articles relied wholly or mainly on agency copy or
public-relations material (Lewis et al., 2008), a practice Davies (2008)
called churnalism. Our detector uses standard near-duplicate detection
with MinHash (Broder, 1997). Work on knowledge conflicts in LLMs (Xu et
al., 2024) studies how models resolve disagreement between context and
memory; we study how they count agreeing context.

How the task differs from deduplication. News deduplication and event
clustering group articles that are similar or cover the same event. We
ask a different question of two reports of one event: does the later one
add information, or does it repeat what the earlier one already
contained? The answer matters when reports serve as evidence for an LLM
judge or as training input for a continual learner, where grouping by
event would merge independent confirmations with copies.

Similarity, deduplication and verification. Sentence embeddings (Reimers
and Gurevych, 2019) are the obvious alternative to our
entity-and-quotation score for linking rewrites; Section 6 compares
against them, including current embedding models and a reranker, and
finds that they rate independent reports of the same event nearly as
similar to the original as rewrites. Deduplicating pre-training data
(Lee et al., 2022) removes near-identical text to reduce memorisation;
we use duplication instead as evidence about how many sources support a
claim. Claim-verification benchmarks such as FEVER (Thorne et al., 2018)
separate retrieving evidence from judging a claim; our judges are given
the evidence, and we study how its dependence structure is counted.

## 3 How news is copied

Our detector links about one in five sampled news articles to a
near-copy from another outlet, and most copies reflect wire services,
shared ownership or syndication rather than independent reporting.

Data and detector. We drew a uniform random sample of 200,000 English
articles longer than 300 characters, from 6,877 outlets, in CC-News
(2017-2019, with outlet domain, date and URL). Each article’s first
2,000 characters were reduced to 5-word shingles and hashed with MinHash
(64 permutations); locality-sensitive hashing found pairs from different
outlets with estimated Jaccard similarity of at least 0.6. Pairs were
joined into chains (connected groups of near-copies).

How much is copied. The detector links 37,705 articles (18.9%) to a
near-copy from another outlet; this estimates near-duplicate text across
outlets, not every form of journalistic dependence. The articles fall in
12,243 chains (connected groups of LSH candidate pairs; these chains
supply the chain sizes of the simulation in Section 5). This is a lower
bound: the sample holds about 28% of the corpus, so many copies’
partners are missing. Which part of the article is compared matters
little (boilerplate removed): 18.5% of articles are linked with the
first 1,000 characters, 18.9% with the first 2,000, 19.2% with the first
4,000, 19.4% with the full text, and 20.0% when an article counts as
linked if its first or its last 2,000 characters match, which catches
wire copy placed below a locally written opening; 98% of the articles
linked with the first 2,000 characters are also linked with the full
text. In 12 sampled extra pairs per window, the extra full-text links
are mostly the same story; the extra links from the last 2,000
characters include shared closing boilerplate (such as rating-agency
disclaimers), so reading the tail adds false links as well as true ones
(data/window_new_pairs.csv).

Kinds of dependence. We labelled 150 detected pairs and 150 control
pairs (same day, different outlets, a shared title word, below the
threshold) with a taxonomy drawn from newsroom practice:

| Dependence     | Definition                                                                      | Detected pairs |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------- |
| Wire copy      | Same news-agency story (AP, Reuters, Canadian Press, PA)                        | 52             |
| Same owner     | Titles of one media group share their own content                               | 40             |
| Syndication    | Licensed non-agency content (public-radio network, CNN affiliates, columns)     | 22             |
| Press release  | Same release or statement reproduced                                            | 8              |
| Aggregator     | Another outlet’s article republished                                            | 1              |
| Detector error | Shared boilerplate (login pages, paywalls, report templates); different stories | 27             |

Each pair received one label, although the kinds can overlap (a group’s
papers may all carry one agency story). The proportions describe this
labelled sample; they are descriptive estimates, not exhaustive ground
truth for all detected pairs, because the labels were drafted with an AI
assistant and checked blind by two authors on a subset (end of this
section). By the AI-assisted draft labels, 123 of the 150 detected pairs
(82%) reflect actual dependence; all 27 false matches in the labelled
sample involve shared boilerplate. Removing, before hashing, every line
that occurs in at least 20 articles of the corpus raises precision in
the labelled sample to 0.97 (116 of the 119 pairs still detected), at a
cost in recall of 7 real dependences no longer detected; the copy
statistics hardly change (18.9% of articles, 12,323 LSH candidate
chains; removing shared lines also removes some links, which splits a
few chains). By the same draft labels, 142 of the control pairs were
unrelated and 8 reported the same event independently. Only wire copies
are what a casual reader would call “the same story”; same-owner sharing
and syndication are as common together and are equally not independent.

Recall. We took 200 articles of at least 1,500 characters (boilerplate
removed) and made two kinds of copy: a reprint edited the way client
papers edit wire copy (dateline and agency credit removed, first line
dropped, last 30% cut), and a rewrite generated by phi4 (“in your own
words, the way a different newspaper would report the same story; keep
every fact, name and number”). The detector links 92% of the edited
reprints to their original (median Jaccard 0.81) and none of the
rewrites (median Jaccard 0.04; the rewrites keep 75% of the original
length). The text-similarity detector catches edited reprints, but the
tested rewrites generated by phi4 evaded it completely, and so do
rewrites of the same 200 articles by Llama 3.2 and Gemini 2.5 Flash with
the same prompt (0 of 200 each, median Jaccard 0.06).

Chains. Joining pairs into connected components could merge articles
that are not copies of each other (A like B, B like C, A unlike C). If
every candidate pair must also reach an estimated Jaccard of 0.6 (LSH
alone admits some pairs just below it), there are 12,107 chains, 7,133
of them pairs. In the 4,974 chains of three or more articles the median
mean pairwise similarity is 0.95; 5% contain a pair below 0.6, joined
only through other articles, and none a pair below 0.3; 98% of their
articles are a direct near-copy of the chain’s earliest article. The
share of articles in chains moves little with the threshold: 19.9%,
18.6% and 17.4% at 0.5, 0.6 and 0.7 (18.9% with the LSH candidates used
above), with or without boilerplate removal. The threshold 0.6 was set
before annotation and not tuned. A chain remains a cluster of
near-duplicates, not a reconstructed lineage: two articles that each
summarise one press release and a third that copies one of them form a
single chain.

The dependence counts in this section use labels drafted with an AI
assistant from titles and the first 1,500 characters of each article;
the assistant also saw the detector’s similarity score. Control pairs
are drawn without repetition among articles with a known publication
date. As a check, the first two authors (who are not independent of the
study) then labelled 100 pairs (70 detected copies, 30 controls) without
seeing the drafts, the detector scores or each other’s labels. They
agreed on 84 of 100 labels (Cohen’s $`\kappa`$ = 0.80 over the nine
labels) and on all 100 decisions whether a pair is a real dependence or
not ($`\kappa`$ = 1.00); agreement with the AI drafts was $`\kappa`$ =
0.84 and 0.92. Of the 16 disagreements, 7 lie between same-owner sharing
and wire copy, when papers of one group carry an agency story; 4 between
unrelated pairs and either detector errors or independent reports of the
same event; 3 involve aggregation and 2 syndication. The counts above
rest on the AI-assisted drafts; the blind check covers 100 of the 300
pairs.

## 4 Do LLM judges count copies?

Under controlled conditions (claims about invented people, invented
outlets), the two local judges accept far more lies as reports and
outlets go from one to three, though not monotonically beyond, and
ignore a note that the copies share one source; Gemini 2.5 Pro
distinguishes identical from differently worded reports and follows
provenance information, as a note or a table. The experiments measure
sensitivity to how evidence is presented, with provenance supplied by
the experimenter; they do not show that a judge can identify independent
reporting.

Setup. We took 320 claims from the world of our earlier paper (seed 10):
80 each of known-fact lies (CounterFact counterfactuals the student
knows), legitimate updates of known facts, lies about invented people
and true facts about invented people. Four judges: Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct
and phi4 (14B), as 4-bit Ollama builds, and Gemini 2.5 Flash and 2.5 Pro
through the API; temperature 0, answers up to 64 tokens (Gemini Pro
cannot switch reasoning off and used the smallest reasoning budget, 128
tokens). Each claim was shown in nine conditions: the claim alone; one,
three, five or ten reports with identical text from different invented
outlets; three reports with different wording; three identical reports
among seven reports about other claims (as long as the ten-copy prompt);
and three or ten identical reports with the note “all of these reports
are reprints of one and the same wire story; they are one source”. The
judge answers True or False; in a second pass on five conditions it may
also answer Unsure. Prompts are in Appendix A. Answers with neither word
count as not accepted; we report them with the Unsure answers as
abstentions: 3 for Llama 3.2 (“I cannot verify the claim”), 35 for phi4
(a reasoning chain that ran past the limit, all of them with five or ten
reports in the prompt) and 29 for Gemini Pro (26 of them “Undetermined”,
all with the one-source note).

Lies about invented people accepted as true (binary True/False answers;
n = 80 per cell; truths about invented people were accepted at nearly
the same rates in every cell; the three-way True/Unsure/False ablation
follows below; 95% Wilson intervals are at most $`\pm`$0.11 per cell,
and claim-level paired tests are reported in the findings):

| ---------------- | -------- | -------- | -------- | --------- | -------------------- | ----------------------- | --------------- | ---------------- |
| Llama 3.2 3B     | 0.06     | 0.71     | 0.55     | 0.44      | 0.34                 | 0.40                    | 0.74            | 0.51             |
| phi4 14B         | 0.21     | 0.90     | 0.78     | 0.50      | 0.97                 | 0.93                    | 0.82            | 0.57             |
| Gemini 2.5 Flash | 0.93     | 1.00     | 1.00     | 1.00      | 1.00                 | 0.97                    | 0.79            | 0.20             |
| Gemini 2.5 Pro   | 0.64     | 0.76     | 0.86     | 0.85      | 1.00                 | 0.99                    | 0.00            | 0.00             |

No judge accepted these claims without a report (at most 0.01).

Known facts (share judged true, claim alone / 3 copies / 3 different
wordings): known-fact lies 0.07 / 0.24 / 0.25 (phi4), 0.06 / 0.31 / 0.51
(Flash), 0.06 / 0.16 / 0.31 (Pro); legitimate updates 0.05 / 0.20 / 0.28
(phi4), 0.06 / 0.39 / 0.53 (Flash), 0.06 / 0.11 / 0.41 (Pro). The
judge’s prior knowledge appears to weigh most, but reports still move
it, and lies and true updates move together.

With an Unsure option (lies about invented people, True / Unsure /
False): phi4 answers 0.33 / 0.68 / 0.00 for one report and 0.96 / 0.04 /
0.00 for three copies; Gemini Pro 0.11 / 0.89 / 0.00 for one report,
0.59 / 0.41 / 0.00 for three copies and 1.00 / 0.00 / 0.00 for three
differently worded reports. Copies move the judges from Unsure to True,
so the effect is not an artefact of forcing a binary answer.

Five findings. Tests are exact McNemar tests on the same 80 claims,
Holm-adjusted over all 112 contrasts (56 for each kind of lie). (1)
Copies act as confirmations for the local judges: three reprints raise
acceptance of lies about invented people over one report for Llama 3.2
(0.06 to 0.71; 52 claims flip to True, none back; adjusted p \<
$`10^{-13}`$) and phi4 (0.21 to 0.90; 55 and 0; p \< $`10^{-14}`$). For
Gemini Flash (0.93 to 1.00, a ceiling) and Gemini Pro (0.64 to 0.76; 22
and 12) the rise is not significant. (2) Gemini Pro responds to the
wording of the reports: it accepts three differently worded reports more
often than three identical copies (1.00 against 0.76; 19 and 0; adjusted
p = 0.0004); for phi4 the difference is not significant, Flash is at
ceiling, and Llama 3.2 prefers the copies (0.34 against 0.71). Different
wordings from different outlets are not established independent
reporting, so this shows sensitivity to report diversity, not that a
judge infers where the reports come from. (3) Gemini Pro follows the
provenance note most consistently: with the note, Gemini Pro accepts
none of the lies, answering False for 0.90 and giving no verdict for
0.10 with three copies, and False for all with ten; given an Unsure
option, it answers Unsure for every claim with the note. Flash also
responds, to 0.79 (three copies) and 0.20 (ten), but less consistently:
it ignores the note on differently worded reports (0.97, probe below);
phi4 (0.82) and Llama 3.2 (0.74) do not change significantly. The probes
below test whether it reasons about provenance or follows the
instruction it is given. (4) Gemini Flash believes a single report
(0.93): for it, one unverified source is already enough. (5) Without
knowledge there is no truth signal: lies and truths about invented
people are accepted at nearly the same rates, so the reports are the
principal evidence available to the judges.

responds to outlet diversity alone, phi4 to both repetition and

significantly raise acceptance for any judge. Gemini Pro accepts
identical copies labelled independent at 0.69 against 1.00 for different

follows notes that lower belief, even when they may be wrong: three
different wordings labelled as rewrites of one story fall from 1.00 to

acceptance of a single unlabelled report (0.64), and true claims about
invented people too (0.00): it treats the note as a warning rather than
counting sources. This asymmetry is safe for a gate against lies, but a
provenance note can also block true news. (d) A provenance table has a
similar effect to an explicit instruction: Gemini Pro accepts 0.01 with
the table, and Flash (0.49 against 0.79) and Llama 3.2 (0.42 against
0.74) respond to it more than to the note; phi4 ignores both. (e) Famous
agency names do not raise acceptance. For different wordings nothing
changes; identical copies under Reuters, AP and BBC News are accepted
less by phi4 (0.55) and Gemini Pro (0.50), as if identical text from
three rival agencies were itself suspicious.

Many identical copies. The two local judges accept ten copies less often
than they accept three (Llama 3.2: 0.71 to 0.44; phi4: 0.90 to 0.50),
with five in between. Length does not explain it: three copies hidden
among seven unrelated reports, a prompt as long as ten copies, are
accepted at 0.93 by phi4 and 0.97 by Flash, at rates similar to three
copies alone; only Llama 3.2 also drops in this control (0.40). Nor does
the answer limit: with 64 answer tokens phi4 accepts 0.50 of the
ten-copy lies, against 0.44 in a first run of the same prompts with 10
tokens; answers without a verdict, mostly reasoning cut off at the
limit, count as not accepted, and excluding them gives 0.61 (0.58 in the
10-token run). The two local judges accept fewer claims with ten
identical reports than with three; Gemini 2.5 Pro shows no such drop
(0.64, 0.76, 0.86, 0.85 for 1, 3, 5, 10 copies).

## 5 Learning from a simulated stream of reports

In a controlled simulation, counting reports or chains does not keep
lies out of a continual learner. Showing an LLM judge one report per
chain cuts the lies it accepts from a copy network by 0.10 to 0.24 for
all four judges, at a mean cost of no more than 4.2 percentage points of
accepted truths across the tested judges (largest upper 95% confidence
bound: 5.9 points). With a text-similarity detector it does nothing
against reworded copies or collusion; Section 6 addresses reworded
copies, and Section 7 repeats the study with the detectors run on real
articles.

Student and claims. We reuse the setup of our earlier paper:
Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct with one LoRA adapter (rank 16, alpha 32, no
dropout, on all attention and MLP projections), trained with AdamW
(learning rate 2e-4, no weight decay) for 8 steps of batch 16 on each
chunk of 16 reports plus 32 claims replayed from a buffer of up to 512
accepted claims. A claim is a sentence prefix and its object (“Ta Tasken
was born in the city of” + “Lima”); the loss is the mean cross-entropy
on the object tokens plus
$`\mathrm{KL}(\text{base}\,\|\,\text{student})`$ on 300 anchor sentences
(weight 1, batch 16). The full configuration is in the repository. Each
world holds 400 true facts about invented people, 100 legitimate updates
of facts the model knows, and 171 lies (30% of the claims; half about
invented people, half CounterFact counterfactuals). Three seeds (10-12);
additional ten-seed robustness runs (seeds 10-19) are reported below.

Reports. Each true claim is reported by 1, 2 or 3 independent chains
(50%, 30%, 20%; varied below); the copies per chain are drawn from the
CC-News chain sizes of Section 3, so most chains are a single article.
Lies follow one of three attacks: copy (one origin and 10 verbatim
copies), paraphrase (the same, each copy reworded) and collusion (two
distinct sources that coordinate on the same false claim without copying
each other). For each copy, the simulated detector either links it to
its chain or misses it. We use the recall measured in Section 3: 0.92
for lightly edited copies and 0.00 for reworded ones (a first set of
runs with assumed values, 0.95 and 0.3, gave the same picture). Reports
arrive over 30 days in time order. The stream is simulated: the
detectors are not run on generated text; their measured rates decide,
report by report, whether a copy is linked to its chain, which keeps
runs comparable across seeds and judges.

Systems. We distinguish claims accepted for training from facts the
student absorbs (measured after the stream). We test these acceptance
rules: accept at every report (_every report_); accept once, at the
first report (_first report_); accept after three reports (_3 reports_);
accept after two chains as the detector sees them (_2 chains_); accept
at the first report if a simulated judge with accuracy 0.9 approves

detector, counting chains alone admits 57% of copied lies, close to the
chance that the detector misses at least one of ten copies

the attacker gains from sending more copies. Rewording and collusion
defeat it completely, and it blocks every truth that comes from a single
source. (3) Showing an LLM judge one report per chain reduces accepted

they accept a quarter (Llama 3.2), half (phi4) or 85% (Flash) of true

and learning a claim once absorb nearly the same share of lies about

reports shown, Llama 3.2 is worse than no filter at all; the simulated
judge reaches 0.04 alone and 0.06 with two chains.

## 6 Detecting LLM rewrites by entity novelty

Idea. A rewrite changes the words but keeps most names, numbers and
quotes; independent reporting of the same event usually introduces new
ones, such as another witness, a figure or a quote. For a report that
resembles an earlier one in topic, we therefore measure its
entity-and-quotation novelty: the share of its named entities (spaCy,
en_core_web_sm) and quoted passages that do not appear in the earlier
report. A low score marks a copy however it is worded. The score is a
proxy for new information, not a measure of new facts: two reports with
the same entities can state opposite things (“the minister confirmed”
and “the minister denied”). A simpler rule-based extractor (capitalised
names, numbers, quotes) serves as a check. The baselines below show that
the entities carry the signal and the quotations add nothing, so the
test is in effect an entity-novelty test; the stream runs, made before
this ablation, keep the quotations.

Definition. For an earlier report _o_ and a later report _r_, let E(x)
be the set of distinct items extracted from the first 2,500 characters
of x (boilerplate removed): spaCy entities of 16 types (PERSON, ORG,
GPE, LOC, NORP, FAC, EVENT, PRODUCT, WORK_OF_ART, LAW, DATE, TIME,
MONEY, PERCENT, QUANTITY, CARDINAL), lower-cased, with runs of spaces
and commas collapsed and a leading “the” removed, and quoted passages of
15-300 characters between quotation marks, each represented by its first
40 lower-cased characters. Entities and quotations are pooled in one
set, so repeated mentions count once. The novelty of _r_ relative to _o_
is $`N(r\mid o)=|E(r)\setminus E(o)|\,/\,|E(r)|`$, and _r_ is flagged as
a copy of _o_ when $`N(r\mid o)`$ \< 0.4. The score is directional: the
later report is scored against the earlier one (in the held-out
evaluation, a pair published on the same day is flagged if either
direction falls below the threshold). Items match only exactly, so a
shortened or differently cut quotation counts as new. A report with no
extractable items gets $`N=0`$ and is flagged; a report whose look-alike
has none gets $`N=1`$. Entity overlap, a baseline below, is 1 - N
computed on entities only.

Test. We compare the 200 phi4 rewrites of Section 3 with comparison
reports of the same event, found for 167 of the originals: another
outlet, published within one day, sharing more than three extracted
items with the original, and 5-gram Jaccard below 0.2. This automatic
selection is noisy. Only 9 of the 167 pairs (5%) carry the same agency
or press-release credit, but a manual check of 40 random pairs found 11
independent reports of the same event, 7 from the same source (the same
agency story, or one interview retold), 21 about a different event that
merely shares names or dates, and 1 unclear. None of the 11 confirmed
independent reports falls below the threshold of 0.4 (their novelty is
0.53-0.74; 0 of 11, 95% Wilson interval \[0, 0.26\]), and 3 of the 7
same-source reports do, which is a correct merge. Restricted to the 11
confirmed independent reports, the AUC is unchanged: 0.98 against the
plain rewrites (0.97 on all pairs) and 0.94 against the adaptive ones
(0.94 on all pairs), so the easy different-event pairs do not inflate
it; confirmed independent reports of the same event do, however, have
lower novelty (median 0.62) than the full comparison set (0.84), so the
margin above the threshold is smaller than the medians suggest. An
adaptive attacker rewrites the same 200 articles with the instruction to
add three or four plausible invented details (names, numbers, places,
quotes) so that the copy reads like independent reporting.

Detector misses are concentrated in a few networks, suggesting that
evasion depends more on the original article than on random variation
between rewrites. We reran the stream with these measured patterns, each
paraphrased lie taking the miss pattern of a randomly drawn generated
network (phi4 judge, 3 seeds). Twenty networks from one rewriting model
are too few to characterise attackers in general. Accepted paraphrased
lies fall from 0.67 with all reports shown to 0.20 (difference -0.48
\[-0.59, -0.36\]), against 0.41 with independent misses, and the
absorbed lies about invented people from 0.92 to 0.32. With invented
details the judge accepts 0.40 (-0.28 \[-0.42, -0.14\]; 0.59 with
independent misses). The cost in truths is 2.5 points as before, and
counting chains alone accepts 0.17 and 0.54. For these generated
networks, independent misses were the more pessimistic case; whether
this holds for other rewriting models or attackers is unknown. The
stream’s own reports are still single sentences: the generated networks
supply the pattern of misses, not the text the judge reads.

Sensitivity to false merges. The stream assumes a false-merge
probability of 0.05, a conservative proxy taken from the flag rate on
the automatic comparison set (the held-out set suggests 0.01). Raising
it to 0.10, 0.20 and 0.30 (phi4 judge, one report per chain, 3 seeds)
leaves accepted copied and paraphrased lies unchanged (0.33 and 0.41),
since false merges touch only independent chains, and costs truths: 0.49
at 0.05, then 0.47, 0.45 and 0.42. Under collusion, merging the two
liars helps: accepted lies fall from 0.58 to 0.56, 0.52 and 0.48. Under
the copy attack, the share of lies among accepted claims rises from 0.19
to 0.21 at 0.30.

## 7 The pipeline on real articles carrying inserted claims

Sections 5 and 6 drew each detector decision at a measured rate. Here
the detectors run on real news articles, and no detector decision is
drawn at random. The claims remain controlled: each is inserted into the
articles that carry it, so this is not an evaluation on a naturally
occurring stream of news claims, and only the two local judges were run.
On real articles, showing the judge one report per detected chain cut
the lies phi4 accepted from 0.57 to 0.09 under the copy attack, from
0.67 to 0.14 under the paraphrase attack and from 0.67 to 0.34 when the
rewriter invents details, and those Llama 3.2 accepted from 0.56-0.62 to
0.07, 0.09 and 0.25. The cost in accepted truths was 2.4 points for phi4
and 10 points for Llama 3.2. Collusion was unaffected. The copy-attack
figures are partly circular, as explained below; the rewrite figures are
not.

Carriers. Every report is a real CC-News article (boilerplate removed,
first 2,500 characters) with the claim sentence inserted after its first
line. Copies of a chain repeat their origin’s wording of the claim;
rewrites use another wording. A true claim reported by one chain is
carried by a real CC-News copy chain (an origin and its reprints by
other outlets) whose size is drawn from the measured distribution, or,
for a chain of size one, by one of 3,000 articles that no other outlet
copied. A true claim reported by two or three chains is carried by one
of the 100 held-out pairs that both authors labelled independent reports
of the same event (Section 6), each article with its real reprints if it
has any; a third chain is an unrelated article. Lies are carried by a
real chain with at least 11 outlets (copy attack; 151 chains), by the
generated networks of ten phi4 rewrites, plain or with invented details
(paraphrase and adaptive attacks; 20 networks each, Section 6), or by an
independent same-event pair (collusion). The carriers comprise 15,084
articles; their texts are rebuilt from CC-News by the released scripts.

Detection. The reports of a claim are processed in time order. A report
joins the chain of the first earlier report it matches, by 5-gram
Jaccard of at least 0.6 (text detector) or, in the full pipeline, also
by entity novelty below 0.4 (the entity-novelty test of Section 6,
“novelty test” in the tables); otherwise it starts a new chain. Worlds,
student, judges, prompts and metrics are those of Section 5, with phi4
and Llama 3.2 as judges and seeds 10-12. The judge still reads the
outlet and the claim sentence, not the article, so differences from
Section 5 come from the detection alone.

Findings. (1) With all reports shown, the real articles reproduce the
simulated stream (phi4 accepts 0.57, 0.67 and 0.62 of copied,
paraphrased and colluding lies, against 0.57, 0.67 and 0.60 in
Section 5). (2) With one report per chain, the gains are larger than in
the simulation. With independent misses, the simulation gave 0.33, 0.41
and 0.59 for copies, rewrites and rewrites with invented details; with
the miss patterns of the generated networks, it gave 0.20 and 0.40 for
the two kinds of rewrite. On real articles the values are 0.09, 0.14 and
0.34. On real articles a copy is also linked through any copy already
linked, and misses concentrate in a few networks, so fewer lies reach
the judge as two sources. For the copy attack, part of the gain comes
from the circular linking noted above. The lies about invented people
that the student absorbs fall from 0.82 to 0.16 (copy), 0.93 to 0.26
(paraphrase) and 0.93 to 0.50 (adaptive) for phi4, and from 0.76 to
0.10, 0.81 to 0.16 and 0.81 to 0.33 for Llama 3.2. The share of accepted
claims that are false falls from 0.27 to 0.06 (phi4, copy attack) and
from 0.36 to 0.09 (Llama 3.2). (3) The entity-novelty test is what makes
this possible: the text detector alone links none of the 10,260 reworded
copies, and the judge then sees exactly what it sees with all reports.
Against the text detector alone, the test lowers accepted paraphrased
lies by 0.52 \[0.44, 0.61\] and adaptive ones by 0.33 \[0.31, 0.34\], at
a cost of 0.4 points of accepted truths \[-0.1, 0.9\]. (4) The cost in
truths depends on the judge. Llama 3.2, which weighs the number of
reports most (Section 4), loses copies of true claims too and accepts 10
points fewer truths; under collusion, where no lie copies are removed,
its share of lies among accepted claims rises from 0.23 to 0.30. For
phi4 the cost is 2.4 points, legitimate updates learned change by -0.02
\[-0.04, -0.01\] (copy attack), retention does not fall, and accepted
truths reach the student on average 0.1 days later. (5) Counting chains
without a judge remains unreliable: it admits 0.45 of adaptive lies and
nearly every colluding one. (6) Collusion is unaffected, as in the
simulation.

Remaining limits of this run. The articles are real, but the claim is
inserted into them, so every report of a claim shares the claim’s names;
this makes copies and independent reports more alike, although wrong
merges stayed at 1.2%. The 20 rewrite networks are reused across the 171
lies of each world. The judge reads the claim sentence, not the article.
A stream of naturally occurring claims, with copying as it occurs in the
news, remains to be tested.

## 8 Discussion

The effect of provenance information varies across judges. Llama 3.2 and
phi4 count reports and ignore a note that the reports share a source, so
for them a note in the prompt is no defence; changing the evidence they
see is. Gemini 2.5 Pro distinguishes identical from differently worded
reports and follows provenance metadata, as a note or as a plain table,
but only in the direction of doubt: it ignores a false claim of
independence and obeys a note of shared origin even below its trust in a
single report. With Gemini 2.5 Pro, provenance in the prompt is
therefore useful if it is correct; a wrong one-source note blocks true
news as well. For Llama 3.2 and Gemini Flash a provenance table works
better than an instruction (phi4 ignores both), and the two local judges
respond to outlet diversity as well as repetition, without reliably
identifying whether the reports provide independent evidence. Copy
removal reduced accepted copy attacks for all four judges and fits where
learners sit in practice: between a news feed and a model, where outlet,
timing and text are available.

Lexical similarity is insufficient; entity overlap adds a signal. A
text-similarity detector never recognised an article rewritten by a
language model. Asking whether a report adds new entities and quotations
recognised most phi4 rewrites and cut the reworded-copy lies the judge
accepted from 0.67 to 0.41 with independent misses, to 0.20 with the
miss patterns of the generated networks and to 0.14 on real articles,
but an attacker who invents details weakens it, it recognised only 17%
of natural same-source derivatives, so it detects the tested LLM
rewrites and not information dependence in general, and when misses are
independent, one missed copy among ten is enough to show the judge two
sources. Colluding sources are distinct and coordinated by construction;
because they do not copy one another, they evade the dependence signals
evaluated here. Stronger defences need evidence beyond the article:
agency credits and datelines, ownership of outlets, publication timing
(which may reveal copies that follow their origin within hours), and
shared errors or quotes. These are the signals journalists use, and our
taxonomy names where each applies: wire copies carry an agency credit,
same-owner sharing follows the ownership register, press releases share
a source document.

Dependence detection does not replace reliable claim verification. Copy
removal is a layer that filters evidence before a judge sees it; it is
not a fact-checker. The local judges accepted only a quarter to half of
true claims and few legitimate updates, consistent with our earlier
finding that judges defend their own beliefs; Gemini Flash accepted most
truths but also believed a single unverified report, which no amount of
copy removal can fix. For claims about unknown entities no judge had a
truth signal of its own. Given an Unsure option, phi4 and Gemini Pro
used it for a single report, and copies moved them from Unsure to True.
A continual learner fed by news needs a judge that can say “not enough
independent evidence yet” and wait, and a pipeline that counts the
evidence correctly before asking. Copy removal fixes one failure,
counting copies as sources; it does not make an unreliable judge
reliable, which is why Gemini Flash, already convinced by one report,
gains least. The reverse holds for a judge that counts reports: on real
articles Llama 3.2 gained most against copied lies but also lost 10
points of accepted truths, because removing copies removes the
repetition that persuaded it of true claims as well.

Counting rules alone fail in both directions. A threshold on reports is
beaten by copies; a threshold on chains is beaten by the detector’s own
misses, even with the entity-novelty test, and it blocks true news that
only one outlet carries. Independence is useful as an input to
judgement, not as a substitute for it, and it is not truth: ten
independent reports can repeat the same error, and one report can be
right.

## 9 Limitations

- •
  Detector in the learning study. Sections 5 and 6 enter measured
  detector rates as per-report probabilities. Section 7 runs the
  detectors on real articles, but the claim sentence is inserted into
  them, 20 rewrite networks are reused across lies, the copy-attack
  chains were found by the text detector itself, and only phi4 and Llama
  3.2 judge. Recall on reworded copies was measured with three rewriting
  models (phi4, Llama 3.2, Gemini 2.5 Flash) and one prompt, and with
  one adaptive prompt (phi4); the stream uses the phi4 rates, and a
  stronger attacker could invent more. The false-alarm rate of the
  entity-novelty test on independent reports rests on 100 pairs labelled
  by the first two authors, who are not independent of the study, and
  the test recognises only 17% of natural same-source derivatives. The
  entity-novelty test was run in the simulated stream with phi4 only,
  and on real articles with phi4 and Llama 3.2. Its score counts
  entities and quotations, not propositions, so it cannot tell a report
  that contradicts its look-alike from one that repeats it. The
  detectors were run on 20 generated networks of ten rewrites, whose
  miss patterns drive the simulated stream; in Section 7 the same
  networks carry the lies.
- •
  Seeds and detector errors. The main learning results use three seeds
  (10-12), so their paired t-intervals have two degrees of freedom.
  Additional ten-seed robustness runs are reported for Llama 3.2 and
  Gemini Flash with the text-similarity detector and for phi4 with the
  entity-novelty detector; Gemini 2.5 Pro and the remaining settings use
  three seeds. Detector misses are drawn independently for each copy
  unless stated; correlated misses and the miss patterns of the
  generated rewrite networks made the defences stronger, not weaker
  (Section 6), which need not hold for every attacker.
- •
  Share of single-source truths. We assume half of true claims come from
  one chain and varied it (20%, 50%, 80%) for phi4 only.
- •
  Invented claims and outlets. Claims about invented people and invented
  outlet names isolate the effect of report counts, but real articles
  carry several claims, hedges and context, and real readers and judges
  also weigh outlet reputation; the acceptance rates should not be read
  as those of a deployed fact-checker. The local judges are 4-bit builds
  and the Gemini judges API models, so model family, quantisation and
  serving are confounded.
- •
  Models. One student (1.5B) and four LLM judges; the strongest judge in
  our evaluation (Gemini 2.5 Pro) was used in the learning stream for
  the copy attack only. Three seeds per setting, except the ten-seed
  runs above.
- •
  Answer length. The learning runs with local judges allowed 10 answer
  tokens, which cut some of phi4’s answers to many reports (counted as
  rejections); this can only understate how often phi4 accepts copied
  lies with all reports shown.
- •
  Annotation. Dependence labels were drafted with an AI assistant; the
  first two authors labelled 100 pairs blind ($`\kappa`$ = 0.80) and the
  250 held-out pairs of Section 6 ($`\kappa`$ = 0.86), but the authors
  are not independent of the study, and the boundary between same-owner
  sharing and wire copy is not sharp.

## 10 Conclusion

Our detector links about one sampled news article in five to a near-copy
from another outlet. The two local LLM judges treat such copies as
confirmation: three reprints of a claim about an unknown person raised
acceptance of lies from 0.21 to 0.90 (phi4), and a note that the reports
share one source did not undo it. Gemini 2.5 Pro was the most responsive
to provenance information and distinguished identical from differently
worded reports more consistently than the other judges; Gemini Flash
believes a single report. In a simulated continual learner, removing
copies before the judge sees the evidence reduced the lies accepted from
a copy network across all tested judges and seeds, most for the judges
that count reports, while counting reports or chains without a judge was
unreliable. Rewrites by phi4, Llama 3.2 and Gemini Flash evade the
tested text-similarity detector; an entity-novelty test recognises most
of them, on the tested sets as well as TF-IDF and the best tested
embedding model, but only 17% of natural same-source derivatives, and
with the miss patterns of generated rewrite networks cut the
reworded-copy lies accepted from 0.67 to 0.20 (0.40 when the rewriter
invents details). Colluding sources still pass. The next step is to
recognise dependence the way journalists do, from credits, ownership and
timing as well as content, and to give judges the option to wait for
independent evidence. Run on real news articles with the real detectors,
the pipeline kept out most copied and reworded lies for both local
judges, at a cost in accepted truths of 2.4 points for phi4 and 10
points for Llama 3.2; the claims in that run were inserted into the
articles, so a stream of naturally occurring claims remains to be
tested.
