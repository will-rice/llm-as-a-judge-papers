###### Abstract

Recent researches indicate that Pre-trained Large Language Models (LLMs)
possess cognitive constructs similar to those observed in humans,
prompting researchers to investigate the cognitive aspects of LLMs. This
paper focuses on explicit and implicit social bias, a distinctive
two-level cognitive construct in psychology. It posits that individuals’
explicit social bias, which is their conscious expression of bias in the
statements, may differ from their implicit social bias, which represents
their unconscious bias. We propose a two-stage approach and discover a
parallel phenomenon in LLMs known as "re-judge inconsistency" in social
bias. In the initial stage, the LLM is tasked with automatically
completing statements, potentially incorporating implicit social bias.
However, in the subsequent stage, the same LLM re-judges the biased
statement generated by itself but contradicts it. We propose that this
re-judge inconsistency can be similar to the inconsistency between
human’s unaware implicit social bias and their aware explicit social
bias. Experimental investigations on ChatGPT and GPT-4 concerning common
gender biases examined in psychology corroborate the highly stable
nature of the re-judge inconsistency. This finding may suggest that
diverse cognitive constructs emerge as LLMs’ capabilities strengthen.
Consequently, leveraging psychological theories can provide enhanced
insights into the underlying mechanisms governing the expressions of
explicit and implicit constructs in LLMs.

## 1 Introduction

Recently, there has been considerable works focusing on the comparative
analysis of the cognitive capabilities between Large Language Models
(LLMs) and human beings Warwick and Shah (2016); Zhao et al. (2022);
Kadavath et al. (2022); Jiang et al. (2022); Jin et al. (2022); Dillion
et al. (2023). Li et al. (2022) undertakes an assessment of the
personalities of diverse LLMs, employing established tools such as the
Short Dark Triad (SD-3) and Big Five Inventory (BFI). In their study,
they meticulously contrast the personality scores obtained from LLMs
against those derived from human participants, highlighting the
manifestation of comparatively darker personality traits in LLMs.

Zhao et al. (2022) presents a comprehensive and task-oriented evaluation
framework aimed at examining the cognitive capabilities of LLMs. They
find that LLMs demonstrate a lack of practical abilities when compared
to humans. In a related vein, Rao et al. (2023) investigates the
feasibility of employing LLMs for the assessment of human personality
traits. Their experimental outcomes demonstrate the consistent and
effective nature of personality assessment conducted by ChatGPT Ouyang
et al. (2022). Notably, these studies involve transferring theories that
explain the fundamental mechanisms of human behaviors to LLMs. Despite
the discernible distinctions in cognition between LLMs and humans, this
transfer of knowledge remains immensely enlightening. It warrants
further exploration wherein human cognitive theories can be suitably
adapted and validated through empirical experimentation in LLMs.
Consequently, these theories hold the potential to explicate the
behavioral mechanisms governing the functioning of LLMs.

In this paper, we explore the intricate relationship between LLMs and
the implicit cognitive theories within the field of psychology. Notably,
a series of works Greenwald and Banaji (1995); Greenwald et al. (1998);
Greenwald et al. (2020) provide significant insights into implicit
cognitive theories. These theories posit that human social cognition
comprises two distinct levels: explicit and implicit. Explicit cognition
refers to conscious cognitive processes that are mainly obtained through
self-report measurements, which can be influenced by individual values,
ethical considerations, and societal norms. In contrast, implicit
cognition operates at an unconscious and automatic level, representing
individuals’ genuine cognitive tendencies. Implicit cognition is mainly
assessed through the Implicit Association Test (IAT) introduced by
Greenwald et al. (1998). The IAT measures reaction times to evaluate an
individual’s automatic associations between specific attributes (e.g.,
occupations) and targets (e.g., genders). It reveals human implicit
social biases that usually deviate from self-reported beliefs,
particularly in sensitive social domains such as gender and race.

To extend the assessment of implicit cognition to pre-trained language
models, Caliskan et al. (2017) proposes the Word Embedding Association
Test (WEAT), which draws inspiration from the IAT. The WEAT quantifies
semantic associations between target words and attribute words within
word embeddings. Building upon this framework, May et al. (2019)
introduces the Sentence Embedding Association Test (SEAT), which further
extends the measurement of bias to sentence encoders through semantic
bleaching. While these studies have demonstrated the associations
between human cognition and the cognition of language models, they have
yet to differentiate the explicit and implicit dimensions of cognitive
constructs within LLMs.

In order to explore the presence of explicit and implicit levels of
cognitive constructs within LLMs, we design a two-stage experimental
methodology. This approach unveils a parallel phenomenon in LLMs, termed
as "re-judge inconsistency" in social bias. The initial stage involves
assigning the LLM with the task of completing gender-related statements,
thereby potentially incorporating implicit social bias. Subsequently, in
the second stage, the LLM re-judges the biased statements generated by
itself earlier but contradicts them. This observed re-judge
inconsistency is posited to reflect the incongruity between LLMs’
unconscious implicit social bias and their conscious explicit social
bias.

To facilitate this investigation, the analogy tests introduced by
Bolukbasi et al. (2016) are extended in the first stage. This extension
adopts a more open-ended template: "A are to X as B are to Y," where X
and Y represent predefined attributes. The LLM is then prompted to
automatically complete the values for A and B. Through this process, the
automatic bias of LLMs is measured, akin to the Implicit Association
Test (IAT) framework. Additionally, the LLM engages in re-judging the
biased statements, resembling the self-report measurements employed in
psychology. Figure 1 provides an illustrative example of the
inconsistencies observed between the automatic bias and re-judgement
bias in GPT-4, particularly concerning specific targets (man vs. woman)
and attributes (secretary vs CEO).

Experiments are conducted on two prominent language models, ChatGPT and
GPT-4 Ouyang et al. (2022); OpenAI (2023). The results demonstrate
significant inconsistencies across various paired attributes. This
observed inconsistency serves as preliminary evidence substantiating the
existence of explicit and implicit levels of cognitive constructs in
LLMs.

Our contributions are delineated as follows:

(1) This is the first known work to theoretically associate LLMs with
implicit and explicit social cognition of psychology.

(2) We design an experimental methodology to measure the automatic and
re-judgement biases of LLMs, drawing on the Implicit Association Test
(IAT) in psychology.

(3) Our experiments on typical LLMs and attributes provide preliminary
evidences that LLMs possess different levels of cognitive constructs. We
believe that it will encourage future work in this direction.

## 2 Re-judge Inconsistency Test of Social Bias

### 2.1 Open-ended Analogy Templates

The IAT measures human automatic biases between a pair of attributes and
a pair of targets through reaction time. We adapt the IAT into a
language-based fill-in-the-blank method. Specially, we present an
open-ended analogy template: "A are to X as B are to Y", where A and B
represent targets, and X and Y signify attributes. The LLM is prompted
to automatically complete either X and Y given A and B, or A and B given
X and Y. Ideally, this flexible template allows for the evaluation of
any type of bias by supplying relevant targets or attributes. As an
illustration, we use ’White American’ for X and ’Black American’ for Y,
then let ChatGPT automatically complete A and B. Although our prompts do
not contain any implicit suggestions of bias, ChatGPT shows strong
racial bias. The results can be found in appendix C.

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback(RLHF) have been used to align
LLMs to human valuesOuyang et al. (2022); Menick et al. (2022). The LLM
is likely to recognize that templates containing specific words are
designed to measure bias and align their responses to human values.
Consequently, we highly recommend supplying non-descriptive attributes
and allowing the LLM to automatically generate target terms. In this
way, we reduces the likelihood of LLMs recognizing the bias measurement,
a strategy similar to the use of target and attribute exemplars in the
IAT.

However, the bias measured by a single template is not reliable(Seshadri
et al. (2022); Goldfarb-Tarrant et al. (2023)). To address this problem
and achieve more stable results, We create additional templates using
several approaches: swapping the order of paired attributes, adding or
removing the punctuation, altering the verb tenses and replacing words
with different words. Following this process, we obtain 10 templates,
and the full list is available in Appendix A. We will use these 10
templates to measure biases associated with different attributes.

### 2.2 Automatic Gender Bias in Occupations

To avoid the LLM recognizing that templates are measuring bias, we use
paired occupations as attributes from works of NLP and
psychologyBolukbasi et al. (2016); Adachi (2013); Clarke (2020). Then,
the LLM is asked to automatically complete A and B in the templates.
Specially, we focus on gender specific words(e.g., man and woman) as
gender bias is the most common form of bias and humans typically show
inconsistencies between explicit bias and implicit bias regarding
gender. In this way, we evaluate automatic gender bias in occupations of
LLMs.

### 2.3 Re-judge Biased Statements

Next, the LLMs are prompted to re-judge if the statements generated by
themselves are right or wrong.

This re-judgement is similar to the self-report measurement in
psychology, which directly measures human bias. If there is a
contradiction between the model’s automatic completion and its
re-judgement, it indicates the re-judge inconsistency.

## 3 Experimental Setup

### 3.1 Large Language Models

#### ChatGPT.

ChatGPTOuyang et al. (2022), a language model developed by OpenAI, has
made significant advancements in various tasks. Additionally, it aligns
with human values and is able to identify toxic and biased content.

#### GPT-4.

GPT-4OpenAI (2023) is an upgrade to ChatGPT, capable of accepting
multi-modal inputs. OpenAI has employed various methods to improve the
safety of GPT-4. Furthermore, it better aligns with human values and is
proficient at rejecting unacceptable prompts.

### 3.2 Implementation

We first measure automatic bias of LLMs. When measuring automatic bias,
not all outputs from LLMs include gender words. Therefore, for each
attribute pair, we independently collect 10 statements that include
gender words per template. We present the set of gender specific words
generated by ChatGPT and GPT-4 in Appendix B. This approach allows us to
obtain 100 statements featuring gender words for each attribute pair.
Some of them exhibit stereotypes(e.g., Men are to engineering as women
are to teaching), while others have anti-stereotypes(e.g., Women are to
engineering as men are to teaching). We calculate the number of
statements with stereotypes. In this way, we obtain the _stereotype
number of automatic_ of 100 gender-based statements.

When measuring re-judgement bias, the LLM is asked to re-judge all
stereotypical statements it generated in the first stage. if its
response is right, then it is considered stereotypical. If the response
is wrong and identifies the bias of the statement, then it is considered
unbiased. In this way, we obtain the _stereotype number of
re-judgement_.

## 4 Results and Discussion

#### Automatic Bias vs. Re-judgement Bias

As shown in Table 1, the stereotype numbers of automatic suggest that
both ChatGPT and GPT-4 exhibit strong automatic stereotype across all
pairs of attributes. However, the stereotype numbers of re-judgement
indicate that they mostly re-judge no stereotype. This discrepancy
indicates a serious inconsistency between automatic bias and
re-judgement bias of ChatGPT and GPT-4.

#### ChatGPT vs. GPT-4

As shown in Table 1, GPT-4 exhibits strong automatic stereotype on more
attribute pairs than ChatGPT(7 vs. 3). Previous work has shown that more
powerful LLMs are less truthfulLin et al. (2022). Similarly, our
experimental results may suggest that more powerful LLMs exhibit strong
automatic implicit bias. Since they exhibit almost no stereotype during
re-judgment, more powerful models may also be more inconsistent.One
possible reason is that while RLHF aligns LLMs with human values, this
alignment is superficial. Therefore, LLMs still exhibit strong automatic
bias.

#### The Explanation of Psychology

In psychology, human explicit social bias is the result of evaluating
implicit social cognition based on human values. Consequently, human
beings may also exhibit the inconsistency between explicit constructs
and implicit constructs, especially when faced with socially sensitive
subjects(e.g.,_gender_ and _race_).

This process is similar to our re-judgement framework. It may suggest
that as LLMs become more capable, different levels of cognitive
constructs also emerge. Specially, The automatic bias and re-judgement
bias in LLMs correspond to human implicit and explicit bias
respectively. The process of RLHF aligning LLMs with human values is
also similar to human learning of social norms. Therefore, LLMs do not
exhibit explicit bias towards gender, race, or other sensitive targets.
However, the LLMs still have strong bias in their inner cognition, which
is implicit bias.

## 5 Conclusion

In this paper, we explore the cognitive constructs of LLMs inspired by
different levels of cognitive constructs of human in psychology. We
propose a two-stage approach to investigate the re-judge inconsistency
of LLMs. This inconsistency demonstrates a distinct reflexivity, where
LLMs not only express bias but also evaluate the bias. We observe this
inconsistency across multiple attributes in ChatGPT and GPT-4. It
highlights the existence of different levels of cognitive constructs in
LLMs, a significant discovery that necessitates further exploration. Our
work invites more investigations into the cognitive capabilities and
biases of LLMs.

## Limitations

There are some limitaions in our work. First, our research investigate
only a limited number of language models, specifically ChatGPT and
GPT-4. Moreover, the limited number of accesses to LLMs results in an
insufficient quantity of bias types and attributes in our research.
Consequently, we will further research more large models and a wider
variety of biases and attributes in the future work. Finally, the
conclusions in this paper are currently limited to the biases of the
models and attributes involved in the experiments. We do not claim that
these results can necessarily be generalized.

## Ethics Statement

Our work does not involve training data related to privacy since we
focus on biases of LLMs. The outputs obtained by the LLMs also do not
involve user privacy. Although the social biases explored in our work
are linked to ethical considerations, our study of bias aligns with
human mainstream values. Finally, The targets and attributes explored in
this paper are only for illustration purposes and do not include any
discrimination or bias.
