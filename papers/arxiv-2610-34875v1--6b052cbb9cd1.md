# RubriX: A Fast, Fine-Tunable Non-Autoregressive Rubric Judge

Balamurugan Paramasivam Affiliation: CobuildX
Email: [balamurugan.p@cobuildx.ai](mailto:)    Sudalai Rajkumar
Affiliation: CobuildX Email: [srk@cobuildx.ai](mailto:)

###### Abstract

Rubric-based evaluation asks which level of a rubric a response meets.
It is usually done by prompting a large language model (LLM) as a judge,
which needs a generative call per judgment and reads every level
description, so its cost grows with the rubric. We present RubriX, a
rubric judge whose architecture is inspired by contrastive language
models (CLM): a LoRA-adapted encoder embeds the input and each level
description, and a softmax over scaled cosine similarities gives a
probability for every level without text generation. Trained on public
rubric data and proprietary synthetic data, RubriX reaches 75.4% exact
accuracy on held-out rubrics, against 57.1% for TypeSafe’s Jev and 49.8%
for a Qwen3-8B judge. On eight human-labelled benchmark sets it is
within 6 percentage points of Jev’s accuracy on most of them, while Jev
keeps a larger lead on two and ranks responses best on average; against
the results reported in the G-Eval paper, RubriX outperforms G-Eval with
GPT-3.5 on every SummEval dimension and both QAGS subsets. With level
embeddings cached, RubriX’s latency stays at about 28 ms per judgment as
level descriptions grow from 10 to 3,000 tokens, while Jev’s
(approximate network time subtracted) rises from 44 ms to 211 ms and the
Qwen3-8B judge’s from 32 ms to 516 ms, making RubriX 7.7$`\times`$
faster than Jev and 18.8$`\times`$ faster than the Qwen3-8B judge at
3,000 tokens per level.

## 1 Introduction

Evaluating AI systems increasingly comes down to one repeated decision:
given a response and a rubric, which level of the rubric does the
response meet? This is usually done by prompting a large language model
as a judge \[29\], as in the rubric-based metrics of Ragas \[10, 23\]
and the G-Eval metric \[18\] implemented in DeepEval \[5\]. These
approaches are flexible, since any criterion can be written as text, but
every judgment needs a full generative LLM call over the whole rubric,
which makes them slow and costly when applied to every response and to
long rubrics. Their scores also depend on prompt wording, decoding
settings and output parsing.

RubriX takes a different approach, with an architecture inspired by
contrastive language models (CLM) \[2\]. The response and each rubric
level are embedded with a language-model encoder, and small trained
heads score how well the response matches each level. A rubric is scored
without text generation, returning a probability for every level, and
the level embeddings can be computed once per rubric and reused for
every response. Our contributions are:

- •
  A non-autoregressive rubric judge whose cost does not grow with the
  rubric. RubriX scores a response against a rubric’s own level
  descriptions with a bi-encoder: one LoRA-adapted encoder embeds the
  input and each level separately, so a rubric of any size is scored
  without generation, and its level embeddings are computed once and
  reused for every response it judges.
- •
  Ordinal training for a CLM-inspired architecture. The original CLM
  treats options as unordered. We train with cross-entropy over each
  rubric’s own levels plus a ranked probability score whose weight
  adapts to the running magnitudes of the two losses, so the ordinal
  term keeps about an equal share of the objective throughout training
  without a hand-tuned weight. Weighting losses adaptively from their
  running statistics is established in multi-task learning \[4, 12\]; we
  apply a simple form of it to the ordinal term.
- •
  Training data. RubriX is trained on a criterion-capped mix of public
  rubric datasets and a proprietary dataset.
- •
  Evaluation and latency. We compare RubriX with Jev \[25\], a Qwen3-8B
  judge \[21\] and the published G-Eval results \[18\] on held-out
  rubrics and eight human-labelled benchmark sets, by accuracy and by
  ranking correlation, and show that with cached level embeddings its
  latency stays flat as rubrics gain levels and longer level
  descriptions, while judges that read the levels slow down.

The RubriX-8B model is open source and available on Hugging Face at
[https://huggingface.co/CobuildX-AI/RubriX-8B](https://huggingface.co/CobuildX-AI/RubriX-8B),
together with code to load it and score rubrics.

## 2 Related Work

#### LLM-as-a-judge.

Strong LLMs agree well with human preferences when prompted to evaluate
responses \[29\], which has made them the default evaluator for
open-ended generation. They are, however, sensitive to prompt format and
position, and every judgment is a generative call. A rubric judge of
this kind also places the level descriptions inside its prompt, so every
judgment reprocesses the whole rubric: its cost grows with the number of
levels and with the length of each description, and the rubric must fit
in the model’s context window together with the input. In our
measurements (Section 5.4), a Qwen3-8B judge with a 32k context holds at
most 20 levels of 1,500 tokens each.

#### Rubric-based evaluation in Ragas.

Ragas \[10\] provides a rubric-based scoring metric for LLM outputs
\[23\]. The user defines a rubric as a description for each score level,
typically 1 to 5. An LLM judge reads the input, the response and
optionally a reference, then returns the score whose description fits
best, along with written feedback. Ragas also supports instance-specific
rubrics, where each example carries its own level descriptions.

#### G-Eval.

G-Eval \[18\] uses an LLM with chain-of-thought to evaluate generated
text against user-defined criteria. The model first expands the criteria
into evaluation steps, then scores the output in a form-filling step.
Instead of taking the single generated score, G-Eval weights each
possible score by the probability the model assigns to it, which gives
finer-grained scores with better alignment to human judgments. DeepEval
\[5\] implements this approach as its GEval metric for custom criteria.
We compare with G-Eval’s published results on SummEval and QAGS.

#### Jev.

Jev from TypeSafe \[25\] is a hosted model built for decisions rather
than generation. Given a context and a set of options, it returns a
probability for each option in a single pass, with no text to generate
or parse. Rubric scoring maps onto it directly: the criterion becomes
the question and the level descriptions become ordered options. Jev is
available as a hosted API; its weights are not released, and a score
question accepts at most 10 options.

#### Contrastive scoring.

Contrastive objectives such as InfoNCE \[20\] and CLIP \[22\] learn a
shared embedding space in which matching pairs score highly, using a
temperature-scaled cosine similarity. CLM \[2\] applies this idea to
choice tasks with a frozen LLM encoder and trained projection heads.
RubriX’s architecture is inspired by CLM; it adapts the encoder and
trains with an ordinal objective for rubric scoring.

#### Summary.

Ragas and G-Eval treat evaluation as a generation task, with the cost
and sensitivity that implies. Jev shows that scoring the rubric’s
options directly is practical; its weights are not released. This leaves
room for a rubric scorer that scores options directly and that can also
be trained and adapted openly.

## 3 Method

![](arxiv-2610-34875v1--6b052cbb9cd1.figures/inline-1.svg)

Figure 1: The RubriX scorer. The input and each level description are
encoded separately by the same encoder, projected by two heads, and
compared by temperature-scaled cosine similarity. A softmax over the
rubric’s own $`K`$ levels gives a probability for each level. Level
embeddings do not depend on the input, so they can be computed once per
rubric and cached.

### 3.1 Scoring model

RubriX scores a response against a rubric with a contrastive approach
(Figure 1). The input $`x`$ (the conversation, the response to evaluate
and the criterion) and each rubric level description $`\ell_{k}`$ are
embedded separately by a language-model encoder, Qwen3-8B \[21\], with
no text generation. Each text’s embedding is its final hidden state, the
last layer’s hidden state at the last token, L2-normalised. The same
encoder, with one shared set of adapters (Section 3.3), embeds both. Two
projection heads, $`f`$ for inputs and $`g`$ for levels, each a
three-layer MLP ($`4096\!\to\!1536\!\to\!1536\!\to\!512`$ with LayerNorm
and GELU), map the embeddings into a shared space. For a rubric with
$`K`$ levels, the score of level $`k`$ is

```math
s_{k}=\tau\cdot\cos\big(f(x),\,g(\ell_{k})\big), \tag{1}
```

and the predicted probability of level $`k`$ is

```math
p_{k}=\frac{\exp(s_{k})}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp(s_{j})}. \tag{2}
```

The temperature $`\tau=100`$ is inherited from CLM, where it is a
learned parameter capped at 100. Each example competes only against the
levels of its own rubric, never against other rubrics’ levels.

### 3.2 Training objective

For a gold level $`y`$, the primary objective is cross-entropy over the
rubric’s levels:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}=-\log p_{y}. \tag{3}
```

Cross-entropy treats the levels as unordered: predicting a level two
steps away costs the same as predicting a neighbouring one. We add the
ranked probability score (RPS) \[9\], which compares the predicted
cumulative distribution with the gold one:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RPS}}=\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\Big(P_{k}-\mathbf{1}[y\leq k]\Big)^{2},\qquad P_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}. \tag{4}
```

Here $`P_{k}`$ is the predicted probability that the level is at most
$`k`$. A prediction one level from the gold level costs less than one
several levels away. RPS is much smaller than cross-entropy in
magnitude, so a fixed weight lets it contribute little. We therefore
weight it adaptively, so that on average it contributes as much as
cross-entropy:

```math
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}+\lambda_{t}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RPS}},\qquad\lambda_{t}=\alpha\,\frac{\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{CE}}}{\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{RPS}}}, \tag{5}
```

where the bars are exponential moving averages (decay 0.99) of the two
losses over micro-batches, computed from detached values so that no
gradient flows through $`\lambda_{t}`$, seeded with the first
micro-batch and capped at 1000; we use $`\alpha=1`$. This is a simple
instance of adaptive loss balancing \[4, 12\]: instead of a fixed
weight, each term is scaled by its own running magnitude.

### 3.3 Encoder adaptation

Low-rank adapters \[13\] are added to all attention and MLP projections
of the encoder and trained jointly with the heads, so that the encoder
itself can learn features that separate adjacent levels. This trains
43.6M adapter parameters (about 0.5% of the encoder) and 18.9M head
parameters. The heads start from the released CLM-8B heads \[2\]. For
serving, the adapters are merged into the encoder weights, so inference
has the cost of the base encoder.

## 4 Experimental Setup

### 4.1 Training data

The training set (Table 1) has 34,261 examples covering 2,284 distinct
criteria. It combines public rubric-labelled data with a proprietary
dataset. The proprietary dataset contains 8,039 rubric-labelled examples
over 324 criteria, covering long-form answers to expert questions,
multi-turn conversations, and other open-ended writing tasks. It was
generated with a large language model and filtered by blind re-grading:
an example was kept only when an independent grader, without seeing the
intended level, assigned exactly that level. The public part is drawn
from seven sources and capped at 25 examples per criterion, balanced
across levels within each criterion, so that sources with a handful of
fixed criteria (HelpSteer’s attributes, for example) contribute few
examples and the many custom rubrics of the Feedback Collection \[15\]
and the principles of HelpSteer3 \[19\] contribute most. The test set is
split from training by criterion: no criterion or level description of
the held-out test set appears in training, and some criteria
(HelpSteer’s complexity and HelpSteer2’s verbosity, for example) are
held out entirely. The validation data, used only to select the
checkpoint, partly shares criteria with training.

### 4.2 Benchmarks

We evaluate on six human-labelled benchmarks (Table 2). JudgmentBench
scores each answer with pass/fail checklist items and with penalty items
that count errors; we report these separately as JudgmentBench, binary
and JudgmentBench, multi-level, with the counts grouped into four levels
(none, one, a few, many). QAGS is reported per subset. Rubric text is
kept verbatim where the benchmark provides it. Where a benchmark has no
level descriptions (HealthBench and the JudgmentBench checklist items
are met/not-met, JudgmentBench’s penalty items are counts, SummEval has
1–5 ratings and QAGS a yes/no question), short generic level
descriptions were added, the same for every judge, since there is no
other way to pose those items as a choice between levels. For QAGS, the
criterion is the question its workers answered, asked of the whole
summary: “Is the summary factually supported by the article?”.

### 4.3 Baselines

Jev \[25\] receives one _score_ question per judgment: the rubric’s
criterion is the question’s instructions, and its level descriptions,
lowest first, are the question’s criteria. Jev returns a probability for
each criterion, which we use as its distribution over levels. Qwen3-8B
\[21\], the same base model as RubriX’s encoder, is used as a zero-shot
judge with thinking disabled: the levels are listed lowest first and
lettered A, B, C, …, and the level distribution is the softmax of the
first generated token’s log-probabilities restricted to those letters.
G-Eval \[18\] results are copied from its paper for the two benchmarks
it shares with us, SummEval (its Table 1) and QAGS (its Table 3), with
GPT-4 (G-Eval-4) and GPT-3.5 (G-Eval-3.5).

### 4.4 Metrics

Exact accuracy is the share of judgments whose most likely level equals
the gold level, defined as the level nearest to the raters’ mean score,
with exact halves assigned to the lower level (on the held-out set, the
dataset’s own label). For QAGS, whose human score is the share of a
summary’s sentences that the workers’ majority judged supported, a
summary is labelled supported only if every one of its sentences is, and
unsupported if even one is not. Within-one accuracy counts a prediction
at most one level away as correct; it is reported for multi-level
benchmarks only, since on two levels it is always 100%. Every judge is
scored on the same judgments.

Ranking correlation: we report summary-level Spearman $`\rho`$ and
Kendall $`\tau`$ \[3\]. Judgments are grouped by input and criterion,
for example one SummEval article’s 16 summaries for one dimension;
within each group the judge’s scores are correlated with the human
scores (Kendall $`\tau`$ as $`\tau`$-b, which handles ties), so the
question is whether a judge orders the responses to the same input the
way people do. Group correlations are averaged within each criterion,
then within each category, and the reported AVG is the mean over a
benchmark’s categories. A judge’s score is its probability-weighted
level, $`\sum_{k}p_{k}\,k`$, the same score G-Eval uses (Jev’s
probabilities, returned to two decimals, are renormalised to sum to
one). Groups with fewer than three responses, or where the human score
or the judge’s score never changes, are skipped. QAGS has one summary
per article, so, as in G-Eval, each subset is one correlation over all
its summaries.

### 4.5 Implementation details

The encoder is Qwen3-8B in bf16 on one H100 80GB GPU, with gradient
checkpointing. LoRA uses rank 16, $`\alpha=32`$ and dropout 0.05 on all
seven attention and MLP projections. We train with AdamW (no weight
decay; learning rates $`10^{-4}`$ for the adapters and
$`5\times 10^{-4}`$ for the heads), micro-batches of 8 with 4
accumulation steps (an effective batch of 32), 5% linear warm-up then
linear decay, and gradient clipping at 1.0, for one epoch of 1,071
steps. The checkpoint with the lowest log-loss on a fixed sample of the
validation set was kept (step 1,068). Training took 10,212 seconds (2.8
hours) over 32.4M tokens. For evaluation, the adapters are merged into
the encoder, which is served in bf16 with vLLM \[17\].

## 5 Results

### 5.1 Held-out rubrics

The test set held out from the training mix contains rubrics whose
criteria and level descriptions never appear in training: 6,749
judgments, from the Feedback Collection (4,999), the proprietary dataset
(1,172), HelpSteer3 principles (378), HelpSteer (100) and HelpSteer2
(100). RubriX is far ahead (Figure 2): 75.4% exact accuracy against
57.1% for Jev and 49.8% for the Qwen3-8B judge, and 97.6% within one
level against 89.6% and 88.4%. These rubrics come from the same sources
as the training data, so this result measures generalisation to new
criteria of a familiar kind.

Figure 2: Held-out test set: 6,749 judgments on rubrics whose criteria
and level descriptions never appear in training.

### 5.2 Human-labelled benchmarks: accuracy

Figures 3 and 4 report accuracy on the benchmarks. Jev has the highest
exact accuracy on six of the eight sets (HealthBench, BiGGen, SummEval,
JudgmentBench binary and both QAGS subsets), but RubriX is close behind
on most of them: by 1.7 points on QAGS-CNN, 3.0 on QAGS-XSUM, 4.8 on
HealthBench, 5.2 on BiGGen and 1.2 on ELLIPSE, where the Qwen3-8B judge
leads. The larger gaps are SummEval (11.6 points) and JudgmentBench
binary (14.1 points). The Qwen3-8B judge is the most accurate on ELLIPSE
and JudgmentBench multi-level, where RubriX is ahead of Jev. RubriX is
close to Jev on QAGS (80.4% against 82.1% on CNN, 75.7% against 78.7% on
XSUM) and has the best within-one accuracy on JudgmentBench multi-level
(96.4%), where Jev reaches 42.4%.

### 5.3 Human-labelled benchmarks: ranking correlation

These correlations answer a different question from accuracy: given
several responses to the same input, does the judge put them in the same
order as people do, even when it does not land on the exact level?
Table 3 reports Spearman $`\rho`$ and Kendall $`\tau`$ per category. Jev
has the highest average correlation on five of the six benchmarks; on
BiGGen the Qwen3-8B judge is level with it (0.527 against 0.526 in
$`\rho`$).

Against G-Eval, on the two benchmarks it reports:

- •
  _SummEval_: RubriX averages $`\rho=0.483`$, above G-Eval-3.5 (0.401)
  and below G-Eval-4 (0.514); on coherence it matches G-Eval-4 (0.581
  against 0.582). Jev averages 0.541, above G-Eval-4, and is best on
  three of the four dimensions; G-Eval-4 is best on relevance.
- •
  _QAGS_: RubriX has the highest Spearman correlation on QAGS-CNN
  (0.719, against 0.715 for Jev and 0.685 for G-Eval-4), but is lower on
  QAGS-XSUM (0.521, against 0.537 for G-Eval-4 and 0.653 for Jev), whose
  one-sentence summaries are more abstractive. Averaged over both
  subsets, RubriX (0.620) is level with G-Eval-4 (0.611) and the
  Qwen3-8B judge (0.616), and Jev (0.684) is ahead.

RubriX is above G-Eval-3.5 in every SummEval dimension and both QAGS
subsets, in both $`\rho`$ and $`\tau`$.

On ELLIPSE and HealthBench, RubriX ranks second, between Jev and the
Qwen3-8B judge. On BiGGen, where each group holds only about four
responses to one instance, it is third. On JudgmentBench, every judge is
close to zero ($`\rho\leq 0.068`$ on average): ordering the answers to
the same legal task by their checklist and error items is not something
any of these judges does reliably.

### 5.4 Latency and rubric size

A judge that reads the rubric inside its input pays for every level on
every judgment. RubriX embeds each level description once per rubric, so
a judgment on a rubric it has seen needs only the input embedded. We
measured latency one request at a time, on dummy text so that token
counts are exact: an 800-token response, a 30-token criterion, and level
texts of a fixed length, with 110 judgments per setting (30 for the
cache miss at 50 and 100 levels, whose rubrics hold 75,000 and 150,000
tokens) and a different response for every judgment. Two settings were
varied: the number of levels, from 2 to 100 levels of 1,500 tokens each,
and the length of each level, from 10 to 3,000 tokens with 5 levels.
RubriX (the evaluated model’s weights, adapters merged) and the Qwen3-8B
judge ran on one H100 80GB with vLLM, with vLLM’s prefix cache off for
both; Jev was called through its API.

Figures 5 and 6 show the result. With its level embeddings cached,
RubriX takes 27.5–28.3 ms per judgment in every setting, up to a
100-level rubric of 150,000 tokens. The Qwen3-8B judge grows from
108.5 ms at 2 levels to 1,241.3 ms at 20 levels of 1,500 tokens, the
most its 32k context can hold, and from 31.7 ms to 516.2 ms as levels
grow from 10 to 3,000 tokens. Jev grows from 73.1 ms to 199.7 ms between
2 and 10 levels, the maximum its score questions accept, and from
44.0 ms to 210.9 ms with level length. A cache miss, when RubriX meets a
new rubric and embeds its levels in the same request, costs about as
much as the Qwen3-8B judge: 108.2 ms at 2 levels, 4,090.2 ms for a
100-level, 150,000-token rubric, and 422.6 ms at 5 levels of 3,000
tokens. It happens once per rubric.

#### Training cost.

RubriX trained 62.5M parameters (43.6M adapter and 18.9M head
parameters) for one epoch in 2.8 hours on one H100. At inference, each
judgment needs one encoder forward pass for its input (the conversation,
the response and the criterion). Each level description is embedded in
its own pass only once per rubric, and the vectors are reused for every
response judged on that rubric; no text is generated.

## 6 Limitations

- •
  Separate encoding. The input and the level descriptions are encoded
  separately and meet only at the final similarity, so the model never
  reads a response alongside a level description. This limits verdicts
  that depend on checking a specific fact against the rubric.
- •
  Model capacity. RubriX uses an 8B encoder with small projection heads
  (18.9M parameters), which likely limits how far it generalises beyond
  the rubric styles it was trained on; we have not trained larger
  encoders to test this. Continuing training for a second epoch, from
  the final model on a partly refreshed data mix, raised validation
  log-loss at every check, so training stopped early and the first-epoch
  model was kept: with this encoder and head size, more training on this
  data does not help.
- •
  Dependence on fine-tuning data. RubriX responds strongly to
  fine-tuning: on held-out rubrics from its training sources it leads
  Jev by 18.3 percentage points in exact accuracy, but on human-labelled
  benchmarks of other formats it trails Jev on most sets. It is
  therefore most useful when it can be fine-tuned on rubrics like the
  ones it will be applied to; on unfamiliar rubric styles, without such
  data, a general-purpose judge such as Jev is stronger.

## 7 Future Work

- •
  _Larger encoders._ Training RubriX on bigger base models, to measure
  how much of the gap to Jev on unfamiliar rubric styles is a matter of
  model size.
- •
  _More training data._ Scaling the training data, in size and in the
  variety of criteria and formats, and analysing how accuracy and
  ranking on held-out rubrics and human-labelled benchmarks change with
  it.
- •
  _Domain-specific fine-tuning._ Fine-tuning on data from a particular
  use case and comparing how the model then performs on that domain’s
  rubrics against general rubrics.
- •
  _Rubrics with hundreds of levels._ Fine-tuning for scoring tasks that
  need very many levels. RubriX’s cost does not grow with the number of
  levels, while an LLM judge must fit every level description in its
  context, which at this scale needs long-context models, for example
  with extended rotary position embeddings (RoPE) \[24\]; we will
  compare the two in accuracy and efficiency.

## 8 Conclusion

RubriX, whose architecture is inspired by contrastive language models,
scores rubrics with a LoRA-adapted encoder, returning a probability for
every level without generation. On held-out rubrics it is well ahead of
Jev and a Qwen3-8B judge. On human-labelled benchmarks it is
competitive: it is within 6 percentage points of Jev’s accuracy on most
sets, Jev keeps a larger lead on SummEval and JudgmentBench binary and
ranks best on average, while RubriX leads on QAGS-CNN and matches G-Eval
with GPT-4 on SummEval coherence. Its clearest advantage is cost: with
cached level embeddings its latency does not depend on the size of the
rubric, so long, many-level rubrics cost no more to apply than short
ones. RubriX is therefore best suited to settings where a few labelled
examples from the target domain are available to fine-tune on: trained
with at most 25 examples per criterion, it beats Jev and the Qwen3-8B
judge by a wide margin on held-out rubrics from the same sources (75.4%
exact accuracy against 57.1% and 49.8%), its fine-tuning took 2.8 hours
on a single H100, and each judgment then costs one encoder pass.
