###### Abstract

Statement autoformalization plays a crucial role in formal mathematical
reasoning by enabling the automatic translation of natural language
statements into formal languages. While recent advances using large
language models (LLMs) have shown promising capabilities in
autoformalization, methods for automatically evaluating
autoformalization remain underexplored. LLM-as-a-judge presents a
promising approach for automating such evaluation, however, existing
methods typically employ coarse-grained and generic evaluation criteria,
which limit their effectiveness for advanced formal mathematical
reasoning, where quality hinges on nuanced, multi-granular dimensions.
In this work, we take a step toward addressing this gap by introducing a
systematic, automatic method to evaluate autoformalization tasks. The
proposed method is based on an epistemically and formally grounded
ensemble (EFG) of LLM judges, defined on criteria encompassing logical
preservation (LP), mathematical consistency (MC), formal quality (FQ),
and formal validity (FV), resulting in a transparent assessment that
accounts for different contributing factors. We validate the proposed
framework to serve as a proxy for autoformalization assessment within
the domain of formal mathematics. Overall, our experiments demonstrate
that the EFG ensemble of LLM judges is a more suitable emerging proxy
for evaluation than a coarse-grained model. These findings suggest that
LLM judges, especially when guided by a well-defined set of atomic
properties, could offer a scalable, interpretable, and reliable support
for evaluating formal mathematical reasoning.¹¹ 1 Code and datasets are

## 1 Introduction

With the increasing capabilities of large language models (LLMs),
reasoning in formal mathematics has received growing attention in recent
years (Jiang et al., 2023b; Xin et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2025; Ahuja et
al., 2025; Poesia et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024).
Autoformalization, which serves as a bridge between natural language and
formal language domains, plays a critical role in improving
accessibility to formal verification tools for formal reasoning (Pan et
al., 2023; Quan et al., 2025; Quan et al., 2024a). Despite recent
advances in autoformalization with LLMs (Wu et al., 2022; Zhang et al.,
2024), its evaluation remains a long-standing challenge. While syntactic
correctness (Zhang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025a; Zhang et al.,
2025b) can be rigorously verified using theorem provers (TPs) such as
Isabelle (Paulson, 2000) or Lean (de Moura et al., 2015), assessing
semantic alignment or consistency (Zhang et al., 2024) between natural
and formal language statements is still a significant open problem.

While human experts can provide direct assessments, formalizations in
highly specialized domains are extremely time-consuming to evaluate and
prone to errors. Human evaluation becomes particularly challenging when
dealing with increasingly complex and novel mathematical objects (Zhang
et al., 2025a), where the required background knowledge grows
substantially. This creates a clear scalability bottleneck for
autoformalization evaluation.

On the other hand, although reference-based metrics (Li et al., 2024;
Poiroux et al., 2025b; Liu et al., 2025a) enable automatic evaluation,
they introduce several limitations: (i) they require ground-truth
formalizations to be correct, which has already been identified as an
issue in formal benchmarks such as ProofNet (Poiroux et al., 2025b);
(ii) they rely on models to infer equivalence, which may introduce
errors that are unrelated to the quality of the formalization itself;
and (iii) they depend heavily on a formal execution environment and
thereby are constrained by syntactic correctness, even though
syntactically incorrect code may still be semantically meaningful and
useful for refinement. These limitations highlight the need for
reference-free evaluation methods for autoformalization.

In this paper, we take a step toward addressing this gap by introducing
a systematic reference-free method for evaluating autoformalization for
formal mathematical reasoning. The proposed method is based on an
_epistemically and formally grounded (EFG)_ ensemble²² 2 The ”ensemble”
term used in this paper refers to using the same backend LLM to assess
different aspects, unlike setups with multiple backend LLMs judging the
same aspect. While more diverse models for different aspects could
enhance generalizability, our results confirm that a single-backend
ensemble suffices for reliable evaluation. of LLM Judges (Zheng et al.,
2023), grounded on a taxonomy for multi-dimensional autoformalization
evaluation. We break down the components involved within the context of
autoformalization tasks and organize them into a taxonomy composed of
four core aspects: logical preservation (LP), mathematical consistency
(MC), formal quality (FQ), and formal validity (FV), along with a set of
atomic properties that serve as computable estimators of these features.
We hypothesize that such properties can serve as an effective proxy for
human evaluators in mathematical autoformalization settings.
Specifically, in this framework (Figure 1), LP, MC, and FQ are assessed
by LLM judges, while FV is verified using theorem provers. A linear
evaluation model integrates the estimators into a unified, interpretable
evaluation outcome, enabling scalable, multi-dimensional, and automated
assessment.

To validate the proposed framework, we conduct human evaluations of
rankings on Lean4, using both human-written formalizations from existing
datasets (Zheng et al., 2022; Azerbayev et al., 2023) and LLM-generated
formalizations. We further analyze how LLM judges and human differ in
the extent to which individual dimensions contribute to the final
assessment when evaluating flawed formalizations on both Lean4 and
Isabelle/HOL. Finally, we examine whether an ensemble of LLM judges
(EFG) can reliably produce consistent and less biased overall
evaluations, thereby supporting a fully automated evaluation protocol
that minimizes human involvement while complementing human judgment.

Overall, our experiments demonstrate that fine-grained LLM judges are
generally more effective than coarse-grained LLM judges and achieve
ranking agreement comparable to that of human evaluators. Our
contributions can be summarized as follows:

1.  1.  We introduce an epistemically and formally grounded (EFG) taxonomy
        for formal mathematical reasoning, encompassing key dimensions such
        as logical preservation, mathematical consistency, formal quality,
        and formal validity. These dimensions are operationalized through a
        set of interpretable atomic properties serving as estimators.
2.  2.  We develop a fine-grained and interpretable framework for automatic
        evaluation of autoformalization based on this taxonomy, which
        aggregates outputs from LLM judges specialized in the defined atomic
        properties via a linear ensemble.
3.  3.  We investigate the impact of coarse-grained versus fine-grained
        evaluation designs using human evaluation. Our findings show that
        smaller LLMs guided by EFG criteria can outperform larger LLMs
        relying on coarse-grained judgments.
4.  4.  We empirically validate the effectiveness of EFG ensembles for
        autoformalization evaluation. Results show strong agreement with
        human rankings, no evidence of cross-model-family bias, and
        robustness to sampling variability, indicating that EFG ensembles
        serve as reliable proxies for evaluating formalization quality.

## 2 Methodology

### 2.1 Formalization Evaluation

Mathematical autoformalization is the task of automatically transforming
an informal mathematical statement $`s`$, expressed in natural language
and LaTeX symbols from domain $`\mathcal{S}`$, into a formal
representation $`\phi`$ within a formal language $`\mathcal{F}`$ (Zhang
et al., 2024). Formalization evaluation refers to the systematic
assessment of the correctness of a candidate formalization (produced
either by a human or a model) and the assignment of an evaluation
function (either a score or a binary function). The conventional
evaluation approach relies on human judgment of correctness, often based
on syntactic validity within a theorem prover, and typically results in
a binary score (e.g. pass). However, such binary assessment treats all
incorrect formalizations equally and fails to convey a more detailed
qualitative feedback.

Case studies (Wu et al., 2022) of autoformalizations produced by a
particular model on a specific dataset can provide more nuanced
insights, but they require human effort and tend to be tied to the
particular combination of dataset, model, and formal language, limiting
their generalizability to other settings. To this end, we aim to obtain
an autoformalization evaluation function that has a twofold purpose: (i)
serving as an automated proxy for human evaluators and (ii) providing a
qualitative description around a set of formalization properties, while
being agnostic towards any particular solver.

To illustrate the broader evaluation challenge, we present examples from
human-validated, ground-truth formalizations in commonly used datasets
in Table 1. In Case 1, the original natural language statement
explicitly asks for the minimum value, but the formalization merely
asserts a lower bound for the expression, omitting the critical
information that the minimum is actually attained for some value of
$`x`$. This highlights a failure to preserve the underlying logical
intent of the natural language. In Case 2, the formalization omits a
negation on the right-hand side of an equality, contradicting the
natural language statement and revealing a semantic inconsistency in the
mathematical content. In Case 3, while the formalization is semantically
correct, it introduces unnecessary variables and hypotheses, increasing
complexity and redundancy, an undesirable property for concise and
readable formalizations. Motivated by these observations, we propose a
fine-grained taxonomy for formalization evaluation that systematically
qualifies a set of target properties that should be satisfied.

### 2.2 A Taxonomy for Formalization Evaluation

We start by introducing a taxonomy which elicits the constituting parts
entailed by a formalization evaluation framing a set of well-defined
computable properties (Figure 1):

Tier 1. A correct candidate formalization $`\phi`$ of a natural language
statement $`s`$ should satisfy two key properties:

- •
  Alignment Faithfulness (AF): As a representation of $`s`$ in the
  formal domain, $`\phi`$ must accurately capture the intended semantics
  of $`s`$ from the natural language domain.
- •
  Formalization Correctness (FC): As a construct within the formal
  domain, $`\phi`$ must be valid, natural and well-formed within the
  conventions of the target formalism.

Tier 2 for AF. To ensure that a formalization $`\phi`$ faithfully
represents a source statement $`s`$, it is essential that $`\phi`$
maintains the logical structure and mathematical intent inherent in
$`s`$. We introduce two conceptual properties to characterize this
alignment:

- •
  Logical Preservation (LP) captures the degree to which the logical
  content and inferential structure of $`s`$ is preserved within
  $`\phi`$.
- •
  Mathematical Consistency (MC) reflects how coherently and accurately
  the mathematical objects and operations present in $`s`$ are
  represented in $`\phi`$.

Tier 2 for FC. To clarify the intrinsic properties, which can be
assessed independently of any source statement $`s`$, of a formalization
$`\phi`$, we present the following conceptual properties:

- •
  Formal Quality (FQ) captures the structural clarity and conciseness of
  $`\phi`$ (e.g. non-redundant statements).
- •
  Formal Validity (FV) refers to whether $`\phi`$ is syntactically valid
  within the target formal system. A formally valid expression conforms
  to all grammatical and structural rules of the formal language.

Tier 3 - Operable Atomic Properties (OAPs). The properties previously
articulated are then materialized into a set of Operable Atomic
Properties (OAPs), which are functions designed to be both interpretable
and computable. We define a total of 9 such properties, each capturing a
distinct and measurable aspect of a candidate formalization: Pre-arg
Structure, Quantification, Formula, Relation, Concept, Constant,
Operator, Conciseness, and Logical Consistency. The detailed description
of each OAP is provided in Appendix B.

An evaluation metric for autoformalization can be constructed as a
linear combination of Tier-2 aspects:

```math
S(s,\phi)=w_{\mathrm{LP}}S_{\mathrm{LP}}(s,\phi)+w_{\mathrm{MC}}S_{\mathrm{MC}}(s,\phi)\\
+w_{\mathrm{FQ}}S_{\mathrm{FQ}}(\phi)+w_{\mathrm{FV}}S_{\mathrm{FV}}(\phi)
```

where
$`S_{\mathrm{LP}},S_{\mathrm{MC}},S_{\mathrm{FQ}},S_{\mathrm{FV}}\in[0,1]`$,
and the weights
$`w_{\mathrm{LP}},w_{\mathrm{MC}},w_{\mathrm{FQ}},w_{\mathrm{FV}}>0`$
satisfy the constraint
$`w_{\mathrm{LP}}+w_{\mathrm{MC}}+w_{\mathrm{FQ}}+w_{\mathrm{FV}}=1`$.

## 3 Empirical Validation

We first evaluate how well LLM judges with different settings can rank
formalizations compared with human evaluation. To this end, we first
randomly sample 50 natural language statements from the validation split
of miniF2F (Zheng et al., 2022) and another 50 from ProofNet (Azerbayev
et al., 2023). We then ask two human evaluators to independently rank ³³

#### 3.1.1 Results & Discussions

We study five LLMs as judges for autoformalization evaluation:
DeepSeek-Math-7B (Shao et al., 2024), Qwen2.5-Coder-7B (Qwen, 2025),
GPT-4.1-mini, GPT-4.1, and GPT-5.4 (OpenAI, 2025). We consider three
settings: (OA-TP): LLM judges directly provide an overall assessment
score based on the same criteria used in the human evaluation, together
with detailed error feedback from the theorem prover; (DA-T2): LLM
judges directly assess the Tier-2 aspects LP, MC, and FQ, while the
theorem prover determines FV, and the resulting scores are averaged to
obtain a final score between 0 and 1; and (OAP): LLM judges
independently assess the OAPs corresponding to the Tier-2 aspects LP,
MC, and FQ, after which the Tier-2 scores are synthesized through
weighted averaging, with each OAP weighted equally within its
corresponding Tier-2 aspect. FV and the final scores are then obtained
in the same manner as in setting (ii). These scores are used to rank the
formalizations. We compare the results of the LLM judges with those of
the human evaluators and report the results in Table 2.

Fine-grained LLM judges perform consistently better than coarse-grained
judges. In Table 2, across all LLMs, the method of using LLM judges with
OAPs achieves the highest exact match score in 12 cases and the highest
pairwise match score in 17 out of 20 cases compared to the other two
settings. On miniF2F, all settings with the highest individual scores
are achieved via OAPs. On ProofNet, three out of four highest individual
scores are also achieved via OAPs. Together, these results demonstrate
the superiority of the OAP setting and support our claim that using
fine-grained aspects in LLM-based judges for autoformalization
evaluation is more accurate than coarse-grained approaches.

Fine-grained LLM judges can reach the same level of human agreement on
autoformalization evaluation. Comparing results in Table 2 with human
agreement scores, with OAP settings, LLM judges can reach performance
levels that are comparable to human inter-annotator agreement,
particularly in pairwise matching where their scores approach or exceed
the agreement between human annotators on both miniF2F and ProofNet.
This indicates that decomposing evaluation into fine-grained aspects
enables LLM judges to more closely approximate human judgment behavior,
improving consistency and reliability in autoformalization assessment.

### 3.2 Contribution of Evaluation Aspects in LLM and Human Judgments

For a perfect formalization, all evaluation aspects should receive
positive assessments. For formalizations judged to be flawed, however,
it is important to understand how LLM judges and human evaluators differ
in how individual evaluation aspects contribute to the final assessment.
To enable a more detailed comparison between human evaluators and LLM
judges, we focused on natural language statements that were likely to
result in formalization errors, even in human-written formalizations. To
this end, we first used GPT-4.1 to estimate whether the human-written
ground-truth formalizations in the validation datasets correctly
represented their corresponding natural-language statements. The
estimated correctness rates were 76.64% for miniF2F Isabelle/HOL, 82.79%
for miniF2F Lean4, and 64.84% for ProofNet Lean4. Based on these
estimates, we randomly sampled 50, 25, and 25 natural-language
statements from miniF2F Isabelle/HOL, miniF2F Lean4, and ProofNet Lean4,
respectively, whose ground-truth formalizations were identified as
potentially incorrect.

For each selected statement, both the ground-truth formalization and the
GPT-4.1 zero-shot formalization are evaluated by one human expert and
LLM judges. The human expert and LLM judges evaluated the formalizations
with respect to LP, MC, and FQ using binary judgments, together with an
overall assessment on a 5-point scale (criteria in Table 6 in
Appendix A). FV, as verified by the corresponding theorem prover, was
also taken into account during the human evaluation and the overall
assessment from LLM judges.

We acknowledge the potential subjectivity inherent in human evaluation,
particularly regarding the relative importance assigned to different
evaluation aspects. Although a multi-annotator study could provide
additional insights by analyzing aspect contributions separately for
each annotator, conducting such an analysis would substantially increase
the complexity of the evaluation procedure and is beyond the scope of
this work. We therefore employ a single expert evaluator to provide a
consistent and practically reliable reference for comparison. We report
the mean and standard deviation of the human assessments in Table 3. As
expected, the annotated formalizations are not perfect, exhibiting
errors across all Tier-2 evaluation aspects.

#### 3.2.1 Results & Discussions

We calculate Pearson correlation coefficients between Tier-2 aspect
evaluations and overall assessments from LLM judges and the human
evaluator on flawed formalizations identified by the human evaluator.
Results for Lean4 and Isabelle/HOL are reported in Figure 2.

Human and LLM judges exhibit different correlation patterns among Tier-2
aspects when evaluating flawed formalizations. As shown in Figures 2(a),
2(b), and 2(c), LLM judges generally exhibit moderate positive
correlations among the soft aspects, logical preservation (LP),
mathematical consistency (MC), and formal quality (FQ), whereas human
evaluators show weak or slightly negative correlations between these
aspects. This suggests that LLM judges may have difficulty
distinguishing subtle differences between aspects as humans do, leading
to more entangled judgments. In contrast, the hard aspect of formal
validity (FV) shows weak or negative correlations with LP and MC for
both human and LLM judges. This indicates that semantic alignment issues
are largely distinct from syntactic correctness: a formally valid
formalization can still be misaligned with the source mathematics. Both
humans and LLMs appear capable of recognizing this distinction. Human
evaluators also exhibit a positive correlation between FQ and FV,
reflecting the tendency for well-structured formalizations to be both
more readable and more likely to satisfy syntactic requirements. This
relationship is largely absent for LLM judges, suggesting that humans
and LLMs rely on different notions of formal quality when assessing
flawed formalizations.

All Tier-2 aspects contribute to the overall assessment for both human
and LLM judges. All aspects exhibit positive correlations with the
overall assessment, indicating that these dimensions serve as
informative signals for formalization quality. However, the strength of
these relationships, as reflected in the correlation coefficients,
varies across evaluators and formal languages. For Lean4, both
Qwen2.5-Coder-7B and GPT-4.1 assign relatively higher associations to LP
and MC compared to FQ and FV, whereas GPT-4.1-mini and human evaluators
show the strongest association with FQ. For Isabelle/HOL,
Qwen2.5-Coder-7B, GPT-4.1, and human evaluators place the strongest
association on FV, while GPT-4.1-mini exhibits a more uniform pattern
across all aspects. These findings indicate that evaluators do not rely
on a single dominant aspect in a uniform manner but instead integrate
multiple factors with varying degrees of emphasis depending on the
evaluator type and formal language.

## 4 Related Work

### 4.1 Autoformalization

Autoformalization bridges the gap between natural and formal languages,
enabling natural language inputs to be translated into formal
representations. This task has seen promising developments across
various domains, such as natural language inference (Quan et al., 2024a;
Quan et al., 2024b), and automated theorem proving (Jiang et al., 2023b;
Tarrach et al., 2024). LLMs have shown efficacy in Isabelle (Wu et al.,
2022), and retrieval-augmented generation has further enhanced
performance in Isabelle (Zhang et al., 2024) and Lean (Yang et al.,
2023; Liu et al., 2025a). In parallel, Jiang et al. (2024) and Liu et
al. (2025b) developed data generation pipelines for constructing
large-scale parallel corpora of theorem statements. On the evaluation
front, Poiroux et al. (2025a) proposed scalable type-check filtering
with self-consistency, while Lu et al. (2024) trained a model to assess
semantic alignment between informal and formal statements. Despite these
advances, standardized and comprehensive evaluation metrics for
autoformalization remain under-explored. Our work seeks to address this
critical gap.

### 4.2 LLM-as-a-Judge

LLM-as-a-Judge (Zheng et al., 2023) was proposed for evaluating
multi-turn questions and dialogues and has become an increasingly
popular approach for assessing NLP tasks using LLMs. In the domain of
mathematical evaluation, Zhang et al. (2025c) explored the use of LLM
judges for scoring natural language proofs across four dimensions.
However, limited research has been conducted on employing LLM-as-a-Judge
in the field of formal mathematics. Our work takes a step in this
direction.

## 5 Conclusion

In this work, we address the under-explored challenge of evaluating
autoformalization by introducing a fine-grained taxonomy that captures
multiple aspects of a candidate formalization and propose a linear
evaluation model to synthesize core properties into a single overall
assessment score.

Through human evaluation and empirical analysis, we demonstrate that
this automatic evaluation model provides meaningful insights into
formalization performance. Furthermore, we show the feasibility of an
EFG ensemble of LLM judges, both directly and via OAPs. Our experiments
reveal that fine-grained LLM-based judgments align more closely with
human assessments than coarse-grained judgements in terms of ranking.
Lightweight LLMs like GPT-4.1-mini, when guided by OAPs, can serve as
effective and cost-efficient judges.

Our findings contribute new insights and tools for automatic and
fine-grained evaluation of autoformalization systems, paving the way for
more systematic benchmarking and future improvements in this emerging
area. Further directions could focus on fine-tuning smaller open-source
LLMs specialized as judges for autoformalization evaluation and using
LLM explanations of judgments to improve autoformalization performance.

## Limitations

As with other studies involving human annotations, some of our results
may be affected by subjective biases. However, we attempt to limit
subjectivity by leveraging the annotators with background knowledge in
both AI and formal languages, capable of articulating the rationale
behind the annotation’s decisions. Additionally, the scale of
human-annotated samples is relatively small due to the complexity
involved in the annotation. While expanding the annotation would likely
yield more robust and generalizable results, we do not expect it to
alter the main findings. Moreover, the set of operable atomic properties
and LLMs used in our approach is not exhaustive. Although alternative
configurations might yield slightly different results, we believe they
would not substantially impact the core conclusions of the study.

## Appendix B Operable Atomic Properties

With the notation of $`s`$ as the original natural language statement
and $`\phi`$ as a candidate formalization, we define operable atomic
properties as the following:

1.  1.  Pre-arg Structure: $`\phi`$ reflects the inherent predicate-argument
        structure of the statement $`s`$.
2.  2.  Quantification: $`\phi`$ accurately formalizes all quantifiers, such
        as universal and existential, present in $`s`$.
3.  3.  Formula: All mathematical formulas and expressions in $`s`$, such as
        equations and inequalities, are correctly and completely represented
        in $`\phi`$.
4.  4.  Relation: The logical and mathematical relationships between
        propositions in $`s`$ are preserved in $`\phi`$.
5.  5.  Concept: All mathematical concepts mentioned in $`s`$, such as
        integers, fractions, real or complex numbers, derivatives,
        integrals, vectors, matrices, probabilities, expectations, and
        variances, are correctly formalized in $`\phi`$.
6.  6.  Constant: All mathematical constants mentioned in $`s`$, such as 1,
        $`\frac{2}{3}`$, $`\pi`$, $`e`$, are properly included in $`\phi`$.
7.  7.  Operator: All mathematical operators used in $`s`$, such as
        addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, summation,
        exponentiation, and product, are correctly represented in $`\phi`$.
8.  8.  Conciseness: $`\phi`$ is expressed in a minimal, non-redundant form,
        avoiding unnecessary repetition or complexity.
9.  9.  Logical Consistency: $`\phi`$ is internally coherent and contains no
        contradictions under the logical rules of the formal system.
