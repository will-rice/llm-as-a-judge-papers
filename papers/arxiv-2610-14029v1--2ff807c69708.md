# Can We Trust This Evaluation? Instance-Level Reliability of LLM-as-a-Judge

###### Abstract

LLM-as-a-judge evaluation commonly aggregates multiple judges’ decisions
into a majority vote, yet this panel decision process does not guarantee
that the evaluation of an individual item is reliable. We propose
Structured Cross-fitted Uncertainty Risk (SCUR), a label-free approach
for estimating instance-level evaluation reliability based on the judge
panel’s behavior. A latent measurement model serves as the backbone for
measurement system analysis, from which SCUR derives three complementary
signals capturing panel uncertainty, measurement cross-entropy, and
perturbation sensitivity. These three sources of evidence are combined
into a risk score. We evaluate the SCUR approach on five real-world
benchmarks using majority-vote error ranking, selective trust, and risk
stratification. Furthermore, we show our approach to be more effective
at detecting high-consensus risk items, which are the blind spots for
many other LLM-as-a-judge evaluation methods. The code is available at
https://github.com/YangBa78/Evaluation-the-reliability-of-LLM-as-a-Judge.

## 1 Introduction

_LLM-as-a-judge_ evaluation has become a standard approach for assessing
open-ended generation tasks, including dialogue, reasoning,
summarization, coding, instruction following, and safety (Liu et al.,
2023; Lin and Chen, 2023; Li et al., 2025), because it is scalable,
inexpensive, and easy to deploy. However, since the judge is itself a
large language model (LLM), it can be biased, unstable,
prompt-sensitive, and overconfident. A natural solution is to use
multiple LLM judges to form a judge panel (Zheng et al., 2023; Verga et
al., 2024; Chan et al., 2024) and output only the majority-vote
decision. A typical pipeline asks multiple LLMs to select one answer
from two options—option $`A`$ or $`B`$—for a single question or prompt,
then aggregates their votes into a label or score. This raises a
fundamental question: _How can we trust this panel’s decision?_

Prior work has documented the option-display-position bias in LLM
evaluators (i.e., the evaluator prefers a certain position, such as the
first position, so leaning to A, instead of the real contents in these
options (Wang et al., 2024a; Shi et al., 2025)), self- and model-family
preference (i.e., different LLMs from the same foundation model family
tend to vote similarly (Wataoka et al., 2024; Li et al., 2026)), and
broader instability in LLM judgments (Schroeder and Wood-Doughty, 2024;
Li et al., 2025). These concerns cannot be fully answered by simply
aggregating multiple LLM judges’ decisions (Zhang et al., 2023; Chan et
al., 2024). Note that these LLM evaluators may share overlapping
training corpora, similar RLHF preferences, internet writing norms,
safety alignment policies, benchmark contamination, and stylistic
priors. As a result, _multiple LLM judges may agree for the same wrong
reason_. Thus, implicitly treating panel agreement as the evidence of
answer correctness is very problematic (Wang et al., 2024a; Shi et al.,
2025; Li et al., 2026; Schroeder and Wood-Doughty, 2024). It may create
a failure mode which we denote as _“correlation collapse”_: a panel
appears to be highly confident because most LLM judges agree with each
other, but this agreement is driven by the shared judge bias rather than
the underlying answer quality. In such cases, high consensus may produce
false confidence, instead of a trustworthy decision. The existing
LLM-evaluation aggregation methods focus on producing better decisions
by weighting on each judge’s reliability. In contrast, we study a
complementary problem: given a panel’s decision on one particular item,
can we estimate how much this decision can be trusted?

Figure 1: Overview of the proposed SCUR framework. The latent

shared and judge-specific perturbation sensitivities. Each raw signal is
separately normalized using its unlabeled reference ECDF; the two
sensitivity percentiles are then combined by their maximum to obtain
$`z^{S}`$. The available evidence $`z^{U},z^{C},z^{S}`$ is fused into
SCUR for automatic acceptance or human review.

Estimating the reliability of an evaluation panel’s decision requires
more than measuring the degree of panel agreement alone. We introduce a
latent measurement model that employs cross-fitted, judge-level
predictive probabilities under original and perturbation conditions.
From these predictions, the Structured Cross-fitted Uncertainty Risk
(SCUR) score is formulated, which preserves three interpretable
misjudgment mechanisms—the latent uncertainty among judges, the
alignment of predictive vs. native preference probabilities, and the
panel’s sensitivity to perturbation conditions—and combines their
reference percentiles into a label-free risk score via a
complement-based geometric mean.

We evaluate SCUR on five real-world LLM-as-a-judge benchmarks spanning
pairwise preference, objective correctness, and instruction following.
These experiments examine whether the proposed risk score correctly
identifies unreliable panel decisions, improves evaluation reliability
through selective review, and produces meaningful risk stratification
across evaluated instances.

Our primary contributions are as follows:

- •
  We propose SCUR, a label-free approach to estimating decision
  reliability from a LLM-as-a-judge panel, without requiring
  ground-truth labels. It treats uncertainty, misaligned responses, and
  condition sensitivity as potential failure alarms for constructing a
  sensible risk score.
- •
  We introduce an instance-level diagnostic analysis that decomposes the
  estimated risk into interpretable behavioral factors, providing
  insights into why a panel decision is deemed unreliable, then
  subsequently recommending human intervention.
- •
  We conduct extensive experiments on five real-world LLM-as-a-judge
  benchmarks, covering pairwise preference, objective correctness, and
  instruction following, and demonstrate that SCUR consistently
  outperforms existing uncertainty-only baselines in terms of
  majority-vote error ranking, selective trust, and risk stratification.
  Furthermore, we demonstrate that SCUR is effective for identifying
  high-consensus risky items and transferable to other vote aggregation
  rules.

## 2 Related Work

#### LLM-as-a-judge evaluation.

LLM-as-a-judge methods use strong language models to evaluate human- or
machine-generated content through pairwise comparison, scalar scoring,
or rubric-guided critique (Lin and Chen, 2023; Wang et al., 2024b; Kim
et al., 2024). These methods are attractive because they scale beyond
manual annotation, but they are not neutral measurement devices. Prior
work documents several deficiencies including position bias, verbosity
bias, self-enhancement, prompt sensitivity, and other evaluator
artifacts (Zheng et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024a; Shi et al., 2025).
Recent work also studies multi-judge or multi-role evaluator networks as
a way to improve alignment with human judgments (Zhang et al., 2023).
Our work is closest in spirit to these reliability concerns, but shifts
the target from average judge quality to the reliability of each panel
decision on individual instances.

#### Noisy annotators and label aggregation.

The proposed framework is related to a long line of noisy-label
aggregation and crowdsourcing models. Dawid–Skene estimates latent
labels together with annotator confusion matrices from redundant noisy
annotations (Dawid and Skene, 1979). Subsequent methods, including GLAD
(Whitehill et al., 2009), MACE (Hovy et al., 2013), and Bayesian
annotation models (Paun et al., 2018), jointly model annotator
competence, item difficulty, and annotation uncertainty to improve
consensus estimation. These methods are designed to recover a more
accurate latent label or aggregate decision. Recent work has improved
LLM evaluation through confounder-aware panel aggregation (Zhao et al.,
2026), item-response-theoretic modeling of judge behavior (Choi et al.,
2026), and selective confidence estimation for pairwise judging (Badshah
et al., 2026b). In contrast, we assume that a panel decision has already
been produced and address a complementary problem: estimating the
reliability of that individual panel decision without requiring
ground-truth labels.

#### Pairwise preference modeling.

Pairwise evaluation is related to Bradley-Terry, Luce-choice, and
related ranking models (Bradley and Terry, 1952; Luce, 1959). These
models infer latent preference or quality scores from comparisons. In
our setting, the comparison is contaminated by the evaluator bias and
random noise due to the stochastic nature of generative LLMs, so the
observed vote cannot be treated as deterministic. We model the LLM judge
process itself and use the fitted structure to quantify the
trustworthiness of the resulting panel decision.

#### Measurement system reliability.

This work is also related to measurement system analysis, especially
gauge repeatability and reproducibility, which partition the total
variation in measurements into variance components due to items, the
measurement system, and repeated measurements (Automotive Industry
Action Group, 2010). A related line of work studies annotation quality
in crowdsourced data through consistency, annotator credibility,
variance decomposition, and spam diagnostics (Ba et al., 2025). We adapt
this perspective to LLM evaluation by treating items, judges, and
perturbations as sources of measurement variation.

## 3 Methodology

We first introduce a latent measurement model (LMM) to model LLM-judge
decisions under various designed perturbation schemes. We then extract
three failure mechanism-specific risk signals for the judge
panel—decision uncertainty, measurement cross-entropy, and perturbation
sensitivity—and combine their reference percentiles into a risk-ranking
score.

### 3.1 Latent Measurement Model

Suppose there are two options for an LLM judge to choose from for each
piece of item to be evaluated. Each item consists of a prompt, two
candidate answers, and an optional judging rubric, and it is denoted by
$`\mathcal{I}_{i}=(\mathrm{prompt}_{i},A_{i},B_{i},\mathrm{rubric}_{i}).`$
For item $`i`$, judge $`j`$, and perturbation condition
$`c\in\mathcal{C}`$, let $`p^{\mathrm{llm}}_{ijc}`$$`\in(0,1)`$ be the
judge’s response probability that the _canonical_ candidate $`A_{i}`$
wins. $`p^{\mathrm{llm}}_{ijc}`$ is obtained by exponentiating and
normalizing the judge’s verdict-token logprobs, the logarithms of the
probabilities the LLM assigns to the possible verdicts. We reserve
$`c=0`$ for the original condition: the unperturbed answer order,
prompt, and rubric. When $`c\neq 0`$ it denotes one prespecified
intervention, such as swapping the display order of answers or changing
the judging rubric. After an answer-order swap, for example, the
probability is mapped back to the same canonical event “original
$`A_{i}`$ wins,” so probabilities remain comparable between the original
and perturbed conditions.

$`p^{\mathrm{llm}}_{ijc}`$ has been widely used to measure how confident
a large language model is in the verdict token it generates. We employ a
latent measurement model (LMM) to decompose its log-odds, making item,
judge, and condition effects separable:

```math
\operatorname{logit}\!\left(p^{\mathrm{llm}}_{ijc}\right)=a_{j}q_{i}+b_{j}+\delta_{c}+\kappa_{jc}+\epsilon_{ijc},\qquad\epsilon_{ijc}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{j}^{2}). \tag{1}
```

This decomposition distinguishes the item-level preference $`q_{i}`$
from systematic judge and condition effects and residual variation,
allowing individual judge responses to be assessed relative to their
model-implied preferences. The latent variable $`q_{i}`$ represents the
panel’s underlying consensus preference for the canonical candidate,
inferred jointly from all judges’ responses rather than a correctness
oracle. The discrimination $`a_{j}=\exp(\alpha_{j})>0`$ controls how
strongly judge $`j`$ responds to that consensus, as in a two-parameter
item-response model (Lord, 1980); $`b_{j}`$ is this judge’s overall
tendency toward canonical candidate $`A`$. The shared perturbation
effect $`\delta_{c}`$ captures the panel-wide response to intervention
$`c`$, whereas $`\kappa_{jc}`$ captures judge $`j`$’s deviation from
that shared response. We set $`\delta_{0}=\kappa_{j0}=0`$, so the
unperturbed original item serves as a reference.

#### Estimation.

We estimate the LMM mean structure using alternating penalized
least-squares updates with equal observation weights. The quadratic
penalties admit a Gaussian-shrinkage interpretation, shrinking
$`q_{i},b_{j},\delta_{c},\kappa_{jc}`$ toward zero and $`a_{j}`$ toward
one. In particular, shrinking $`\kappa_{jc}`$ toward zero regularizes
each judge’s condition effect toward the shared effect $`\delta_{c}`$.
Judge-specific residual variances are estimated after fitting the mean
structure. Appendix A provides the penalty strengths, scale and
centering conventions, and optimization details.

#### Dataset partition and cross-fitting.

We partition each benchmark by items in a 50/30/20 ratio into
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{fit}}/\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{ref}}/\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{eval}}`$
datasets: $`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{fit}}`$ estimates LMM parameters,
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{ref}}`$ supplies the reference distributions for
risk normalization, and $`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{eval}}`$ is held out for
evaluation. No panel decision-error labels are used for LMM fitting or
risk estimation. For each reference or evaluation item, we freeze the
fitted judge and condition parameters and infer $`q_{i}^{(-j)}`$ from
other judges, excluding judge $`j`$’s responses for that item. The
cross-fitted prediction is
$`\widehat{p}_{ijc}^{(-j)}=\sigma(a_{j}q_{i}^{(-j)}+b_{j}+\delta_{c}+\kappa_{jc})`$,
where $`\sigma`$ is the logistic function; for simplicity, we rewrite it
as $`\widehat{p}_{ijc}`$. Excluding judge $`j`$’s own responses ensures
that this prediction is based on evidence from the remaining panel,
preventing a prediction contamination due to self-response
reinforcement.

### 3.2 Evidence-Based Risk Signals

We define three complementary risk signals: $`U`$ measures ambiguity in
the pooled preference, $`C`$ assesses individual judge responses against
the learned panel preference structure, and $`S`$ measures dependence on
perturbation conditions.

#### Model-based preference uncertainty.

Let $`\overline{p}_{i}=J_{i}^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{J_{i}}\widehat{p}_{ij0}`$,
where $`J_{i}`$ is the number of available judges for item $`i`$, be the
mean cross-fitted preference probability under the original condition.
We define

```math
U_{i}=H(\overline{p}_{i}),\qquad H(p)=-p\log p-(1-p)\log(1-p).
```

$`U_{i}`$ is largest when $`\overline{p}_{i}`$ is close to 0.5, meaning
that the panel does not clearly favor either candidate. This may occur
because individual judges are uncertain or because different judges
confidently favor different candidates. However, it may remain low when
the judges confidently agree and therefore cannot identify errors shared
across the panel.

#### Measurement cross-entropy.

We compare each judge’s LLM confidence $`p^{\mathrm{llm}}_{ij0}`$ with
its cross-fitted LMM prediction $`\widehat{p}_{ij0}`$ under the original
condition using binary cross-entropy:

```math
C_{i}=-\frac{1}{J_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{J_{i}}\left[p^{\mathrm{llm}}_{ij0}\log\widehat{p}_{ij0}+(1-p^{\mathrm{llm}}_{ij0})\log(1-\widehat{p}_{ij0})\right].
```

$`C_{i}`$ increases when a judge’s LLM confidence disagrees with the
cross-fitted prediction based on the remaining panel. The mismatch is
greater when the judge confidently favors one candidate while the
cross-fitted prediction favors the other. Such a mismatch indicates that
the judge’s confidence is not well supported by the preference structure
inferred from the remaining panel, making it informative for assessing
decision risk. Cross-entropy also reflects the uncertainty of the LLM
confidence itself, so $`C_{i}`$ can be high even when the two
probabilities are similar but uncertain. Thus, $`C_{i}`$ reflects both
individual-judge uncertainty and cross-fitted mismatch, complementing
the pooled preference uncertainty measured by $`U_{i}`$.

#### Perturbation sensitivity.

For benchmarks with paired perturbations, where the same item is
evaluated under the original and perturbed conditions, let
$`\mathcal{C}_{i}^{+}=\mathcal{C}_{i}\setminus\{0\}`$ denote the
available perturbed conditions, such as answer-order swaps,
strict-rubric changes, and evidence-first rubric changes. Let
$`\eta_{ij0}=a_{j}q_{i}^{(-j)}+b_{j}`$. Keeping the cross-fitted item
margin fixed, we separately measure the probability changes due to the
shared and judge-specific condition effects:

```math
\displaystyle S_{i}^{\mathrm{shared}} \displaystyle=\frac{1}{J_{i}|\mathcal{C}_{i}^{+}|}\sum_{j=1}^{J_{i}}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}_{i}^{+}}\left|\sigma(\eta_{ij0}+\delta_{c})-\sigma(\eta_{ij0})\right|,
```

```math
\displaystyle S_{i}^{\mathrm{judge}} \displaystyle=\frac{1}{J_{i}|\mathcal{C}_{i}^{+}|}\sum_{j=1}^{J_{i}}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}_{i}^{+}}\left|\sigma(\eta_{ij0}+\delta_{c}+\kappa_{jc})-\sigma(\eta_{ij0}+\delta_{c})\right|.
```

$`S_{i}^{\mathrm{shared}}`$ measures the probability change associated
with a perturbation across the panel, while $`S_{i}^{\mathrm{judge}}`$
measures additional judge-specific changes beyond this shared effect.
These two components capture complementary forms of perturbation
sensitivity and are combined after reference normalization, as described
below. Large changes indicate that a preference depends on the judging
conditions, which can undermine its stability.

### 3.3 Label-Free Risk Score

#### Reference normalization.

We use $`r_{i}^{k}`$ for raw signal $`k`$ and $`z_{i}^{k}`$ for its
normalized evidence. Specifically, let $`r_{i}^{U}=U_{i}`$,
$`r_{i}^{C}=C_{i}`$,
$`r_{i}^{S,\mathrm{shared}}=S_{i}^{\mathrm{shared}}`$, and
$`r_{i}^{S,\mathrm{judge}}=S_{i}^{\mathrm{judge}}`$. For each available
raw signal $`k`$, we estimate an empirical cumulative distribution
function (ECDF) (Wasserman, 2004) on the unlabeled reference split:

```math
z_{i}^{k}=\widehat{F}_{k,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{ref}}}(r_{i}^{k})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{ref}}^{k}|}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{ref}}^{k}}\mathbf{1}\!\left\{r_{h}^{k}\leq r_{i}^{k}\right\},
```

where $`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{ref}}^{k}`$ contains reference items with
risk signal $`k`$. The ECDF is estimated without golden (true) labels,
and it is then frozen for evaluation. The transformation from
$`r_{i}^{k}`$ to $`z_{i}^{k}`$ maps heterogeneous raw signal scales to
comparable percentiles. For perturbation sensitivity, we then combine
its two components as

```math
z_{i}^{S}=\max\left\{z_{i}^{S,\mathrm{shared}},z_{i}^{S,\mathrm{judge}}\right\}.
```

Taking the maximum after normalization retains whichever form of
perturbation sensitivity is more unusual relative to its reference
distribution. Therefore, the normalized evidence captures three
complementary aspects of decision risk: preference ambiguity
($`z_{i}^{U}`$), individual-judge uncertainty and mismatch
($`z_{i}^{C}`$), and perturbation sensitivity ($`z_{i}^{S}`$).

#### Structured Cross-fitted Uncertainty Risk.

Let $`\mathcal{K}_{i}`$ contain the available channels: $`\{U,C,S\}`$
with paired perturbations and $`\{U,C\}`$ otherwise. We construct
_Structured Cross-fitted Uncertainty Risk_ ($`\mathrm{SCUR}`$) as the
complement of the geometric mean of the complements of normalized
evidence:

```math
\mathrm{SCUR}_{i}=1-\left[\prod_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{i}}(1-z_{i}^{k})\right]^{1/|\mathcal{K}_{i}|}. \tag{2}
```

Specifically,
$`\mathrm{SCUR}_{i}=1-[(1-z_{i}^{U})(1-z_{i}^{C})(1-z_{i}^{S})]^{1/3}`$.
The complement-based geometric mean aggregates the available risk
evidence: a high percentile for any evidence type reduces its complement
and increases the resulting SCUR score. The $`|\mathcal{K}_{i}|`$-th
root accounts for the number of available evidence types, allowing the
same aggregation rule when perturbation sensitivity is unavailable. The
resulting $`\mathrm{SCUR}_{i}\in[0,1]`$ provides a relative risk score,
which will be used to rank items for automatic acceptance or human
review.

#### Benchmarks.

We validate the proposed reliability framework on five
multi-LLM-as-a-judge benchmarks: Chatbot-Arena (Zheng et al., 2023),
HaluEval (Li et al., 2023), MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021), LLMBar (Zeng
et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2024), and LLMEval² (Zhang et al., 2023; Gao
et al., 2024; Zeng et al., 2024). They cover pairwise preference,
hallucination detection, multiple-choice correctness, instruction
following, and multidimensional prompt-following. We use a 50/30/20 data
split: 50% fits the LMM, 30% supplies the reference distributions for
SCUR, and 20% is untouched test data. We run 5 independent trials.

Chatbot-Arena, HaluEval, and MMLU use existing judge outputs.
Chatbot-Arena includes original and swapped answer orders; HaluEval and
MMLU contain only the original condition. For LLMBar and LLMEval², we
collect verdict-token log-probabilities from five judge models, crossing
original/swapped answer order with standard, strict, and evidence-first
rubrics to obtain six conditions per item–judge pair. Thus
Chatbot-Arena, LLMBar, and LLMEval² provide $`U,C,S`$, whereas HaluEval
and MMLU provide only $`U,C`$. See Appendix C.2 for the
benchmark-specific conditions and judging protocol.

#### Risk baselines.

We compare SCUR with four panel-level uncertainty baselines: vote
entropy (VE), which is the binary entropy of the majority-vote rate
related to vote-agreement confidence used for LLM judges (Radharapu et
al., 2026); predictive entropy (PE), which is the entropy of the
panel-mean probability, extending predictive entropy used for LLM-judge
abstention (Badshah et al., 2026a); DS entropy, which is the posterior
entropy of the Dawid-Skene model, adapting its LLM-evaluator aggregation
applications (Gao et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2026) to risk scoring; and
mean per-judge MSP uncertainty (Hendrycks and Gimpel, 2017; Radharapu et
al., 2026). DS entropy ranks items by uncertainty; DS aggregation,
discussed later in this section, instead determines their verdicts.
Appendix B specifies the formulas and the panel adaptations of these
prior studies.

#### Evaluation metrics.

Our primary target is the error made by majority voting verdict,
$`e_{i}^{\mathrm{MV}}=\mathbf{1}\{\widehat{y}_{i}^{\mathrm{MV}}\neq y_{i}^{\mathrm{gold}}\}`$.
AUROC measures how well risk separates incorrect from correct verdicts;
we report $`1-\mathrm{AURC}`$, the complement of area under the
risk–coverage curve (Geifman et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2025),
equivalently the mean retained accuracy over all coverage levels
$`k/n`$, $`k=1,\ldots,n`$ (higher is better); and Err@50 is the error
rate among the lowest-risk half of evaluation items. We also examine
error by increasing risk quintile and across retained coverage. For an
alternative aggregation rule $`g`$ (Section 4.3), we recompute the error
as
$`e_{i}^{g}=\mathbf{1}\{\widehat{y}_{i}^{g}\neq y_{i}^{\mathrm{gold}}\}`$,
while using the same SCUR scores to rank the items.

#### Majority-vote error ranking.

Table 1 shows that SCUR has the highest mean AUROC and
$`1-\mathrm{AURC}`$ and the lowest mean Err@50 on each benchmark.
Figure 2 summarizes the equally weighted benchmark averages. Compared
with PE, the best baseline on average, AUROC increases from .673 to
.702, $`1-\mathrm{AURC}`$ from .818 to .841, and Err@50 decreases from
.174 to .147. The improvement in AUROC shows that SCUR better separates
incorrect from correct panel decisions. The lower Err@50 gives this
ranking gain an operational meaning: at the same 50% acceptance budget,
the retained set contains fewer errors. The $`1-\mathrm{AURC}`$
improvement shows that this advantage persists across coverage levels
rather than at a single cutoff. Overall, SCUR provides a more effective
ranking of panel-error risk than the uncertainty baselines.

#### Selective trust and risk strata.

Figure 3 examines how observed error changes with SCUR risk and retained
coverage. On Chatbot-Arena, HaluEval, and MMLU, error increases
monotonically from the lowest to the highest SCUR quintile, showing that
higher SCUR scores correspond to higher empirical error. LLMEval² shows
the same monotonic pattern, while LLMBar shows some variation among the
lower-risk quintiles but substantially higher error in the two
highest-risk groups.

Across the coverage curves, SCUR generally achieves lower error at the
same coverage than the baselines, particularly at lower coverage. On
LLMBar and LLMEval², which have substantially fewer evaluation items
than the other benchmarks, estimates are more variable and the baselines
show weak or inconsistent ordering across risk quintiles. Despite this
increased variability, SCUR retains a clearer separation between lower-
and higher-risk items.

### 4.2 High-Consensus Blind Spots

Figure 4: Performance on high-consensus items. AUROC on items with at
least 80% vote agreement. Points show five-seed means and whiskers
indicate $`\pm 1`$ standard deviation. The dashed line marks
chance-level AUROC.

High-consensus (HC) errors are challenging cases for any
disagreement-based risk measurement, because incorrect verdicts can
remain hidden when most judges agree on this incorrect choice. We
therefore evaluate items for which at least 80% of judges choose the
same answer under the original condition, using each judge’s binary A/B
choice. This defines the high-consensus (HC) subset; gold labels are
used only to determine whether the majority verdict is correct. We ask
whether SCUR can still distinguish incorrect from correct verdicts by
marking them with high risk.

Figure 4 shows that SCUR achieves the highest mean AUROC on the HC
subset across all five benchmarks. The advantage is particularly clear
on the larger benchmarks, while estimates are more variable on LLMBar
and LLMEval². These results show that SCUR can identify risk differences
even among high-consensus decisions, where other methods struggle to
separate incorrect verdicts from correct verdicts.

The matched-vote analysis in Appendix D provides a stricter test by
comparing items with identical original-condition vote patterns across
judges (e.g., a 4-to-1 vote is compared only with other 4-to-1 votes in
which the same four judges support the same candidate). The appendix
also considers an alternative definition of high consensus based on
panel-mean probabilities, selecting items with a panel-mean confidence
of at least .8. Across both settings, SCUR outperforms the baselines in
separating incorrect from correct verdicts, indicating that SCUR
consistently identifies panel errors that remain difficult to detect
under high consensus.

### 4.3 Risk Generalizability

SCUR assesses the reliability of panel decisions independently of how
judge evaluations are aggregated, allowing it to be applied to different
aggregation rules. We evaluate this setting with Dawid–Skene (DS), MACE,
GLAD, and CARE (Zhao et al., 2026) in addition to majority vote. Each
method produces its own verdict and error labels.

Figure 5 shows that retaining lower-risk decisions according to SCUR
improves accuracy across all five benchmarks and five aggregation rules,
with an average gain of 4.6 pp at 80% lowest-risk threshold and 12.2 pp
at 50% threshold, compared with accepting all panel decisions. These
results show that SCUR is robust to different aggregation methods;
therefore, it can serve as a common risk-assessment metric for either
accepting the panel decision or moving the case to human review.

## 5 Conclusion

SCUR is a label-free risk score developed for assessing individual LLM
judge-panel decisions. A cross-fitted latent measurement model provides
three risk signals: preference uncertainty, measurement cross-entropy,
and perturbation sensitivity. Across five benchmarks, SCUR improves
error ranking and selective acceptance, identifies errors that remain
difficult to detect under high panel consensus, and is robust to
different aggregation rules. These signals further provide interpretable
evidence for why individual decisions may warrant human review.
Together, our results show that agreement alone does not guarantee
decision reliability and that SCUR provides a valuable tool for
prioritizing decisions for automatic acceptance or human review.

## Ethics statement

SCUR is intended to support, rather than replace, human judgment. A low
SCUR score should not be interpreted as a guarantee of correctness,
particularly when multiple LLM judges share systematic errors or biases.
The framework assesses relative decision risk rather than establishing
factual correctness. In high-stakes settings, its scores should be used
as evidence for prioritizing human review rather than as a basis for
fully automated decisions.

## Appendix A Model Structure and Estimation

This appendix specifies the latent measurement model and the estimator
used for the reported experiments. The latent item margin describes a
preference shared by the judge panel. Truth labels are used only in
evaluation.

### A.1 Measurement Model and Parameter Interpretation

The measurement model in Equation 1 is

```math
\operatorname{logit}(p^{\mathrm{llm}}_{ijc})=a_{j}q_{i}+b_{j}+\delta_{c}+\kappa_{jc}+\epsilon_{ijc},\qquad a_{j}>0,\qquad\epsilon_{ijc}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{j}^{2}).
```

The model is fitted to log-odds. Judge verdicts are retained for
majority voting and the vote-based baselines. Each non-original
condition is a categorical intervention; Appendix C.2 lists the
conditions available for each benchmark.

|                 |                                                                                      |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Parameter       | Interpretation                                                                       |
| $`q_{i}`$       | Direction and strength of the shared item preference.                                |
| $`a_{j}`$       | Positive discrimination: how strongly judge $`j`$ responds to $`q_{i}`$.             |
| $`b_{j}`$       | Judge-specific tendency toward canonical candidate $`A`$.                            |
| $`\delta_{c}`$  | Shared shift under condition $`c`$, relative to the original condition.              |
| $`\kappa_{jc}`$ | Judge-specific deviation from the shared condition shift.                            |
| $`\sigma_{j}`$  | Residual log-odds scale for judge $`j`$, estimated after fitting the mean structure. |

Table 3: Parameters in the fitted continuous-response measurement model.

When only original-condition responses are available, $`\delta_{c}`$ and
$`\kappa_{jc}`$ are absent and the model reduces to
$`\operatorname{logit}(p^{\mathrm{llm}}_{ij0})=a_{j}q_{i}+b_{j}+\epsilon_{ij0}`$.

### A.2 Shrinkage and Coordinate Conventions

The released estimator uses ridge least-squares updates for the mean
structure. Its blockwise quadratic penalties are

```math
\begin{split}\mathcal{R}={}&\frac{\lambda_{q}}{2}\sum_{i}q_{i}^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{a}}{2}\sum_{j}(a_{j}-1)^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{b}}{2}\sum_{j}b_{j}^{2}\\
&+\frac{\lambda_{\delta}}{2}\sum_{c\neq 0}\delta_{c}^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\kappa}}{2}\sum_{j}\sum_{c\neq 0}\kappa_{jc}^{2}.\end{split}
```

The strengths are $`\lambda_{q}=\lambda_{a}=\lambda_{b}=1`$,
$`\lambda_{\delta}=5`$, and $`\lambda_{\kappa}=10`$. These penalties
correspond to Gaussian shrinkage toward zero for $`q,b,\delta,\kappa`$
and toward one for $`a`$, with positivity enforced for $`a`$. The model
separates shared item and condition components from judge-specific
responses and condition deviations.

The original condition is fixed by $`\delta_{0}=\kappa_{j0}=0`$. To fix
the multiplicative scale, after each discrimination update the
implementation sets $`g=\exp[J^{-1}\sum_{j}\log a_{j}]`$ and replaces
$`a_{j}\leftarrow a_{j}/g`$ and $`q_{i}\leftarrow gq_{i}`$, preserving
$`a_{j}q_{i}`$. For each non-original condition, it transfers the mean
of $`\kappa_{\cdot c}`$ into $`\delta_{c}`$, so that
$`\sum_{j}\kappa_{jc}=0`$ while preserving the total condition effect.

### A.3 Optimization and Held-Out Item Inference

Let $`m_{ijc}=\operatorname{logit}(p^{\mathrm{llm}}_{ijc})`$. Each
update regresses the residual left by the other blocks on the current
block. For example, with $`\mathcal{O}_{i}`$ denoting the observed
judge–condition cells for a fit item,

```math
q_{i}\leftarrow\frac{\sum_{(j,c)\in\mathcal{O}_{i}}a_{j}(m_{ijc}-b_{j}-\delta_{c}-\kappa_{jc})}{\lambda_{q}+\sum_{(j,c)\in\mathcal{O}_{i}}a_{j}^{2}}.
```

Before scale normalization, each discrimination update is clipped to
$`[0.05,20]`$. The mean-structure fit uses equal observation weights.
After fitting, the residual scale is estimated as

```math
\widehat{\sigma}_{j}^{2}=\frac{1}{n_{j}}\sum_{(i,c)\in\mathcal{O}_{j}}(m_{ijc}-\widehat{a}_{j}\widehat{q}_{i}-\widehat{b}_{j}-\widehat{\delta}_{c}-\widehat{\kappa}_{jc})^{2}+10^{-9}.
```

These residual estimates do not reweight the fitted mean structure. Here
$`\mathcal{O}_{j}`$ contains the observed item–condition cells for judge
$`j`$, and $`n_{j}=|\mathcal{O}_{j}|`$.

We use three random restarts, a nominal 700 iterations, and an automatic
extension up to 2,100 iterations. The standard stopping rule requires
the root mean square change in predicted margins to be below
$`10^{-6}`$, the maximum change below $`10^{-5}`$, and the relative
change in the objective below $`10^{-7}`$ for three successive
iterations. Among converged restarts, the one with the smallest fit
objective is retained. The criterion is mean squared residual plus the
implementation’s item- and judge-bias ridge diagnostic; it is used for
convergence and restart selection.

For each seed, items are randomly partitioned into fit, reference, and
evaluation splits in a 50/30/20 ratio. Only fit items estimate the judge
and perturbation condition parameters. For a reference or evaluation
item, the leave-one-judge-out margin is obtained using all available
conditions from judges other than $`j`$:

```math
q_{i}^{(-j)}=\frac{\sum_{(\ell,c)\in\mathcal{O}_{i}:\ell\neq j}\widehat{a}_{\ell}(m_{i\ell c}-\widehat{b}_{\ell}-\widehat{\delta}_{c}-\widehat{\kappa}_{\ell c})}{\lambda_{q}+\sum_{(\ell,c)\in\mathcal{O}_{i}:\ell\neq j}\widehat{a}_{\ell}^{2}}.
```

The corresponding held-out prediction is
$`\widehat{p}_{ijc}=\sigma(\widehat{a}_{j}q_{i}^{(-j)}+\widehat{b}_{j}+\widehat{\delta}_{c}+\widehat{\kappa}_{jc})`$.
Excluding every condition of the held-out judge prevents its own
responses from determining the item margin used to assess them.
Reference items supply the signal ECDFs, which are frozen before
evaluation. Neither model fitting nor reference normalization uses
ground-truth labels. For numerical stability, the score implementation
floors each complement $`1-z_{i}^{k}`$ at $`10^{-12}`$. A sensitivity
component that is absent or constant on the reference split is omitted;
the maximum uses the remaining sensitivity component, if any.

## Appendix B Risk Baselines

We compare SCUR with vote entropy (VE), predictive entropy (PE),
Dawid–Skene posterior entropy (DS entropy), and mean per-judge MSP
uncertainty. All four use original-condition responses ($`c=0`$), with
larger scores indicating greater uncertainty.

### B.1 Common Inputs and Evaluation Protocol

We use the notation of the main text: $`J_{i}`$ is the number of
available judges for item $`i`$, and $`p^{\mathrm{llm}}_{ij0}`$ is judge
$`j`$’s probability that the original candidate $`A_{i}`$ wins. Let
$`y_{ij0}\in\{0,1\}`$ denote its binary verdict for that same candidate,
with one indicating $`A_{i}`$. The sums below run over the $`J_{i}`$
available judges.

We use the binary entropy function $`H`$ defined in Section 3.2, with
base-2 logarithms for the entropy baselines. PE, VE, and MSP require no
fitted parameters. DS is fitted on the unlabeled
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{fit}}`$ and then held fixed on
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{eval}}`$. Gold labels are used only for
evaluation.

### B.2 Predictive Entropy

Predictive entropy of verdict-token probabilities has been used to guide
abstention by LLM judges (Badshah et al., 2026a). We apply it to the
panel by averaging the LLM probabilities before taking entropy:

```math
\overline{p}_{i}^{\mathrm{llm}}=\frac{1}{J_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{J_{i}}p^{\mathrm{llm}}_{ij0},\qquad\mathrm{PE}_{i}=H(\overline{p}_{i}^{\mathrm{llm}}).
```

The superscript distinguishes this mean from $`\overline{p}_{i}`$ in the
main text, which averages cross-fitted LMM predictions and is used to
compute $`U_{i}`$. PE uses the LLM probabilities directly, without
fitting the LMM.

PE measures uncertainty in the panel-mean preference. It is largest when
the mean is $`0.5`$ and small when the mean is near zero or one. A mean
near $`0.5`$ can reflect individually uncertain judges or confident
judges favoring opposite answers. Its range is $`[0,1]`$.

### B.3 Vote Entropy

VE uses the binary verdicts rather than their probabilities:

```math
\mathrm{VE}_{i}=H\!\left(\frac{1}{J_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{J_{i}}y_{ij0}\right).
```

It is zero for unanimous votes and largest for an evenly divided panel.
Items with the same vote split have the same VE, even when their LLM
probabilities differ. Thus both unanimously correct and unanimously
incorrect panels receive zero VE. Its range is $`[0,1]`$.

Vote entropy is a committee-disagreement measure (Dagan and Engelson,
1995; Settles, 2009). Recent LLM-judge calibration work evaluates
majority confidence across repeated verdicts (Radharapu et al., 2026).
For binary verdicts, VE is a monotonic transformation of one minus
majority confidence.

### B.4 Dawid–Skene Posterior Entropy

A Bayesian variant of the Dawid–Skene model (Dawid and Skene, 1979) has
been applied to LLM-evaluator calibration (Gao et al., 2024), while DS
is used as an aggregation baseline in CARE (Zhao et al., 2026). We use
entropy of its posterior as a risk score, extending these aggregation
applications to error ranking.

DS estimates a class prior and a confusion matrix for each judge from
original-condition binary verdicts, assuming judges are conditionally
independent given an item’s unobserved label. Let $`\widehat{\rho}_{i}`$
be its fitted posterior probability that candidate $`A_{i}`$ is
preferred, given $`y_{i10},\ldots,y_{iJ_{i}0}`$. Then

```math
\mathrm{DS\ entropy}_{i}=H(\widehat{\rho}_{i}).
```

This DS posterior is distinct from both the observed LLM probabilities
$`p^{\mathrm{llm}}_{ij0}`$ and the cross-fitted LMM predictions
$`\widehat{p}_{ij0}`$.

We fit the prior and confusion matrices by expectation–maximization on
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{fit}}`$, with additive smoothing of $`0.5`$.
Initialization uses the proportion of votes for $`A_{i}`$, clipped to
$`[10^{-3},1-10^{-3}]`$. Estimation stops after 200 iterations or when
the maximum change in posterior probabilities is below $`10^{-7}`$. The
label orientation is chosen by agreement with fit-set majority votes,
without gold labels; swapping the orientation does not change entropy.
The fitted parameters remain fixed for evaluation.

DS entropy measures uncertainty after accounting for learned judge
confusion patterns. It uses binary votes, not
$`p^{\mathrm{llm}}_{ij0}`$. A confidently wrong posterior can still have
low entropy. Its range is $`[0,1]`$. As in the main text, DS entropy
ranks items by risk; DS aggregation instead uses the posterior to choose
their final verdicts.

### B.5 Mean Per-Judge MSP Uncertainty

Maximum softmax probability (MSP) is a confidence measure (Hendrycks and
Gimpel, 2017), also evaluated for selective prediction with LLM judges
(Radharapu et al., 2026). Our risk baseline averages one minus this
confidence across judges:

```math
\mathrm{MSP}_{i}=\frac{1}{J_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{J_{i}}\left[1-\max\!\left(p^{\mathrm{llm}}_{ij0},1-p^{\mathrm{llm}}_{ij0}\right)\right].
```

The MSP baseline reports uncertainty: larger values indicate more
uncertain judges. Its range is $`[0,0.5]`$. It remains low when every
judge is confident, including when confident judges disagree. The
maximum is taken separately for each judge _before_ averaging. Taking
$`1-\max(\overline{p}_{i}^{\mathrm{llm}},1-\overline{p}_{i}^{\mathrm{llm}})`$
instead would measure uncertainty of the panel mean and produce the same
ranking as binary PE.

#### Comparability and ties.

All four baselines are evaluated against the same
$`e_{i}^{\mathrm{MV}}`$ defined in the main text, on the same held-out
items. AUROC uses average ranks for equal scores. For
$`1-\mathrm{AURC}`$ and Err@50, we average over uniform random orderings
within tied scores, so file order cannot affect selective acceptance
results. Different raw score ranges do not affect these ranking-based
comparisons.

## Appendix C Benchmark Details and Judging Protocol

### C.1 Benchmark Construction and Validation Labels

#### Chatbot-Arena.

A pairwise-preference benchmark contains $`17{,}325`$ items, 17 judge
models, and $`589{,}050`$ judge observations. Each seed has $`5{,}198`$
reference items and $`3{,}465`$ evaluation items. The panel-error target
compares the original-condition majority decision with the human
pairwise-preference label.

#### HaluEval.

A hallucination-detection benchmark contains $`20{,}000`$ items, 6
judges, and $`120{,}000`$ observations. Each seed has $`6{,}000`$
reference items and $`4{,}000`$ evaluation items. The benchmark label
determines whether the original-condition panel verdict is correct.

#### MMLU.

A multiple-choice benchmark contains $`28{,}084`$ items, 17 judges, and
$`477{,}428`$ observations. Each seed has $`8{,}425`$ reference items
and $`5{,}617`$ evaluation items. The objective answer key supplies the
validation label.

#### LLMBar.

An instruction-following benchmark contains 419 items, 5 judges, and
$`12{,}570`$ observations across 6 conditions. Each seed has 126
reference items and 83 evaluation items. The expert preference label
determines whether the original-condition panel verdict is correct.

#### LLMEval².

We use a 200-item subset of the pairwise-preference benchmark, evaluated
by 5 judges, yielding $`6{,}000`$ observations across 6 conditions. Each
seed has 60 reference items and 40 evaluation items. The source
preference label determines whether the original-condition panel verdict
is correct.

## Appendix D High-Consensus Analysis

Figure 6: Error ranking under exact matching and high consensus with
probability. (a) AUROC comparing items with the same vote pattern; VE
and DS entropy are .5. (b) AUROC among items with panel-mean confidence
at least .8. Both panels compare SCUR with the same four baselines.
Points and whiskers show means and $`\pm 1`$ sample standard deviation
over five splits. Dashed lines mark chance performance.

Figure 6 complements the high-consensus risk analysis in Figure 4 in two
settings: comparing items with the same vote pattern, and evaluating
items with high panel-mean confidence.

#### Exact-matched analysis.

We compare items with the same vote pattern under the original
condition: each judge chooses the same candidate across the compared
items. This keeps both the vote split and the judges supporting each
candidate fixed. We compute AUROC within each group containing both
incorrect and correct decisions, then average these values using the
number of incorrect–correct item pairs in each group as weights:

```math
\mathrm{AUROC_{pattern}}=\frac{\sum_{g}n_{g}^{+}n_{g}^{-}\,\mathrm{AUROC}_{g}}{\sum_{g}n_{g}^{+}n_{g}^{-}},
```

where $`n_{g}^{+}`$ and $`n_{g}^{-}`$ count incorrect and correct
decisions in group $`g`$. VE and fitted DS entropy are constant within
each group, giving AUROC .5. SCUR’s average AUROC is .693, versus .610
for PE and .621 for MSP (panel a). Thus SCUR can distinguish incorrect
from correct decisions even when the judges make the same choices.

#### High consensus with probability.

Using the panel-mean probability $`\overline{p}_{i}^{\mathrm{llm}}`$
defined in Appendix B, high consensus with probability selects items
satisfying
$`\max(\overline{p}_{i}^{\mathrm{llm}},1-\overline{p}_{i}^{\mathrm{llm}})\geq.8`$.
This is equivalent to $`\mathrm{PE}_{i}\leq H(.8)`$: the test asks
whether errors remain distinguishable within a low-PE subset.

SCUR has the highest mean AUROC among the five methods on all benchmarks
in panel (b). On Arena, its AUROC is .676, compared with .596 for the
best baseline, MSP. This supports detecting residual risk even when the
panel’s mean probability strongly favors one answer. The LLMBar AUROC
remains near chance (.539), and LLMEval² has only 3–5 errors per split,
limiting the precision of its estimate. The two panels examine
complementary aspects of high consensus, showing that strong judge
agreement does not eliminate meaningful variation in error risk.
