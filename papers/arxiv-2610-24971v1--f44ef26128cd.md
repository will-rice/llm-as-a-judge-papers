Rubrics Carry the Standard

# Rubrics Carry the Standard: Shapley Attribution for Checklist-Based LLM Judges

Saeid Asgari    Rui Ying    Sara Malvar    Rakshanda Agarwal    Bruce
Sun    Claire Wang Affiliation: Leonardo de Oliveira Nunes, Ranveer
Chandra Affiliation:  Email: [saeidasgari@microsoft.com](mailto:)

###### Abstract

Checklist rubrics applied by large language model (LLM) judges have
become a standard instrument for evaluating open-ended outputs and for
rewarding them during training. Two assumptions surround this practice:
that leniency or strictness is a property of the judge model, and that a
rubric can be improved by asking a model to review it. We test both in a
controlled grading task with planted flaws and on two human-labeled
datasets, SummEdits and RAGTruth. Holding the judge and the outputs
fixed, rubric content moves the pass rate of correct outputs from 2% to
100%, whereas changing the judge model or the rendering of the same
checks moves it by a few points. Model self-review then drifts the
rubric toward the reviser’s own standard: it adds requirements the task
never stated and deletes checks that human labels had taught, with three
reviser models and on all three datasets. To measure and control this,
we show that under all-must-pass aggregation the Shapley value of each
check has an exact closed form, which yields per-check catch credit,
false-fail blame and unique catches in linear time. Attribution shows
that self-review deletes the checks that do unique work and keeps the
checks that cause false fails. A change-control gate that combines these
quantities with a provenance freeze on label-derived checks holds nearly
every drifting revision chain at its starting quality, with identical
decisions under three judge models.

## 1  Introduction

A large language model (LLM) applying a rubric has become the default
grader for open-ended work: agent trajectories, written reports,
summaries, medical answers \[33, 1\]. Rubrics are increasingly written
as checklists of atomic yes/no questions, both for evaluation \[5, 17\]
and as reward signals for reinforcement learning \[9, 29\]. Two
assumptions have grown up around this practice. The first is that
leniency or strictness belongs to the judge: when grading looks wrong,
the remedy is a stronger model or a sterner prompt. The second is that a
rubric can be improved by asking a model to review it for ambiguity and
gaps, a step that is cheap, feels like diligence, and is often run in a
loop.

This paper tests both assumptions under controlled conditions and finds
that each misplaces the standard. The standard a grader enforces is
written in the rubric’s content; the judge largely executes it, and
unsupervised model revision moves it.

Our primary testbed is a security reconnaissance task: an agent inspects
a synthetic cloud tenant and writes a report of security posture issues,
and a judge grades the report against the tenant’s known inventory.
Because every flaw is planted, every report carries a label, and because
we author the rubrics, we can vary one factor at a time. We replicate
the central findings on two public datasets with human labels: SummEdits
\[16\] for summary consistency and RAGTruth \[31\] for faithfulness in
retrieval-augmented generation (RAG).

Four findings organize the paper. First, rubric content sets strictness
(Section 4). Hand-written checks pass 96 to 98% of correct reports and
model-generated checks for the same environments pass 2 to 8%, under
every rendering we tried and under three judge models that agree within
a few points. The generated checks are not harsher in tone; they require
content the task never asked for, and under all-must-pass aggregation
each such check multiplies the pass rate of correct outputs by its own.
On RAGTruth, ten generic faithfulness checks pass 41% of responses that
human annotators judged faithful, against 81% for a free-form verdict. A
validation set dominated by planted defects hides all of this, since a
rubric that fails everything scores 89% accuracy on a set that is 90%
flawed.

Second, for all-must-pass rubrics the Shapley value \[24\] of each check
has an exact closed form (Section 5): a report’s outcome is divided
equally among the checks that failed it. This gives every check a catch
credit, a false-fail blame and a count of unique catches at the cost of
one pass over the judge’s answers, and it decomposes a rubric’s two
headline rates exactly.

Third, model self-review drifts a rubric toward the reviser’s own
standard (Section 6). From a hand-written rubric it adds requirements
such as remediation advice, and correct passed falls from 97% to 6%.
From a rubric that human labels had taught to fail any report omitting a
real risk, it deletes that coverage check as “vague”, and the catch rate
on unanticipated omissions falls from 100% to 8 to 25%. Holding the
check count fixed does not prevent the drift; only delete-only revision
was safe. The effect appears with Claude Opus, Sonnet and Haiku as
revisers and on both human-labeled datasets. Attribution exposes the
mechanism: of 186 checks that self-review deleted, 78 held a catch no
other check made and 2 carried any false-fail blame.

Fourth, rubric revisions can be gated like code changes (Section 7). We
compare a regression test on labeled calibration data, a provenance
freeze with a label-free screen, a Shapley gate, and their combination.
Only the combination protects both failure directions: on held-out
environments it keeps 9 of 10 drifting chains within 5 points of their
starting quality, with decisions unchanged across three judge models,
and on RAGTruth it holds the label-taught rubric at its starting
quality.

#### Contributions.

1.  1.  A controlled decomposition of judge strictness into rubric content,
        rendering, aggregation rule and judge model, showing that content
        dominates, with a prevalence analysis of why accuracy conceals it.
2.  2.  An exact, closed-form Shapley attribution for all-must-pass rubrics
        that yields per-check catch credit, false-fail blame and unique
        catches in $`O(nk)`$ time.
3.  3.  Evidence, across three reviser models, three revision constraints
        and three datasets, that model self-review of a rubric drifts toward
        the reviser’s own standard, together with an attribution-based
        account of what it removes.
4.  4.  Change-control gates for rubric revision, evaluated with
        leave-one-out labels on 12 synthetic and 8 human-labeled revision
        chains and under three judge models.

## 2  Related work

#### LLM judges and their biases.

LLM-as-a-judge was popularized by MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena \[33\].
Documented failure modes include position bias \[30\], self-preference
\[21\] and correlated errors across models \[14\]; panels of diverse
judges reduce some of them \[28\], and fine-tuned evaluators such as
Prometheus condition on a rubric \[15\]. JudgeBench measures judge
accuracy \[27\], and the alternative annotator test asks when a judge
may replace a human annotator \[4\]. This literature holds the rubric
fixed and varies the judge; we do the reverse and find the rubric to be
the larger factor. Bagaria et al. \[2\] report that rubric text alone
predicts judge labels on HealthBench and ResearchRubrics, consistent
with the rubric carrying the standard.

#### Checklists and generated rubrics.

TICK \[5\] and CheckEval \[17\] decompose evaluation into generated
yes/no checklists; HealthBench uses physician-written criteria \[1\].
Rubrics and checklists serve as rewards \[9, 29\], and recursive
decomposition with filtering of misaligned criteria improves
rubric-based judges \[25\]. Recent meta-evaluations find generated
rubrics coherent within a model family but poorly transferable \[26\],
and longer, finer-grained and skewed toward high scores than
human-written ones \[12\]; RIFT offers a taxonomy of rubric failure
modes with automated diagnostics \[22\]. Our contribution is causal: a
comparison of hand-written and generated checks under fixed judges and
outputs that isolates task-irrelevant requirements as the source of
strictness.

#### Rubric refinement and drift.

EvalGen observed criteria drift, in which users’ criteria shift as they
grade, and proposed aligning assertions with human grades \[23\];
reflect-and-revise refines essay-scoring rubrics from disagreements with
human scores \[10\]. Closest to our work, Ding et al. \[6\] show that
criterion-preserving rubric edits that pass standard validation can
shift a judge’s preferences on target domains by up to 27.9% and that
the shift propagates through alignment; Rulers prevents per-instance
reinterpretation by freezing a rubric into a hashed specification
\[11\]. We study a different source of drift, model self-review without
labels, characterize its direction, and contribute per-check attribution
and gates that decide which edits to accept rather than freezing the
whole rubric. That self-review fails without an external signal echoes
results on self-correction in reasoning \[13\] and contrasts with the
gains reported for self-refinement of outputs \[19\].

#### Rubrics as rewards.

Optimizing against a proxy reward overfits it \[7\]. Rubric-based
reinforcement learning can be exploited through partially satisfied
compound criteria \[20\], and generated rubrics can reward dishonest
answers on unanswerable tasks \[32\]; Constitutional AI relies on model
critique against written principles \[3\]. Our drift result implies that
a rubric revised by the model family it grades can absorb that family’s
own standard without any optimization pressure.

#### Shapley attribution.

Shapley values \[24\] underlie feature attribution \[18\] and data
valuation \[8\], where they are usually approximated by sampling. For
the all-must-pass rubric game the value is exact and closed-form, which
makes per-check attribution cheap enough to run inside a revision loop.

## 3  Experimental setup

### 3.1  Tasks and datasets

#### Recon.

We built 12 synthetic cloud environments (storage exposure, legacy
authentication, dangling Domain Name System (DNS) records, guest
sharing, open management ports, leaked secrets and others). Each has a
ground-truth inventory with two planted findings and several minor,
optional observations. _Recon-72_, used for the strictness experiments,
holds 2 correct reports and 4 single-flaw reports per environment (a
missed finding, a fabricated finding, a finding on the wrong resource,
an overstated impact): 24 correct, 48 flawed. _Recon-108_, used for all
revision and gating experiments, adds per environment three reports
judged against an extended inventory that contains one additional real
exposure outside the planted scenario: a correct report that mentions
it, a report with a fabricated finding, and an _unplanned miss_, the
original correct report, which now omits a real exposure. Rubric authors
and revisers never saw the extended inventories. Unplanned misses test
whether a rubric can fail a report for omitting something its author did
not anticipate. Recon-108 holds 36 correct and 72 flawed reports. For
gating, 4 environments (36 reports) serve as calibration and 8 (24
correct, 48 flawed, 8 unplanned misses) as evaluation.

#### SummEdits.

From the chat and meeting domains of SummEdits \[16\] we sampled 146
summaries of 38 conversations, up to two human-labeled consistent and
two inconsistent summaries per conversation (73 consistent). Twelve
conversations (46 items, 22 consistent) form the calibration split and
the remaining 100 items the evaluation split.

#### RAGTruth.

From the RAGTruth test split \[31\] we drew 40 sources (20 question
answering with retrieved passages, 20 news summarization, each at most
3,000 characters) and, for each, two responses with no annotated
hallucination span and two with at least one, keeping only responses the
dataset marks as good quality: 160 responses, 80 faithful. Twelve
sources (48 responses, 24 faithful) form the calibration split and 28
sources (112 responses, 56 faithful) the evaluation split. The responses
were written by the LLMs used to build RAGTruth, not by the models we
study.

### 3.2  Labels

Every label in this paper is a single bit per output: pass or fail.
There are no per-check labels, no scores and no written rationales. On
Recon the bit is known by construction (a report passes iff it is
correct and complete); on SummEdits it is the human judgment consistent
or inconsistent; on RAGTruth it is faithful or containing a
hallucination, and although RAGTruth also marks the hallucinated span,
we use only the binary label. Labels are used in two ways, always from
the calibration split (36 reports on Recon, 46 summaries on SummEdits,
48 responses on RAGTruth). To _teach_ a rubric, the reviser sees each
calibration output with its label next to the judge’s verdict and
per-check answers under the current rubric, with disagreements flagged,
for example:

> Report: “Critical: storage account datalake01, container ‘hr-exports’,
> has anonymous read enabled and holds payroll CSVs; … Medium:
> diagnostic logging is disabled on datalake01, …”  
> Human label: pass; judge with your current rubric: fail \<--
> DISAGREES; judge’s answers: \[Yes, Yes, Yes, Yes, No, No, No, Yes\]

This correct report is failed by three checks of the generated draft
that ask for things it need not contain (logging on a second account,
resource locks, and a recommendation to disable anonymous access);
disagreements like this one are what taught the label-taught rubric that
remediation and these minor items are optional. To _gate_ a revision,
the same labels are combined with the judge’s per-check answers: one
pass/fail bit per output is enough to tell whether each check that
answered No caught a flaw or failed a correct output, which is all the
attribution of Section 5 requires. On Recon the reviser was told the
labels came from security analysts; the full teaching prompts and
labeled examples from both public datasets are in Appendices A, C and D.

### 3.3  Judging protocol and models

The judge sees the source (inventory, conversation, or passages), one
output and the rubric, and answers each check Yes or No. An output
passes if every check is Yes (all-must-pass) unless stated otherwise.
Each judging batch contains at most one output per source, so the judge
never compares versions of the same output, and item identifiers are
opaque. Judges and revisers run as sandboxed sessions that read only
their input file and write only their output file. We use Claude Haiku
5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Opus 5.5; Sonnet is the default judge and Opus the
default rubric author and reviser.

### 3.4  Metrics

We report two rates for every rubric: _correct passed_, the share of
correct outputs that pass, and _flawed caught_, the share of flawed
outputs that fail. On Recon-108 we also report _unplanned caught_ on the
unplanned-miss slice. We avoid accuracy for the reason given in
Section 4.3. Paired comparisons between rubric versions use an exact
McNemar test on the outputs whose verdict changed.

### 3.5  Rubrics

On Recon, the _hand-written_ rubric has 6 checks per environment written
by the author against the planted scenario. Two rubrics were written by
Opus from the inventories alone: a _ranked_ rubric of exactly 12 checks
per environment, ordered by importance so that its prefixes give the 4-
and 8-check rubrics of Section 4, and a _generated draft_ of 4 to 10
checks per environment (92 in all, 7.7 per environment) that is the
starting point of every revision chain from a generated rubric. The
_label-taught_ rubric is the generated draft revised once by Opus after
seeing the 36 labeled calibration reports with the judge’s verdicts; it
learned that remediation advice is optional and added an open coverage
check (“does the report mention every other real weakness in the
inventory?”), averaging 7.9 checks per environment. On SummEdits and
RAGTruth, Opus wrote one ranked list of 10 generic checks per dataset
from the same prompt template, and a label-taught version was produced
in the same way from each calibration split. Appendix B shows one
environment with all of its reports and rubric versions, and Appendix A
reproduces every prompt.

## 4  Rubric content sets strictness

### 4.1  Decomposition on Recon-72

We varied the rubric along four axes: _content_ (a free-form verdict, a
generic 1 to 5 scale over groundedness, completeness and actionability,
the hand-written checks, the generated checks), _count_ (nested prefixes
of 2, 4 and 6 hand checks and of 4, 8 and 12 generated checks),
_rendering_ (a yes/no checklist, one paragraph read holistically, or
each check scored 1 to 5 with pass at 4 or above) and _judge model_.
Sonnet conditions ran twice; Haiku and Opus once.

Figure 1: Strictness on Recon-72 (24 correct, 48 flawed reports). (a)
Operating point of each rubric under a Sonnet judge. Hand-written and
generated checks form two clusters that rendering does not move. (b)
Correct reports passed under the same rubrics by three judges, which
agree within a few points except with a generic scale and a pass
threshold of 5. _Takeaway:_ what the checks ask for moves correct passed
from about 4% to about 96%, while rendering and judge model move it by a
few points, as claimed: strictness is set by rubric content.

#### Content decides; rendering and judge do not.

Figure 1a shows two clusters. The six hand checks pass 95.8% of correct
reports as a checklist, 97.9% as a paragraph and 95.8% when each is
scored 1 to 5, and catch every flaw in all three renderings. The twelve
ranked generated checks pass 4.2%, 4.2% and 8.3%, also catching every
flaw. Across judges (Figure 1b) the hand checklist passes 95.8 to 100%
of correct reports and the generated checklist 0 to 4.2%. Nearly every
generated check that fails a correct report asks for content outside the
output contract, such as “does the report recommend disabling anonymous
access?”.

#### Count costs only what the added checks cost.

Nested hand checks raise flawed caught from 52.1% (2 checks) to 74.0%
(4) to 100% (6) at a constant 95.8% correct passed. Nested prefixes of
the ranked generated rubric pass 20.8% (4 checks), 2.1% (8) and 4.2%
(12). Under all-must-pass, correct passed is the probability that every
check answers Yes; if false fails were independent it would equal the
product of the per-check pass rates on correct outputs. Across 12 rubric
versions on Recon-108 the per-environment product predicts the observed
rate within 2 points. Ten checks that each wrongly fail 5% of correct
outputs pass only $`0.95^{10}\approx 60\%`$ of them, and no single check
looks wrong on inspection.

#### A scale threshold is a per-judge dial.

The one condition on which judges diverge is the generic 1 to 5 scale
with pass only at 5: Haiku passes 12.5% of correct reports, Sonnet 56.2%
and Opus 79.2%, because the threshold measures how readily each model
awards a top score. On reports with a missed finding, Sonnet’s pass rate
is 95.8% at threshold 3, 58.3% at 4 and 0% at 5.

#### Leniency belongs to the empty rubric.

With a free-form verdict every judge passes every correct report, and
Sonnet also passes 100% of reports with a missed finding (Haiku 83.3%,
Opus 75.0%). Nothing asked the judge to check completeness.

#### The aggregator outweighs the wording.

With per-check answers fixed, changing the generated draft’s aggregator
on Recon-108 from all-must-pass to “at most one No” raises correct
passed from 5.6% to 69.4%. Three rounds of rewording by self-review left
it at 5.6%.

### 4.2  Human-labeled datasets

Figure 2: Generic checklists of increasing length under a Sonnet judge,
mean of two runs. (a) On SummEdits each added check trades a few
consistent summaries passed for inconsistent ones caught. (b) On
RAGTruth, going from 3 to 10 checks costs 36 points of faithful
responses passed for at most 4 points of catches; the same 10 checks
read as a paragraph pass 75%. _Takeaway:_ the content effect replicates
on human labels, small where generic checks rarely demand anything a
correct output lacks (SummEdits) and large where they forbid the light
synthesis annotators accepted (RAGTruth); RAGTruth is also the one place
where rendering mattered, through the aggregator.

The same count experiment with generic checklists reproduces the effect
on human labels (Figure 2). On SummEdits, free-form passed 91.1% of
consistent summaries and caught 76.0% of inconsistent ones; 3, 6 and 10
checks moved this to 86.3/77.4, 79.5/80.8 and 76.7/83.6. The direction
matches Recon with a smaller magnitude, since generic checks rarely
demand content a consistent summary lacks.

On RAGTruth the effect appears at full size. Free-form passed 80.6% of
faithful responses and caught 88.1% of hallucinated ones; 3, 6 and 10
checks gave 77.5/92.5, 55.6/96.2 and 41.2/95.0. Ten checks fail 29 and
34 faithful responses (two runs) that free-form passes and pass none
that free-form fails ($`p<10^{-8}`$ in each run), for 7 points of
additional catches. The strictness is heavier in question answering,
where 33.8% of faithful answers pass ten checks against 48.8% of
faithful summaries: the check against “conclusions, inferences, causal
links, or generalizations that go beyond what the source directly
supports” fails 21 to 22 of the 40 faithful answers, the check against
merging passages 15 to 17, and the check against unsupported answers 11
to 14. Answering a question from several passages requires exactly the
light synthesis these checks forbid, and the annotators accepted it.
Here rendering matters: the same ten checks read as one paragraph pass
75.0% of faithful responses. Since the paragraph carries the same
propositions, the difference lies in the aggregator; read holistically,
the judge does not let one borderline inference fail an otherwise
faithful answer.

### 4.3  Why accuracy hides strictness

Figure 3: Accuracy of three Recon rubrics as a function of the flawed
share of the validation set, computed from their two rates. The
generated draft has balanced accuracy 52, the level of chance, yet
outscores the hand-written rubric whenever more than 80% of the set is
flawed. _Takeaway:_ accuracy on a defect-heavy validation set can rank a
rubric that fails almost everything above one that works, which is why
we report the two rates throughout.

Judge validation sets are commonly built by planting defects, so most of
their items are flawed, and accuracy is a prevalence-weighted mixture of
the two rates. The generated draft passes 5.6% of correct reports and
catches 98.6% of flawed ones; its accuracy is 89% on a set that is 90%
flawed and 52% on a balanced set (Figure 3). Reporting both rates, or
balanced accuracy, removes the dependence.

## 5  Exact Shapley attribution for all-must-pass rubrics

Which checks are responsible for a rubric’s catches and for its false
fails? Per-check fail rates do not answer this, because a flawed output
failed by three checks should not count three times. We treat each
graded output as a cooperative game among the checks.

Fix an output $`i`$ and the rubric’s checks $`K`$, and let
$`F_{i}\subseteq K`$ be the checks the judge answered No. Define
$`v_{i}(S)=\mathbb{1}[S\cap F_{i}\neq\emptyset]`$: a coalition of checks
fails the output if any of its members does, and the rubric’s verdict is
fail iff $`v_{i}(K)=1`$.

###### Proposition 1.

For the game $`v_{i}`$, the Shapley value of check $`j`$ is
$`\phi_{j}(v_{i})=1/|F_{i}|`$ if $`j\in F_{i}`$ and $`0`$ otherwise.

###### Proof.

If $`j\notin F_{i}`$, $`j`$ never changes $`v_{i}`$ and is a null
player, so $`\phi_{j}=0`$. If $`j\in F_{i}`$, in a uniformly random
ordering of $`K`$ the marginal contribution of $`j`$ is 1 exactly when
$`j`$ precedes every other member of $`F_{i}`$, which happens with
probability $`1/|F_{i}|`$, and 0 otherwise. Efficiency holds:
$`\sum_{j}\phi_{j}=1=v_{i}(K)`$. ∎

Summing over labeled outputs gives each check a _catch credit_
$`C_{j}=\sum_{i\,\text{flawed}}\phi_{j}(v_{i})`$ and a _false-fail
blame_ $`B_{j}=\sum_{i\,\text{correct}}\phi_{j}(v_{i})`$. A check holds
a _unique catch_ on flawed output $`i`$ when $`F_{i}=\{j\}`$: removing
it would let that output pass. By efficiency, credits sum to the number
of flawed outputs caught and blames to the number of correct outputs
failed, so the two headline rates decompose exactly over checks.
Computing all three quantities takes one pass over the answer matrix,
$`O(nk)`$ for $`n`$ outputs and $`k`$ checks, against $`2^{k}`$
coalitions for a generic Shapley computation. (The Banzhaf value would
assign $`2^{1-|F_{i}|}`$ and lose efficiency.) Attribution uses the
per-check answers that checklist judges already produce, and assumes an
answer does not depend on which other checks are present; in the nested
rubrics of Section 4, the same check graded inside a shorter or longer
checklist changed 2.1 to 3.5% of answers, within the 0 to 3.2% range
between plain reruns of one rubric. Appendix B.4 works through the
attribution of one environment.

#### What is perturbed.

Readers who know Shapley values from feature attribution may expect the
output to be perturbed. Here the output and the judge’s per-check
answers are fixed, and the players are the checks: a coalition $`S`$ is
a smaller rubric, and $`v_{i}(S)`$ is the verdict that rubric would
give. Because the judge answers each check separately, every
sub-rubric’s verdict can be read off the stored answers without
re-grading.

#### A worked example.

Consider a rubric of four checks graded on five outputs (Table 1).
Output R2 is failed by A and B: if the four checks are added to the
rubric in a random order, the verdict flips when the first of A or B
arrives, each is first in half of the orders, so each receives
$`\tfrac{1}{2}`$ and C and D receive 0.

| Output | Label   | Checks answering No ($`F_{i}`$) | Share of the outcome           |
| ------ | ------- | ------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| R1     | flawed  | A                               | A: 1 (unique catch)            |
| R2     | flawed  | A, B                            | A, B: $`\tfrac{1}{2}`$ each    |
| R3     | flawed  | B, C, D                         | B, C, D: $`\tfrac{1}{3}`$ each |
| R4     | correct | D                               | D: 1 of blame                  |
| R5     | correct | none                            | none                           |

Table 1: A four-check rubric on five outputs. Totals: A has credit 1.5
and one unique catch; B 0.83 and C 0.33, both without unique catches; D
has credit 0.33 and blame 1. Credits sum to the 3 flawed outputs caught
and blame to the 1 correct output failed. _Takeaway:_ only deleting A
would lose a catch and only deleting D would let a correct output pass,
so unique catches and blame, not raw fail counts, identify which checks
matter; these are the rules the Shapley gate applies in Section 7.

The totals answer the questions a reviser’s edits raise, which can be
checked by deleting each check and re-reading the verdicts. Deleting A
lets R1 pass: A holds the only catch on R1 and must stay. Deleting B or
C changes no verdict, because every output they fail is also failed by
another check; credit that is shared does not protect a check. Deleting
D loses no catch, since R3 is still failed by B and C, and lets the
correct output R4 pass: D’s blame is the cost it imposes, and a new
check with blame should not be admitted. These are exactly the rules of
the Shapley gate in Section 7.

Two caveats follow from the example. First, a check’s quantities depend
on the other checks present: B and C are each safe to delete alone, but
deleting both leaves R3 caught only by D, so attribution has to be
recomputed after every accepted change. Our gates recompute between
revision rounds, not between deletions within a round. Second, zero
credit means only that no labeled output exercised the check. A check
guarding against a flaw absent from the labeled data scores zero however
necessary it is; Section 7 shows this failure for a coverage check whose
flaw had a single labeled example, which is why the strongest gate pairs
attribution with a provenance freeze.

Attribution immediately characterizes the rubrics of Section 3. In the
hand-written rubric, the 72 checks carry 60.0 reports of catch credit
and 1.0 report of blame on Recon-108. In the label-taught rubric, the 23
checks that the label round introduced carry 25.5 reports of credit,
zero blame and 13 of the rubric’s 24 unique catches, while the 72 checks
inherited from the generated draft carry all 6.0 reports of blame. In
the generated draft itself, 92 checks carry 34.0 reports of blame among
36 correct reports.

## 6  Self-review drifts toward the reviser’s standard

### 6.1  Protocol

Starting from the hand-written rubric (H) and the label-taught rubric
(L) on Recon, a reviser revised the checks of all 12 environments for
four rounds under a generic instruction: review for ambiguity, gaps,
checks a different judge might misread, and flaws no check would catch.
The arms were free revision with Opus (two seeds), Sonnet and Haiku;
Opus with the check count held fixed; and Opus restricted to deleting or
merging checks. After rounds 2 and 4 the Sonnet judge graded all 108
reports. On SummEdits and RAGTruth, Opus revised the label-taught rubric
for two rounds under the free, fixed-count and delete-only constraints,
and the generated rubric for two free rounds.

### 6.2  Results on Recon

hand-written rubric, free revision adds requirements and correct reports
stop passing. (b) From the label-taught rubric, free and fixed-count
revision remove or narrow the open coverage check and unplanned misses
stop being caught. Delete-only revision is flat in both. _Takeaway:_
drift runs in opposite directions from the two starting rubrics, too

moves the standard rather than refining it.

Figure 4 shows drift in two directions. From H, free revision by Opus
took correct passed from 97.2% to 5.6% and 11.1% (two seeds), Haiku to
5.6%, and Sonnet more slowly to 63.9% (12 of 36 correct reports newly
failed, none gained, $`p=0.0005`$); fixed-count revision reached 72.2%.
The additions were dominated by remediation requirements: Opus’s round-4
rubrics contain 13 of them across the 12 environments (Haiku’s 12,
Sonnet’s 11), against 1 at the start. The same revisions raised
unplanned caught from 0% to as much as 91.7%, but a rubric that fails
most correct reports is not usable.

From L, Opus deleted the open coverage check in nearly every
environment, describing it in one change note as “the vague completeness
check”; unplanned caught fell from 100% to 25.0% and 8.3%. Sonnet kept
the check in place but rewrote it as a closed list of the items it
expected (“apart from the payroll exposure, the logging gap…”), which no
longer catches an omission it did not list: 100% to 25.0%. Haiku kept
the check but, in several environments, wrote the answer into it (“the
inventory states no further weakness, so answer Yes unless the report
omits the dangling record”), and unplanned caught fell to 66.7%; fixed
count fell to 16.7%. Three revisers, three ways of closing the same
check (Appendix B). In every L arm the lost catches were all unplanned
misses; the 60 planted-flaw catches were untouched. Only delete-only
revision left every metric at its starting value on both rubrics (H:
97.2% to 100% correct passed; L: unchanged) while shrinking them from
6.0 to 4.0 and from 7.9 to 5.8 checks per environment.

Check count is not the mechanism. Free revision grew the H rubric by 1.7
to 3.1 checks per environment and shrank or preserved the L rubric; both
got worse. What changed was content, and in each case it moved toward
what the reviser considered a good report.

#### Model-written tests do not detect the problem; labels do.

Before these loops we tried to repair the generated draft (5.6% correct
passed) with a test-writing loop: the model wrote test reports with
intended verdicts and per-check answers, a judge graded them, and the
model revised on disagreement. Verdicts agreed on 71 or 72 of 72 test
reports in every round, and correct passed remained at 5.6% after three
rounds, as it did under plain self-review. The model wrote its
intended-pass reports to its own standard, remediation advice included,
so the loop had no way to see the standard was wrong. One revision from
36 labeled reports raised correct passed to 80.6 to 83.3% while keeping
every catch; self-review then removes what the labels taught.

### 6.3  Results on human-labeled data

On SummEdits the label round relaxed clauses the annotators did not
enforce and added a paraphrase check (11 checks). The first self-review
round reversed this; its change note says it “tightened leniency clauses
a literal judge could exploit”. On the evaluation split, consistent
summaries passed fell from 78.4% to 54.9% after one round and 58.8%
after two (10 lost, 0 gained, $`p=0.002`$), and balanced accuracy from
80.0 to 72.3. Fixed count drifted as far ($`p=0.012`$). Delete-only was
mildest but not neutral (78.4% to 70.6%, 4 lost, $`p=0.125`$), as its
merges produced longer combined checks.

On RAGTruth the label round rewrote all 10 checks to target material
hallucinations and added carve-outs for peripheral imprecision,
paraphrase and light inference, lifting faithful responses passed on the
evaluation split from 42.9% to 71.4% (18 gained, 2 lost, $`p<0.001`$)
with catches nearly unchanged (91.1% to 89.3%). The first self-review
round split the taught contradiction check into “a strict accuracy check
for every copied detail and a contradiction check without the
peripheral-error exemption, since misquoted numbers or dates in minor
details are still unfaithful”, a direct reversal of what the labels had
shown. Faithful passed fell to 46.4% (14 lost, 0 gained, $`p=0.0001`$)
and stood at 48.2% after round two; fixed count fell to 51.8%
($`p=0.003`$); delete-only was flat (69.6%, 4 lost and 3 gained).
Self-review of the generated rubric grew it from 10 to 15 checks and
moved it further toward failing everything (98.2% caught, 35.7% passed).

### 6.4  What self-review removes

Figure 5: (a) Shapley catch credit and false-fail blame per check on

opposite of what an improving revision would do.

Attribution makes the mechanism visible. We trace each check’s origin
through revisions by text similarity to its parent. After four rounds of
Opus review of the hand rubric, the 40 added checks carry 19.3 of the
rubric’s 34 reports of blame and rewritten hand checks another 14.7
(Figure 5a). Figure 5b shows what the 12 Recon chains deleted: of 186
deleted checks, 179 had caught at least one flawed report and 78 held a
unique catch, among them 56 of the 71 deleted label-derived checks. Only
2 of the 186 carried any false-fail blame. Self-review removes the
checks that do work no other check does and keeps, or adds, the checks
that fail correct outputs.

## 7  Change control for rubric revision

If rubric content carries the standard and unsupervised revision moves
it, a revision should be accepted the way a code change is: check by
check, against evidence. Each revision proposes a new rubric; a gate
decides, per environment and per check, which changes to accept.
Proposed checks are matched to current ones by text similarity and
treated as rewrites, or otherwise as additions; unmatched current checks
are deletions. We compare four gates.

- •
  Gate R (regression). Accept the whole revision only if, on the
  calibration reports, correct passed and flawed caught each drop by
  fewer than 2 reports and no unplanned miss is lost.
- •
  Gate P (provenance, label-free). Checks that came from a human author
  or from labels are frozen against deletion and rewriting; new checks
  that fail more than half of all reports in their environment are
  blocked.
- •
  Gate S (Shapley). Block deletion of any check holding a unique catch;
  block any addition with false-fail blame above zero; block a rewrite
  that adds a false fail or loses a unique catch. Labels come from the
  same environment with the scored report held out (leave-one-out), so
  no report’s own label decides its verdict.
- •
  Gate S+P. Gate S together with the provenance freeze of Gate P.

Appendix B.5 traces Gates S and S+P through one environment.

## 8  Discussion

#### What the results imply.

Three implications go beyond the individual experiments. First, when a
checklist grader looks too lenient or too strict, the rubric is the
first place to look and the judge model the last. Replacing the judge
moved correct passed by a few points; the content of the checks moved it
by up to 98. A check that fails nearly every output, correct or not, is
almost always demanding something the task does not require, and it can
be found from unlabeled outputs alone. Second, a rubric’s standard
cannot be repaired from inside the model that wrote it. Self-review
imported the reviser’s own standard with all three revisers and on all
three datasets, and a loop in which the model wrote its own test cases
agreed with itself on 71 or 72 of 72 tests while the rubric still failed
94% of correct reports. In every setting, the only intervention that
moved the standard toward the intended one was a small set of labeled
outputs, and self-review then undid it. Third, the same mechanism
matters wherever rubrics serve as rewards. A rubric revised by the model
family it grades can absorb that family’s preferences with no
optimization pressure at all, so a policy trained on it is rewarded for
agreeing with its own family rather than with the task. This is a
quieter route to reward hacking than the exploitation studied in prior
work \[20, 32\].

#### Recommendations.

For teams that grade with checklists:

- •
  Report correct passed and flawed caught, or balanced accuracy, never
  accuracy alone; a validation set built from planted defects rewards
  rubrics that fail everything.
- •
  Treat every added check as a cost. Under all-must-pass, each check
  multiplies the pass rate of correct outputs by its own, so audit each
  check’s fail rate on unlabeled outputs and keep only checks that do
  work no other check does.
- •
  Choose the aggregation rule deliberately and report it; on our data it
  moved correct passed by more than any rewording.
- •
  Record the provenance of every check and freeze human- and
  label-derived checks against automated revision.
- •
  Permit unattended revision to delete or merge, never to add or
  tighten. Even this is not free: on SummEdits, merges produced longer
  checks and cost 8 points of correct passed.
- •
  Gate every revision with per-check Shapley credit and blame on labeled
  data, recompute attribution after each accepted change, and keep at
  least two labeled examples of each rare flaw per environment.

#### What each gate protects, and where it fails.

No single gate was sufficient, and the failures are informative. The
regression gate (R) catches any damage its calibration labels can see
and nothing else: it accepted a revision that changed no calibration
verdict but cut correct passed elsewhere from 95.8% to 70.8%, and on
SummEdits it missed drift concentrated outside the items the rubric had
been taught on. The provenance gate (P) protects label-derived checks
without any labels at revision time but lets harmful new checks through
when they fail only a minority of outputs. The Shapley gate (S) blocks
harmful additions and rewrites precisely, but cannot protect a check
whose flaw is rare in the labeled data, because a zero score there means
no evidence rather than no value. The combination (S+P) covers both
directions and was the most reliable, yet it inherits two limits: a
standard expressed as a clause inside a check is protected only as far
as the check-level freeze reaches, and a rewritten check that the
similarity matcher fails to recognize enters as an addition. In practice
the gates are complements: S+P for routine revisions, R as a final check
on a calibration set refreshed with new labels.

#### Limitations.

The Recon environments and labels are synthetic and ours; the SummEdits
and RAGTruth replications cover consistency and faithfulness grading
with human labels but not agentic tasks. All judges, rubric authors and
revisers are Claude models (the RAGTruth responses come from other
LLMs), so the insensitivity to the judge is shown within one model
family, and the direction of drift toward remediation advice may be
particular to these revisers’ standards even though drift itself
appeared with all three. Samples are small: 24 to 36 correct reports per
comparison and 8 to 12 unplanned misses, so differences of a few points
are within noise, while the main effects (97% to 6%, 100% to 8%) lie far
outside it. Free revision on Recon ran with two Opus seeds and one seed
for the other arms, and each rubric version was graded once per round.
Gates were evaluated by replay from stored per-check answers, which
assumes a check’s answers do not depend on its neighbors; spot re-grades
agreed within 4.2 points. Check matching across revisions relies on a
text-similarity threshold, which misclassified one rewrite as an
addition in the failure case of Section 7.

## 9  Conclusion

A checklist rubric carries the standard its judge enforces: content sets
strictness, the judge model and the rendering move it by a few points,
and the common practice of improving a rubric by model self-review moves
the standard toward the reviser’s own, deleting exactly the checks that
labels taught. Because all-must-pass rubrics admit exact Shapley
attribution in closed form, each check’s contribution can be measured at
negligible cost, and revisions can be gated like code changes. A gate
that combines Shapley rules with a provenance freeze held nearly every
drifting chain at its starting quality, on synthetic and human-labeled
data and under three judge models.

_Change note:_ The old checklist failed responses for minor imprecision
(a slightly miscounted streak, a hedge made a bit firmer, a wrong
passage number, a reasonable inference, generic framing), while
annotators fail only material hallucinations: invented details, steps,
examples, causes or outcomes, reversed or misassigned central facts,
unsupported character labels, and added outside advice. I rewrote the
checks to target those material errors explicitly, added carve-outs for
peripheral imprecision, paraphrase, light inference and passage citation
slips, and added checks for gap-filling with general knowledge, appended
unsupported notes, and inaccurate or self-contradictory ’cannot answer’
claims.

First round of free self-review (rubrics/LRTU_1.json, 13 checks). Change
note: “I split the old contradiction check into a strict accuracy check
for every copied detail and a contradiction check without the
peripheral-error exemption, since misquoted numbers or dates in minor
details are still unfaithful. I added checks for preserving the source’s
certainty (the old wording allowed hedges to be strengthened into
facts), for attributing quotations correctly, and for omissions that
mislead, and I aligned the outside-detail check with the
outside-knowledge check so they no longer conflict. I also separated the
’new relationship’ and ’misdescribed content’ issues into their own
checks and replaced content-specific examples with generic ones.” The
two checks that replaced the taught contradiction check:

1.  3.  Are all specific details the response takes from the source (names,
        numbers, dates, counts, places, quantities, titles) reproduced
        accurately, with no altered values, even for minor or peripheral
        details?
2.  4.  Does the response avoid contradicting the source, such as reversing
        an outcome, negating a stated fact, or changing who or what was
        involved?

Free self-review of the generated checklist (rubrics/RTU_1.json, 14
checks). Change note: “I tightened the support standard so true outside
knowledge counts as unfaithful. I also made the number check tolerate
faithful paraphrase but not miscalculation, and rewrote the confusingly
worded question-answering check so it states its intent clearly and says
to answer Yes when no question is being answered. I added checks for
gaps the old list missed: misquoted or invented quotations, misleading
omissions, false claims about what the source does or does not say, and
responses that contradict themselves.”
