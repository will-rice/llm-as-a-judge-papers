###### Abstract.

Large language model (LLM)-based judges are increasingly used to
evaluate AI systems. An underexplored area is applying these judges to
multi-agent systems, where they may see the interactions behind a final
answer. These interactions, which we call _peer context_, can affect an
evaluation in two ways: (1) they can change the answer produced by the
multi-agent system, because an agent might be swayed by other agents,
and (2) they can change how a judge evaluates that same answer,
depending on whether the judge sees them. In this paper, we separate
these two effects through a controlled experiment (Figure 1). We first
let an agent answer a question and then answer again after seeing peer
responses. We then prompt judges to rate the same final answer with and
without the peer context. Finally, we check whether the judges’ ratings
establish a valid measure of answer quality. Across three benchmarks,
four open-weight LLMs as agents, and six judges, we find that peer
context changes not only the answer but also how an identical answer is
judged, and judges react to it in opposite directions. Moreover,
providing peer context for a wrong answer misleads agents more than
correct peers help them; without the correct answer, judges struggle to
tell known-correct from known-wrong answers—a problem that a well-fitted
statistical model does not reveal. Our results show that even when
evaluating the same answer, how a judge is set up can change the
conclusions differently. We therefore call for evaluations to report
what each judge sees, document detailed results per judge, and validate
rating scales. These practices are an important step toward the
accountable use of LLM-based judges.

## 1. Introduction

LLM-based judges (also known as LLM-as-a-judge) are widely used to
evaluate AI systems for output quality, e.g., accuracy and
helpfulness (Zheng et al., 2023; Gu et al., 2026), toxic or unsafe
responses (Souly et al., 2024), and social biases like stereotypes and
discrimination (Kumar et al., 2024; Eloundou et al., 2025). In some
evaluation settings, they can closely match human preferences, while
being much cheaper and easier to scale than human annotators. As a
result, they are now used to rank models on leaderboards (Zheng et al., 2023) and to decide which models are ready to deploy (OpenAI, 2024;
Anthropic, 2026). However, LLM-based judges are not always neutral.
Prior studies have shown that their judgments can be influenced by an
answer’s position (Shi et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025), its
length (Thakur et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025), whether the judge wrote
the answer itself (Panickssery et al., 2024; Wataoka et al., 2024), cues
of authority (Hwang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025), and extra material
given to the judge like a reference answer (Xu et al., 2025; Krumdick et
al., 2025; Lee et al., 2026).

Although these studies reveal different biases in LLM-based judges, most
share a common design: they change something in the judge’s prompt and
observe how the judgment changes. Few have studied how judges behave
when evaluating multi-agent systems. In these systems, several LLM
agents work on the same task: they may debate a question or revise their
answers after reading each other’s responses (Du et al., 2024). What
makes these systems special for LLM-based judges is that, when a judge
evaluates the final answer, it may also see what the other agents said
along the way (Chan et al., 2024).

We call this information the peer context. Peer context can affect an
evaluation in two ways (Figure 1). First, it can change the answer. For
example, an agent may incorrectly claim that swallowed gum stays in the
body forever after seeing peers make the same claim. Second, it can
change the judgment: the same revised answer receives an illustrative
rating of 2/5 without the peer context and 3/5 with it, even though the
answer itself stays the same.

This raises a measurement problem: when an LLM-based judge evaluates a
multi-agent system, should it see the interaction between the agents?
The interaction may help the judge understand the answer, but it may
also influence the judge. If so, the judgment no longer measures the
answer quality only, which undermines measurement validity (Jacobs and
Wallach, 2021). Clinical trials face a similar problem: the clinicians
who assess patient outcomes are often not told which treatment a patient
received, because their expectations could affect the assessment (Schulz
and Grimes, 2002). Studies of multi-agent systems have the same
structure, with the peer context as the treatment. Prior work shows that
this treatment matters: agents often change their answers after seeing
other agents’ responses, even when this leads them to a wrong
answer (Weng et al., 2025; Cho et al., 2025; Qu et al., 2026). However,
this work typically assumes that the judgment used to measure these
changes is neutral. If the judge also sees the peer context, a drop in
judgments could come from worse answers, from the judge reacting to the
peer context, or from both. To separate these two effects, we ask two
research questions:

- •
  RQ1: How does peer context change the answer being evaluated?
- •
  RQ2: Does peer context change how a judge evaluates the same answer,
  and do different judges react the same?

We answer these questions with a controlled experiment in three steps.
First, we prompt an agent to answer a question independently, show it
peer context, and ask it to answer again. We vary two aspects of the
peer context: what it supports (the correct answer, a wrong answer, or a
mix of both) and how it is presented (as plain information or as
responses from peers). As a baseline, the agent also answers again
without any peer context. Because all final answers start from the same
initial answer, any difference between them comes from the peer context
(RQ1). Second, LLM-based judges evaluate the same final answer twice,
once without and once with the peer context. Because the answer is the
same both times, any difference comes from the judge (RQ2). Third, we
combine the judgments with a hierarchical Bayesian model that accounts
for differences in answer quality and in how each judge uses the rating
scale. Because the model uses answers known to be right or wrong to
define the scale, we also check whether judges can tell these answers
apart.

Across three benchmarks, four open-weight LLMs as agents, and six
judges, we find that peer context affects both the answer and the
judgment. For RQ1, peer context changes the agent’s answer, and wrong
peers mislead the agent much more than correct peers help it. For RQ2,
showing the peer context to a judge changes its judgment of the same
answer, and judges react differently: for some judges, it makes the
effect of the peers look bigger, and for others, smaller. Judges also
often disagree with each other even without the peer context. Finally,
our measurement audit shows that a model with good convergence
diagnostics can still yield a weak measure: judges give known-correct
answers high ratings (4–5) only 73.3% of the time without the correct
answer, compared with 97.5% when it is provided.

These findings convey an important message. When LLM-based judges
evaluate multi-agent systems, the validity of an evaluation depends not
only on the system, but also on how the evaluation is set up. This
includes what the judge sees, which judge is used, and whether the
measure is checked against known answers. However, these configurations
are rarely reported, which makes claims based on LLM-based judges hard
to check or challenge. This is a familiar problem from the perspective
of measurement validity: a measure can be consistent without capturing
what it is meant to capture (Jacobs and Wallach, 2021; Wallach et al.,
2025). It also connects to work on algorithmic auditing, which asks who
audits the auditors and how evaluations should be
documented (Costanza-Chock et al., 2022; Raji et al., 2020; Mitchell et
al., 2019). Our results suggest that the answer must include the
LLM-based judge itself: the evaluator is part of the experiment. We
therefore recommend that evaluations of multi-agent systems compare
judgments with and without the peer context, report results for each
judge separately, and check the measure against answers of known
quality.

This paper makes three contributions. First, we frame the use of
LLM-based judges to evaluate multi-agent systems as a measurement
problem, and we propose an experimental paradigm that separates how peer
context changes the answer from how it changes the judgment. The
paradigm applies whenever a judge sees more than the answer it
evaluates. Second, we provide a measurement framework for auditing
LLM-based judges, which uses a hierarchical Bayesian model to make
differences between judges visible and checks the resulting measure
against answers of known quality. Third, we provide evidence of a
largely overlooked problem: when LLM-based judges evaluate multi-agent
systems, their judgments can reflect how the evaluation is set up, not
only the system being evaluated.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1. Multi-Agent Interaction

In multi-agent LLM systems, agents exchange information, review one
another’s outputs, and contribute to shared decisions (Du et al., 2024;
Wu et al., 2024; Chan et al., 2024). Prior work examines how such
interactions affect reasoning, accuracy, and conformity (Gu et al.,
2025; Ki et al., 2025; Choi et al., 2025). For example, Kaesberg et al.
(2025) compare different decision-making protocols and find that voting
performs better on reasoning tasks, while consensus is more effective on
knowledge tasks. Du et al. (2024) show that multi-agent debate can
improve reasoning and factual accuracy. Multi-agent systems are commonly
evaluated by final answer accuracy and task success (Smit et al., 2024).
Recent studies also evaluate agent interactions, using LLM judges and
automated metrics to assess communication and intermediate progress (Zhu
et al., 2025a; Sun et al., 2025).

Another line of research examines conformity, where models change their
answers in response to other agents (Zhu et al., 2025b; Zhang et al.,
2024; Weng et al., 2025). Cho et al. (2025) find that conformity depends
on the confidence of the model relative to its peers and how peer
information is presented. Such influence can also lead models toward
incorrect answers. Qu et al. (2026) show that incorrect peer consensus
more readily causes harmful revisions than correct consensus produces
beneficial ones.

These studies establish that peer context can change the answer being
evaluated. However, they primarily examine how peer interaction affects
the agent’s answer, rather than how the same context affects the
evaluation of that answer. Our work addresses this distinction by
examining whether an LLM-based judge changes its rating when the answer
stays the same but the peer context becomes visible.

### 2.2. LLM-as-a-Judge

LLM judges are widely used to assess model-generated outputs (Ho et al.,
2026; Zheng et al., 2023; Gu et al., 2026). They typically assign
quality scores to individual responses or compare pairs of responses
based on criteria such as correctness and helpfulness. Such evaluations
provide a scalable alternative to human annotation, especially for
open-ended tasks where exact-match metrics are insufficient. LLM judges
have also been applied to evaluations of fairness, discrimination, and
representational bias (Eloundou et al., 2025; Kumar et al., 2024; Tan
and Lee, 2025; Wu et al., 2025).

However, LLMs are not neutral evaluators. Prior work has identified
systematic biases related to answer position, response length, and
self-preference (Zheng et al., 2023; Gu et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2025).
For example, Koo et al. (2024) examine six cognitive biases across 16
LLM evaluators and find that their preferences often diverge from human
judgments. These results show that an evaluation score may reflect
factors beyond the quality of the answer itself.

Another concern is how additional context affects evaluation. Xu et al.
(2025) study tasks where judges must use external information to assess
responses. They find that even strong evaluators struggle with
contextual evaluation. Other studies examine the influence of social
cues. For example, Wang et al. (2025) investigate bandwagon and
authority biases and find that even reasoning models remain susceptible
to these effects. Similarly, Hwang et al. (2025) show that persuasive
cues, including majority opinions, can inflate judges’ scores for
incorrect mathematical solutions. Ma et al. (2025) further examine bias
in multi-agent LLM judging and find that debate-based evaluation can
amplify biases, including bandwagon effects. Unlike reference
information that may help establish correctness, such cues can influence
judgments without providing reliable evidence about answer quality.

These studies show that both useful information and social cues can
affect LLM judgments. However, in multi-agent systems, peer context may
influence not only the judge but also the answer being evaluated. We
examine how the same controlled peer context affects answer revision and
the subsequent evaluation of the revised answer, separating changes in
the answer from changes in the judge’s rating.

### 2.3. Measurement Validity

Research on measurement validity distinguishes consistent measurement
from the accurate representation of an intended construct (Jacobs and
Wallach, 2021; Wallach et al., 2025). In LLM-based evaluation, a judge
may consistently prefer certain response styles even when they do not
reflect answer quality. Khan et al. (2025) examine methods for
evaluating cultural alignment in LLMs and find that results can vary
substantially across presentation formats and prompting conditions. Even
when a statistical model produces stable estimates, the underlying
ratings may not accurately measure the intended construct. Similar
challenges arise in human assessment. Statistical models can account for
differences in how strictly raters assign scores (Linacre, 1994).
Clinical trials use assessor blinding to reduce the influence of
treatment knowledge on judgments (Schulz and Grimes, 2002). These ideas
motivate our judge-specific measurement model and blind/informed
evaluation design.

Related research on algorithmic auditing examines whether AI systems and
their evaluation procedures behave as intended. For example, Raji et al.
(2020) propose an internal auditing framework that documents risks and
evaluations throughout the AI development lifecycle. Amirizaniani et al.
(2024) introduce a probing-based approach to identify weaknesses in LLM
behavior. Other studies highlight the limitations of existing auditing
practices and black-box access (Birhane et al., 2024; Casper et al.,
2024; Costanza-Chock et al., 2022). Our work extends this auditing
perspective to the evaluator itself by measuring its sensitivity to peer
context and checking whether its ratings distinguish answers with known
correctness.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1. Experimental Framework

To determine how peer context within multi-agent systems affects
LLM-based judges, we distinguish changes in the answer from changes in
how that answer is judged. Our experimental framework therefore has
three parts (Figure 2).

First, we study the agent whose answer is evaluated, which we call the
_focal agent_. The focal agent answers a question on its own and then
answers it again under three conditions: with no additional responses,
with additional responses shown without attribution, or with the same
responses presented as the views of named people. This part tests how
additional information, and the way it is presented, changes the answer.

Second, we study the LLM-based judge (hereafter, _judge_) that evaluates
the focal agent’s final answer. We hold the answer fixed and compare two
conditions: _blind_ evaluation, in which the judge does not see the peer
responses, and _informed_ evaluation, in which it does. This comparison
isolates the effect of peer context on the judgment.

Finally, we combine the judgments with a measurement model that accounts
for differences across judges, and we check whether the resulting scale
distinguishes answers whose correctness is known. Section 3.2 describes
the protocols, and Section 3.3 describes the metrics and the measurement
model.

### 3.2. Evaluation Protocol

#### 3.2.1. Focal Agent Evaluation Protocol

Each trial begins with the focal agent answering a question on its own
(Round 1). Although we use benchmarks of multiple-choice questions, we
prompt the agent to give an open-ended answer of one to three
sentences.¹¹ 1 We made this choice deliberately: (1) an open-ended
answer can change in degree without switching options, for example by
adopting part of a wrong claim or dropping an important caveat; and (2)
it gives the judge an answer whose quality must be assessed, rather than
an option label that could simply be checked against the answer key.
Meanwhile, the multiple-choice format still supplies a known correct
answer and a set of wrong options, which we use to write the peer
responses and to check the judges. In Round 2, the focal agent answers
the same question again under one of three conditions:

- •
  No-Peer: The focal agent answers the same question again without
  additional responses. This serves as a control for changes caused by
  answering the question a second time.
- •
  Content-Only: The focal agent receives the answer content of the
  additional responses as plain text, with no indication that anyone
  holds this view. This condition measures the effect of the information
  per se.
- •
  Peer-Presented: The focal agent receives the additional responses as
  statements from six named people, using pseudonyms from a fixed pool.
  Comparing this condition with Content-Only allows us to examine the
  effect associated with peer attribution.

We call the Round 2 answer the focal agent’s _final answer_; it may or
may not differ from its initial answer. Table 1 shows the full prompt
templates for every step and condition, in the order of the experiment.

In the Content-Only and Peer-Presented conditions, the additional
responses take one of three modes. We refer to the responses as they
appear in the Peer-Presented condition as the _peer context_; this is
also what the judge sees in informed evaluation.

- •
  All-Correct: All six peer responses give the benchmark’s correct
  answer.
- •
  All-Wrong: All six peer responses give the same wrong answer, which we
  call the _targeted wrong answer_. It is drawn at random (with a fixed
  seed) from the incorrect options for each question.
- •
  Mixed: Half of the peer responses give the correct answer, and half
  give the targeted wrong answer.

Within the Peer-Presented condition, we add one additional binary
manipulation. In half of the trials, one of the six people carries an
authority label, such as “team leader,” “research director,” or
“department head”; in the other half, no one does. We include this
variation because prior work shows that authority cues can sway
LLMs (Hwang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025), and it lets us test
whether authority contributes to the effect of presenting content as
coming from peers. For the ablation study (Section 5.1), we also include
a repetition condition in which the same claims are repeated six times
without names, roles, or first-person framing. We report pooled results
across both authority variants, with separate results in Appendix D.2.

Because all Round 2 answers branch from the same Round 1 answer,
comparing them removes differences that would otherwise come from the
initial answer itself. Differences between final answers can therefore
be attributed to the information the focal agent received.

#### 3.2.2. Judge Evaluation Protocol

The experiment on the focal agent investigates whether peer context
changes the answer, but not whether it also changes the evaluation—which
is our second RQ. If the judge’s rating shifts with that interaction,
then a difference in ratings between conditions could reflect a change
in the answer, a change in the judge, or both. To separate the two, we
hold the answer fixed and vary only what the judge sees:

- •
  Blind Evaluation: The judge sees the question and answer, but not the
  associated peer context.
- •
  Informed Evaluation: The judge sees the question and answer, plus the
  peer context that the focal agent saw.

The two conditions differ only in whether the judge sees the peer
context. Because the focal agent’s answer is held fixed across blind and
informed evaluations, any systematic difference between the ratings
reflects the judge’s response to the additional peer context rather than
a change in answer content. Initial, No-Peer, and Content-Only answers
are rated only under blind evaluation, because no responses attributed
to people were shown with them; the blind/informed comparison is
therefore defined only for Peer-Presented answers.

Judges rate each answer on a five-point scale. For reference-free
evaluation, we use the following rubric: 1 = a clear falsehood or
misconception; 2 = a mostly inaccurate or misleading answer; 3 = a
partially truthful answer with both correct and incorrect content; 4 = a
truthful answer with minor imprecision; and 5 = a fully truthful and
non-misleading answer. We also instruct judges to assess factual quality
rather than style, length, or tone.

As shown in Table 1 and Appendix B, model-generated answers (Round 1 and
Round 2 under all conditions) were graded without the benchmark’s
correct answer, in both blind and informed rating passes where
applicable. For a separate check of the rating scale (the anchor audit;
Section 3.3), judges also rate known-correct and known-wrong answers,
both with and without the correct answer provided. The
reference-augmented anchor ratings are used to calibrate the latent
scale.

### 3.3. Evaluation Metrics

#### 3.3.1. Focal Agent Metrics

Because the focal agent writes an open-ended answer rather than choosing
an option, we cannot simply check whether it picked the correct option
or not. Instead, we use three measures that compare the free-text final
answer with the targeted wrong answer, the correct answer, or the peer
context.

First, _false endorsement_ measures whether the final answer supports
the targeted wrong answer. We use a fixed RoBERTa-large classifier
trained for natural language inference (Liu et al., 2019; Williams et
al., 2018; Facebook AI, ), which estimates whether one text implies
another. The classifier compares the final answer with the targeted
wrong answer and estimates how strongly the final answer supports that
wrong answer. We report the mean of this probability for each condition.
Higher values indicate stronger support for the wrong answer.

Second, _semantic similarity_ measures how close two texts are in
meaning. We use Sentence-BERT (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) to represent each text
as a numerical vector, then use cosine similarity to compare these
vectors (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019; Sentence Transformers, ). We
compute the similarity of the final answer to the benchmark’s correct
answer ($`\mathrm{sim}_{\mathrm{correct}}`$) and to the peer context
shown in Round 2 ($`\mathrm{sim}_{\mathrm{peer}}`$). A higher
$`\mathrm{sim}_{\mathrm{correct}}`$ means that the final answer is
closer in meaning to the correct answer. A higher
$`\mathrm{sim}_{\mathrm{peer}}`$ means that it is closer to what the
peers said, whether or not the peers were correct.

Third, *BERTScore F1* (Zhang et al., 2020) compares the final answer
with the correct answer word by word, using contextual word embeddings.
Unlike exact word matching, it gives credit for paraphrases. A higher
value means that the final answer overlaps more in meaning with the
correct answer.

#### 3.3.2. Judge Measurement Model

The blind/informed comparison allows us to examine whether peer context
changes a judge’s rating of the same answer. However, judges may use the
five-point rating scale differently. Some may give higher scores
overall, while others may be better at distinguishing correct from
incorrect answers. To account for these differences, we use a
hierarchical ordinal model that estimates answer quality, how each judge
uses the five-point rating scale, and how peer context affects each
judge’s ratings. Since a judge’s rating may not reflect an answer’s true
quality, we estimate a quality value $`\theta_{i}`$ for each answer
$`i`$:

```math
\theta_{i}=b_{t(i)}+r_{i}\eta_{g(i),c(i)}+\sigma_{\theta}\epsilon_{i},\qquad\epsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1). \tag{1}
```

Here, $`b_{t(i)}`$ is the quality of the Round 1 answer in trial
$`t(i)`$, $`r_{i}`$ equals 1 for a Round 2 answer and 0 for a Round 1
answer, and $`\eta_{g(i),c(i)}`$ is the change in quality that Round 2
condition $`c`$ produces for focal agent $`g`$. The term
$`\sigma_{\theta}\epsilon_{i}`$ lets individual answers vary around
these shared effects.

For answer $`i`$, judge $`j`$, and evaluation pass $`p`$, we model the
evaluator’s rating using

```math
\mu_{ijp}=\alpha_{j}(\theta_{i}-\beta_{j})+\mathbf{1}[p=\mathrm{informed}]\gamma_{j}d_{i}, \tag{2}
```

followed by

```math
y_{ijp}\sim\mathrm{OrderedLogistic}(\mu_{ijp},\kappa). \tag{3}
```

The cutpoints $`\kappa`$ map the continuous score $`\mu_{ijp}`$ onto the
five rating levels.

The parameter $`\alpha_{j}`$ captures how strongly judge $`j`$
distinguishes answers of different quality, $`\beta_{j}`$ captures judge
severity or leniency, and $`\gamma_{j}`$ captures how the evaluator’s
rating changes after being exposed to peer context. The all-correct,
all-wrong, and mixed modes are coded as:

```math
d_{i}=\begin{cases}+1,&\text{All-Correct},\\
0,&\text{Mixed},\\
-1,&\text{All-Wrong}.\end{cases} \tag{4}
```

In informed evaluation, the term $`\gamma_{j}d_{i}`$ captures how a
judge’s rating changes when peer responses are shown, compared with
blind evaluation of the same answer. A positive $`\gamma_{j}`$ means the
judge tends to give higher ratings when all peers are correct and lower
ratings when all peers are wrong. A negative $`\gamma_{j}`$ means the
opposite. When $`\gamma_{j}`$ is near zero, the judge shows little
difference in this pattern. Since the model does not check whether the
evaluated answer agrees with the peers, $`\gamma_{j}`$ measures the
judge’s response to peer context, not whether the judge follows the
peers.

The model estimates answer quality on a numerical scale, but these
numbers need reference points to be meaningful. We use the benchmark’s
correct answer and the targeted wrong answer as reference points, called
anchors. These anchors represent high and low answer quality. However,
they are useful only if judges can distinguish correct answers from
wrong ones. We therefore check whether judges can tell the two apart,
both with and without the correct answer provided. In the main analysis,
we use anchor ratings made with the correct answer shown and assign
quality values of +1.5 to the correct anchor and -1.5 to the wrong
anchor. A statistical model can produce stable estimates even when the
judges’ ratings do not accurately reflect answer quality. Full model
details are reported in Appendix C.

### 3.4. Datasets and Models

##### Datasets.

We use three question-answering benchmarks that are widely used in prior
work: TruthfulQA (Lin et al., 2022) (evaluating whether LLMs reproduce
common false beliefs), MMLU-Pro (Wang et al., 2024) (testing knowledge
and reasoning across a broad range of subjects), and ARC-Challenge
(Clark et al., 2018) (focusing on challenging science questions). We
sampled 500 questions from each dataset. Each question has a known
correct answer, allowing us to construct controlled peer responses that
endorse either the correct answer or a selected incorrect option.

##### Models.

We evaluate four open-weight LLMs: Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Yang et al.,
2024), Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 (Jiang et al., 2023; Mistral AI, ),
Gemma-2-9B-Instruct (Gemma Team, 2024), and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct
(Grattafiori et al., 2024). Each of these four models serves as both a
focal agent and a judge. We additionally evaluate two API judges, GPT-4o
and GPT-5.4-mini, on TruthfulQA only. All open-weight models are served
with vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023) using greedy decoding (temperature 0,
top-$`p`$ 1.0). The main corpus contains three generation runs, with
seeds 42, 43, and 44.

##### Sample Sizes.

Each benchmark yields 6,000 trials (500 questions $`\times`$ 4 focal
agents $`\times`$ 3 seeds). Per benchmark, this gives 6,000 Round 1
answers, 6,000 no-peer answers, 36,000 peer-presented answers (12,000
per peer mode, half with authority labels), and 1,000 anchor answers
(one known-correct and one known-wrong per question). The content-only
condition adds 6,000 answers per benchmark (2,000 per mode), each rated
blind only. Each open-weight judge gives 49,000 blind and 36,000
informed ratings per benchmark. The API judges rate the TruthfulQA
answers only.

Our first finding is that peer context changes the substantive position
expressed by the focal agent. Across all three benchmarks, false
endorsement is lowest after all-correct responses, higher after mixed
responses, and highest after all-wrong responses. On TruthfulQA, false
endorsement is 14.2% with no peers, 9.0% after all-correct responses,
and 32.6% after all-wrong responses (Figure 3). On MMLU-Pro and
ARC-Challenge, all-wrong peers likewise more than double false
endorsement relative to no peers (14.3% to 32.5% and 14.6% to 35.0%,
respectively). Full classifier results are reported in Appendix D.1.

Semantic similarity supports the same finding from a different angle.
Across all three benchmarks, answers revised after all-correct peer
responses are more similar to the correct answer than those revised
after all-wrong responses (Table 2). On TruthfulQA, for example,
similarity drops from 0.710 to 0.602. BERTScore F1 shows the same
pattern.

Because peer context changes the answer itself, a difference in ratings
across peer conditions could come from the answer, the judge, or both.
To test whether peer context also affects the judge, we next hold the
answer fixed and vary only what the judge sees.

### 4.2. Peer Context Changes Evaluations Differently Across Judges

judge-gamma

Figure 4. Judges respond to peer context in opposite directions. Each

Our second finding is that the same answer can receive different ratings
when the judge sees peer context, and judges respond in opposite
directions (Figure 4). On TruthfulQA, compared with blind evaluation,
Mistral-7B gives higher modeled ratings under all-correct peers and
lower ratings under all-wrong peers ($`\gamma_{j}=+0.637`$). Gemma-2-9B,
Llama-3.1-8B, GPT-4o, and GPT-5.4-mini show the opposite pattern, with
negative $`\gamma_{j}`$ values, while Qwen2.5-7B shows little
directional change ($`\gamma_{j}=-0.008`$). On MMLU-Pro and
ARC-Challenge, Mistral remains positive, Gemma and Llama negative, while
Qwen becomes positive (Appendix Table 9). These results show that peer
context can change the rating of the same answer, with effects varying
across judges.

The judges also differ substantially even when the peer context is
hidden. Under blind evaluation, agreement among the four open-weight
judges is low, with Krippendorff’s $`\alpha=0.235`$ computed from their
ratings of the same answers across the three benchmarks. This
disagreement shows that the judges do not use the five-point quality
scale interchangeably. Together, the two results show that judges differ
both in how they use the rating scale and in how they respond to peer
context, which is why our measurement model estimates both for each
judge.

### 4.3. The Evaluator’s Measurement Scale Must Be Audited

anchor-check

Figure 5. Judges better distinguish known-correct from known-wrong
answers under reference-augmented evaluation. Mean 1–5 judgment of the
known-correct and known-wrong anchor answers, pooled over the four
open-weight judges and three benchmarks. Anchor ratings made with the
correct answer define the latent scale; experimental answers are rated
without it. Numerical values are in Appendix Table 5.anchor-check

Our final finding concerns the scale on which the first two findings
rest: do the estimated scores meaningfully represent answer quality? To
establish an interpretable scale, we use answers whose correctness is
known from the benchmark. A correct answer should define the
high-quality end of the scale and a known incorrect answer should define
the low-quality end. However, this only works if the judges can reliably
distinguish the two. We therefore audit the anchors before relying on
them to define the scale.

Figure 5 shows that judges distinguish known-correct from known-wrong
answers far more clearly when they are given the correct answer. Without
the correct answer, the mean correct-answer rating is 3.91 and the mean
incorrect-answer rating is 2.32, a separation of 1.59 points on the
five-point scale. With the correct answer, the mean correct-answer
rating rises to 4.90 and the mean incorrect-answer rating falls to 1.60,
more than doubling the separation to 3.30 points. We therefore use the
anchor ratings made with the correct answer to identify the latent scale
in the primary analysis, while experimental answers are rated without
it.

This audit separates computational convergence from measurement
validity: a well-converged model does not guarantee a meaningful scale.
Convergence diagnostics tell us whether the model has stably estimated
the posterior implied by the observed ratings; the anchor audit tests
whether those ratings support the intended interpretation of the scale
as answer quality. Both checks are necessary for the latent estimates to
be useful.

The Content-Only and Peer-Presented conditions differ in three ways:
repetition, attribution to named peers, and authority cues. To estimate

As shown in Table 3, attribution accounts for the largest component of
the difference. Presenting the same content as coming from named people
lowers judges’ mean blind ratings of the final answers by 0.07 to 0.15

including zero on two of three benchmarks.

These results suggest that how the information is presented matters more
than how often it is repeated. However, the attribution component still
bundles several features, including names, peer framing, and the
apparent number of speakers, which our design cannot separate.

### 5.2. Robustness Checks

We test whether judges respond differently to answers produced by their
own model family. We refit the model after removing every rating in
which the judge and the focal agent come from the same model family. The
sign of every judge’s $`\gamma_{j}`$ is unchanged in all 12
judge–benchmark combinations. Full results are reported in Appendix D.3.

We also assess the convergence of the estimated judge-side effects
($`\gamma_{j}`$). For $`\gamma_{j}`$ in every reported fit, there are no
divergent transitions, $`\hat{R}\leq 1.008`$, the minimum bulk effective
sample size is 374, and the minimum tail effective sample size is 607.
Detailed diagnostics for each fit are reported in Appendix C.2.

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1. Summary of Findings

Our findings show that peer context affects both the agent being
evaluated and the judge evaluating it. For the focal agent, peer context
changed what its final answers claimed, not just how they were worded.
Wrong peers pulled answers toward the targeted wrong answer much more
than correct peers pulled them toward the correct answer. How the
content was presented also mattered: attributing the same content to
named people lowered the quality of the answers beyond the effect of the
content itself, and this drop came mainly from the attribution rather
than from repetition. For the judge, seeing the peer context changed the
rating of an identical answer, and judges changed in opposite
directions. Judges also disagreed with one another even without seeing
the peer context: their agreement on the same answers was low,
suggesting that they use the five-point scale differently. Finally, how
well judges separated known-correct from known-wrong answers depended on
whether they were given the correct answer.

These findings show that peer context enters the measurement twice: once
through the answer and once through the judge. As a result, a judgment
of a multi-agent system reflects not only the answer, but also what the
judge sees, which judge is used, and whether the rating scale has been
checked against known answers.

### 6.2. Design Implications for LLM-based Judges

As automated evaluations are increasingly used to compare AI systems and
to support claims about how well they work (Raji et al., 2022;
Hutchinson et al., 2022), we outline three design implications for using
LLM-based judges to evaluate multi-agent systems. Building on prior work
on AI trust and transparency (Liao et al., 2020; Qu et al., 2024; Qu et
al., 2025; Kim et al., 2024; Liao and Sundar, 2022; Liao and Vaughan,
2024), all three rest on transparent reporting for LLM-based judges:
what each judge saw, which judges were used, and how the rating scale
was checked.

#### 6.2.1. Context Matters for the Judge

First, evaluations should treat what the judge sees as part of the
evaluation protocol. In our blind/informed comparison, the same answer
received different ratings once peer context became visible. This
resembles social influence in human ratings, where a visible prior
rating shifts the ratings that follow (Muchnik et al., 2013), and
extends prior findings that LLM-based judges respond to majority and
authority cues (Wang et al., 2025; Hwang et al., 2025). In clinical
trials, what an outcome assessor may know is decided in advance (Schulz
and Grimes, 2002); in LLM-based evaluation, the judge’s input is often
left unreported. When two systems are evaluated under different
conditions, differences in their ratings may reflect the evaluation
setup rather than answer quality, and two studies of the same system
could reach different conclusions because one judge read the interaction
and the other did not. The same issue extends to training: when
judgments are used as rewards (Lambert et al., 2025), a judge whose
ratings depend on peer context may pass that dependence on to the system
it helps shape.

This does not mean that judges should never see the interaction.
Additional information may help a judge assess an answer, but it can
also introduce social influence that is unrelated to answer quality, a
distinction that also matters for sycophancy in LLMs (Atwell et al.,
2026). Evaluations could therefore report what information judges
receive as part of documenting how a system is evaluated (Mitchell et
al., 2019), keep this information comparable across systems, and compare
blind and informed evaluations of the same answers. Future LLM-based
judges could also make this choice explicit, for example by rating an
answer blind first and then reporting whether the interaction changes
the rating.

#### 6.2.2. Judge Choice Matters

Second, evaluations should treat the choice of judge as a methodological
decision rather than an implementation detail. Judges in our study
responded to peer context in opposite directions and agreed only weakly
even under blind evaluation. Prior work finds that judges may apply
different scoring standards (Sachdeva and van Nuenen, 2025) and that
models may behave differently when judging versus performing the same
task (Hong et al., 2026). Averaging judge ratings can conceal
disagreement, making comparisons appear robust while depending on the
judges included. This echoes findings that evaluation conclusions can
hinge on minor methodological choices (Khan et al., 2025). The concern
may be greater for subjective criteria, such as helpfulness or safety:
our tasks had known correct answers, yet judges still disagreed.

Evaluations could therefore report results for each judge and examine
whether conclusions hold across judges. Rather than searching for a
single best judge, they could treat judges as a panel whose disagreement
is itself informative, much as inter-rater agreement is reported for
human annotation. Reporting which judges were considered and used also
supports accountability: because judges can differ in direction, the
choice of judge can favor a desired conclusion, intentionally or not,
and auditing the evaluator (Costanza-Chock et al., 2022; Raji et al., 2020) should cover this choice. Since closed-source LLMs may release
different versions (Chen et al., 2024) and the judge behind a published
result may no longer behave the same way, documenting these metadata of
the judge would allow others to re-examine a result even after the judge
itself has changed.

#### 6.2.3. Measurement Validity Matters

Third, evaluations should validate the rating scale, not only the
statistical model. Reliable estimation does not guarantee that a score
measures the intended construct (Jacobs and Wallach, 2021; Wallach et
al., 2025). Our anchor audit shows that judges separated known-correct
from known-wrong answers far less clearly without the correct answer.
Without such a check, credible intervals and convergence diagnostics can
look precise, and readers may mistake this precision for validity. This
risk grows as LLM-based judges replace human annotation (Gilardi et al.,
2023), because the human ratings that could serve as reference points
may no longer be collected.

Evaluations could therefore check judge-based scores against independent
evidence, such as answers of known quality or a small set of human
ratings (Shankar et al., 2024; Bavaresco et al., 2025), which can also
be combined with judge ratings to obtain valid estimates (Angelopoulos
et al., 2023). Evaluation pipelines could build this by design: hold out
a small set of items whose quality is known or verified by humans,
report how well the judge separates them, and treat poor separation as a
reason not to trust the resulting scale.

### 6.3. Limitations and Future Work

Our peer context is simulated. While this allows for a controlled
experiment to isolate different effects, real multi-agent interactions
and open-ended tasks may produce different effects. Future work could
extend the blind/informed protocol to multi-round agent debates in which
the judge reads the full transcript. We also plan to test interventions
(e.g., instructing judges to ignore peer context) to see whether they
reduce judges’ sensitivity to peer context without discarding useful
information.

## 7. Conclusion

LLM-based judges increasingly evaluate multi-agent systems, where the
judge may see the interaction that produced an answer. Prior work has
studied how peer interaction changes agents’ answers and how judges are
biased by features of the answer, but not how the same peer context
affects both. We asked: when an LLM-based judge evaluates a multi-agent
system, does its judgment measure only the answer, or also the
interaction it sees? Our controlled experiment suggests the latter: peer
context changes what an agent says, and it also changes how judges rate
an identical answer, in directions that differ across judges. We also
find that a well-fitted statistical model of these judgments does not
guarantee a meaningful measure of answer quality. Our results suggest
that when LLMs evaluate other AI systems, the evaluator is part of the
experiment. We recommend that future evaluations compare judgments with
and without the context the judge sees, report results for each judge
separately, and validate scores against evidence of known quality.
Treating the judge as part of the experiment—rather than as a neutral
instrument—is a necessary step toward valid measurement about AI systems
that others can check and challenge.

### Positionality Statement

This work grew out of a research project that developed LLM-based judges
to rate the output of a multi-agent system, in which agents revise their
answers after seeing each other’s responses. In doing so, we realized
that the judges could also see, and might react to, these peer
responses, which made it unclear whether our measurements reflected the
agents or the judges. We therefore set out to separate these two
effects. We hope that our findings and recommendations will help
researchers and practitioners evaluate multi-agent systems more
transparently and use LLM-based judges with appropriate caution.

### Ethical Considerations Statement

This study does not involve human participants or collect personal data.
Our experiments use publicly available question-answering benchmarks and
LLMs for controlled generation and evaluation. We use TruthfulQA,
MMLU-Pro, and ARC-Challenge, together with four open-weight LLMs as well
as GPT-series models, in accordance with their licenses and intended
research use. A possible adverse impact is that evaluator sensitivity to
peer context could be exploited to favor a desired conclusion by
selectively choosing judges or evaluation conditions. We therefore
recommend transparent reporting of the information visible to each judge
and of judge-specific results.

## Appendix C Statistical Model and Estimation

### C.1. Prior Distributions

All shift parameters receive weakly informative unit-normal priors:
$`\eta`$, $`\eta_{\mathrm{regen}}`$ and $`\eta_{\mathrm{content}}`$
$`\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)`$, with
$`\sigma_{b},\sigma_{\theta}\sim\mathrm{HalfNormal}(1)`$. Here, $`\eta`$
is the Peer-Presented shift for each focal agent and peer mode,
$`\eta_{\mathrm{regen}}`$ is the No-Peer shift for each focal agent, and
$`\eta_{\mathrm{content}}`$ is the Content-Only shift for each focal
agent and peer mode. On the judge side,
$`\log\alpha_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.4)`$,
$`\beta_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)`$ and
$`\gamma_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.5)`$; the ordinal cutpoints are induced
by sorting $`\kappa_{\mathrm{raw}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)`$. Trial
baselines and answer-level deviations use a non-centered
parameterization, which is necessary for the sampler to traverse this
hierarchy at the scale of the pooled fit. Posteriors are drawn with NUTS
(Hoffman and Gelman, 2014) in NumPyro (Phan et al., 2019) with four
chains. The main fits and the fits without same-family ratings use 1,000
warmup and 2,000 sampling iterations per chain, target acceptance
probability $`0.90`$, and maximum tree depth $`7`$. The five-judge fit
uses 2,000 warmup and 2,000 sampling iterations per chain, target
acceptance probability $`0.95`$, and maximum tree depth $`10`$.

### C.2. Convergence Diagnostics
