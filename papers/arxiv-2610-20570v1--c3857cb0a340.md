###### Abstract

Brazilian Portuguese remains underserved by open-source language models,
and the few that are safety-aligned rarely document what did _not_ work
or validate the comparison with statistical significance. We present
Manacá-1B-Instruct-2, the second generation of the post-training
alignment of Manacá-1B (1.72 billion parameters, trained from scratch
for PT-BR), with an SFT corpus of 291,223 examples and a safety
alignment validated with the statistical rigor that the first generation
lacked. We document three DPO attempts, all with negative results,
including a case where the training reward curve reaches a perfect
margin (1.0) while free generation does not change at all, and we show
that safety again came from a dedicated safety-SFT (LLM-jp’s
_AnswerCarefully_ style), refined over three iterations until closing
the safety$`\times`$helpfulness frontier. We experimentally isolated the
composition variable of the training mix (fraction of general data, 10%
to 60%) and found a non-monotonic over-refusal curve, with a knee point
at 40–50%. The final model (v4) is compared against four external PT-BR
models on the same items, with Wilson confidence intervals and paired
McNemar tests corrected by Holm-Bonferroni: the floor of v4’s safety
confidence interval (94.1%) sits above the ceiling of all four
baselines. Along the way, we document a methodological finding of
general interest for LLM-judge evaluation: on two of six safety items in
MT-Bench-PT, the judge categorically refuses to assign a score,
regardless of the content of the evaluated response, silently reducing
the effective $`N`$ of that category in every historical report of the
project up to this work. We complement this with an n-gram
decontamination check between the training corpus and the evaluation
benchmarks, and a Chinchilla-anchored compute-budget table that explains
the observed difference in general capability as a pre-training scale
effect, not an alignment methodology effect. All code, raw data,
training and evaluation logs, and the scripts that produce every number
in this report are published so that every result can be recomputed.

## 1 Introduction

Open language models for Brazilian Portuguese have grown in number, but
the safety alignment of these models is rarely documented with the same
rigor as pre-training: negative results tend to be left out, small $`N`$
for safety probes rules out any significance test, and evaluation by an
LLM judge is treated as a black box trusted by construction.
Manacá-1B-Instruct \[1\] (first generation) had already adopted LLM-jp’s
_AnswerCarefully_ method \[2\] to align safety via dedicated SFT, after
on-policy DPO failed to reverse the model’s compliance with harmful
requests, but with probes of 16/34 items, insufficient for any paired
test.

This report documents the second generation (instruct-2), which closes
these methodological gaps rather than simply scaling the model. Our goal
is not scale, it is rigor: the same LLM-jp method, but with probes large
enough for statistical significance, a controlled scan isolating the
composition variable of the training mix, an audit of the evaluation
process itself (the LLM judge, corpus decontamination, compute fairness
across baselines), and the publication of everything, including three
DPO attempts that did not work.

Our contributions are as follows.

- •
  A reproducible, statistically validated safety alignment. v4 is
  compared against four external PT-BR baselines on the same 218/184
  items, with Wilson intervals and paired McNemar corrected by
  Holm-Bonferroni, not only against the project’s own history.
- •
  Three DPO attempts documented as negative results, including the
  lesson that the DPO training metric (reward margin) can become perfect
  without changing free generation at all.
- •
  An empirical finding on how to instantiate the model: prompt template
  _family_ matters far more than the phrasing within it (generation
  confidence drops by half outside the trained Alpaca-PT format), and
  asking the model to admit uncertainty does not make it verbalize
  doubt, it only swaps one confabulation for another.
- •
  A controlled composition scan of the safety-SFT mix (10–60% general
  data), isolating a single variable and revealing a non-monotonic
  over-refusal curve with a knee point at 40–50%.
- •
  A methodological finding on LLM-judge evaluation: categorical refusal
  to score safety items, regardless of the evaluated response, which
  silently reduces the effective $`N`$, corrected retroactively in eight
  historical project reports.
- •
  An n-gram decontamination check between the training corpus and the
  evaluation benchmarks, with manual audit of the findings.
- •
  A Chinchilla-anchored compute-budget table \[6\], quantifying the
  pre-training asymmetry across the five compared models.

## 2 Related Work

This work builds on three lines of research. The first is the
development of open LLMs native to Portuguese: Manacá-1B-Instruct
itself, first generation \[1\], whose evolution this report documents;
the Tucano family \[3, 4\], which includes both models trained from
scratch in Portuguese (Tucano-1b1, Tucano-2b4) and a
continual-pretraining model on Qwen3 (Tucano2-qwen); and TeenyTinyLlama
\[5\]. All four are used as external baselines in Section 4.4. The
safety-alignment method followed here, dedicated SFT in
_AnswerCarefully_ style, comes from the LLM-jp project \[2\], an
open-LLM initiative for Japanese whose evaluation practice also informs
the design of this report.

The second line is the relationship between pre-training compute budget
and final model capacity, formalized by Hoffmann et al. \[6\]
(Chinchilla) and used in Section 4.7 to separate the effect of scale
from the effect of alignment methodology when comparing models with
vastly different pre-training budgets.

The third is the reliability of LLM judges, relevant to the finding in
Section 4.5. The founding MT-Bench paper \[7\] characterizes positional
bias, verbosity bias, self-enhancement bias, and limited
reasoning/mathematical ability as the known flaws of the judge-LLM
paradigm. More recent work addresses adjacent problems: Eiras et al.
\[8\] show that safety judges are fragile to changes in the style of the
evaluated response and to adversarial attacks that trick them into
classifying harmful content as safe; Pasch \[9\] documents that LLM
judges score ethics-motivated candidate refusals more favorably than
human evaluators (_moderation bias_); Vishnubhotla et al. \[10\]
document high disagreement across different safety judges, varying by
criterion and harm category; and Zhang \[11\], using Claude Sonnet 4-6
as the sole judge over 400 HarmBench behaviors, shows that the judge’s
prompt choice shifts the measured harmful-response rate by up to 24.2
percentage points. None of these works describes the specific mechanism
reported in Section 4.5: the judge itself triggering its API-level
safety layer (stop_reason=refusal, officially documented as a normal
HTTP 200 response, not an error \[12\]) when instructed to score a
response to a sensitive prompt, silently removing the item from the
denominator instead of producing a score.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Model and Instruction Corpus

Manacá-1B-Instruct-2 fully inherits the architecture of the Manacá-1B
base model (Table 1): a 24-layer Llama-style decoder-only transformer,
model dimension 2048, 8192 SwiGLU FFN, 32 attention heads with 8
key-value groups (GQA), RoPE, RMSNorm, and learned biases in all linear
layers. The tokenizer is a 64k-token SentencePiece (nmt_nfkc_cf), which
makes the model lowercase by construction. Post-training is a tune on
top of these weights: full fine-tuning (SFT) followed by safety-SFT via
LoRA.

| ---------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Parameters                   | 1,722,951,680 ($`\approx`$1.72B)                                  |
| Layers / model dim. / FFN    | 24 / 2048 / 8192 (SwiGLU)                                         |
| Heads / KV groups / head dim | 32 / 8 (GQA) / 64                                                 |
| Position / norm / bias       | RoPE ($`\theta{=}5{\times}10^{5}`$) / RMSNorm / all linear layers |
| Tokenizer                    | SentencePiece, 64k (nmt_nfkc_cf)                                  |
| Context / precision          | 4096 / bfloat16                                                   |
| Chat template                | Alpaca-PT (\### Instruç~ao: / \### Resposta:)                     |

SFT uses full fine-tuning on the Manacá-1B base, 291,223 training
examples and 3,000 validation examples (split before training, never
merged), for 3 epochs with load_best_model_at_end (selects the
checkpoint with the lowest eval_loss, not the last one). The actual
eval_loss curve, measured every 500 steps, fell monotonically across the
6 recorded points: 1.3474 $`\to`$ 1.2661 $`\to`$ 1.2359 $`\to`$ 1.2119

epochs, which validates the decision to extend beyond the 2 epochs
faithful to LLM-jp used in the first generation. train/loss follows the
same trend (from 3.14 at step 10 to the 1.05–1.15 range at the end),
with no train$`\times`$eval divergence. The raw curves (loss, gradient
norm, learning rate) are published with the repository, and Figure 3
shows them in full.

### 3.2 Model Instantiation Protocol

During SFT, we tested live (on CPU, over intermediate checkpoints) a
practical question: what is the best way to _call_ the model so the
answer comes out better, and why (results in Section 4.1). We tested two
intermediate checkpoints of manaca-1b-instruct-2-full (steps 2,000 and
2,500 of 3,411, both _before_ safety-SFT), greedy decoding with
repetition_penalty=1.3 and no_repeat_ngram_size=3, over a fixed set of
factual/enumeration questions (photosynthesis, the founding of Flamengo,
Brazil’s last president, Fermat’s theorem, the planets of the solar
system, Brazilian states). As a _confidence_ metric we used the average
per-token probability under the model’s own softmax distribution, a
proxy for how “within the training distribution” the generation is, not
a measure of factual correctness.

### 3.3 Mandatory-Core Protocol

We compare v4 (the approved safety-SFT checkpoint, results in
Section 4.4) against four external PT-BR models that a serious
evaluation cannot leave out: TeenyTinyLlama-460m-Chat \[5\],
Tucano-1b1-Instruct and Tucano-2b4-Instruct \[3\], and
Tucano2-qwen-1.5B-Instruct \[4\], under the same harness, on the same
items. Two fairness corrections were applied before running: (i) each
baseline uses its own _chat template_ where available, rather than
forcing Manacá’s Alpaca-PT format; (ii) we instrumented
n_tokens_generated/truncated per response, because Manacá’s 64k
vocabulary versus the baselines’ 32–49k means a shared --max_new_tokens
is not an equal length budget; truncation was confirmed real (up to 32%
at 512 tokens) and fixed by regenerating all 5 models at 1,024 tokens.

The safety (218 held-out items) and over-refusal (184 items) probes were
expanded from 16/34 to 218/184 specifically to allow for narrow Wilson
intervals and paired McNemar tests; at $`N{=}16`$, differences such as
“93.8% versus 75%” do not survive non-overlapping confidence intervals.
Statistics implemented with no external dependencies: Wilson interval
(95%), exact paired McNemar, Holm-Bonferroni correction for multiple
comparisons.

### 3.4 Decontamination and Compute Budget

No evaluation benchmark is used by name in the training corpus
(Section 3.1), but the large-scale _web-scrape_ sources (GigaVerbo-v2,
MADLAD-400, FineWeb-2, HPLT2, ClassiCC-PT, LegalPT) may have captured
them incidentally. To check this (results in Section 4.6), we compared
exact 13-gram textual overlap (the same threshold used by GPT-3/Llama)
between the 15,026 items of the 11 evaluation benchmarks (the 7 from the
mandatory core via HF plus 4 local ones) and the 294,223 lines of the
training corpus on disk.

To quantify the pre-training budget asymmetry across the compared models
(results in Section 4.7), we anchor to Chinchilla’s compute-optimal
point \[6\] ($`D_{\text{optimal}}\approx 20N`$) and pre-training FLOPs
($`C\approx 6ND`$), reconciling $`N`$ (parameters) and $`D`$ (processed
tokens) from each baseline’s configuration YAML and official model card.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Robustness to Instantiation Templates

A first test compared five phrasing variants within the same Alpaca-PT
family (original preamble, no preamble, requesting precision, requesting
detail, _few-shot_ with two correct examples): five different
confabulations, zero correct answers. This already signals this
section’s central conclusion: rephrasing the instruction _within_ the
trained format does not recover missing factual knowledge.

A second, more decisive test compared three templates from the Alpaca-PT
family (the training one) against three templates from _chat_ families
the Manacá tokenizer never saw as special tokens, ChatML (OpenAI/Qwen
standard), Llama-2-chat, and Vicuna. Figure 4 shows the result: average
confidence of $`0.656`$ for the Alpaca-PT family versus $`0.347`$ for
the chat families, less than half.

Figure 4: Average generation confidence (average per-token probability)
by template, 4 factual/enumeration questions, checkpoint-2500
(pre-safety-SFT). Gray dots are the 4 individual questions; the bar is
the average. T1/T6/T7 (Alpaca-PT family, trained) sit consistently above
T8/T9/T10 (chat families never seen by the tokenizer).

And qualitatively it is worse than the number alone suggests: it is not
just “a wrong answer”, it is a complete derailment of topic. In ChatML,
the question about photosynthesis received an answer about a literal
_photo_ (“look at the photo above, it shows a plant…”), the model
confused the very subject of the question. The same family, asked about
Brazilian states, drifted into computer hardware (“processor, RAM, hard
drive, motherboard…”). In Vicuna, the question about Flamengo (a
Brazilian football club) ended in a hallucinated entire fan-forum
thread, with a player name, “site visitor” comments, and even a made-up
URL (flaestatisticas.com/…); the question about states turned into a
tourism-chatbot conversation about visiting São Paulo, without ever
listing a single state. In Llama-2-chat, one of the answers was nothing
but forum formatting tags (\[b\]\[color=#0896a5\]…) with no content at
all. None of the six responses outside the Alpaca-PT family was useful,
it is not “answers worse”, it is a complete loss of the thread.

A third test, on checkpoint-2000, isolated a different mechanism:
instead of changing the template’s _format_, we added an explicit
instruction asking the model to verbalize doubt instead of making things
up (“If you are not sure about specific dates, names, or numbers,
clearly say you are not sure instead of making up an answer”), keeping
the rest of the Alpaca-PT template identical.

| Question             | Base conf. | Abstention conf. | Did it abstain?                                   |
| -------------------- | ---------- | ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Fermat’s theorem     | 0.49       | 0.35             | No (Euclid/Pythagoras $`\to`$ “Pascal’s theorem”) |
| Flamengo’s founding  | 0.66       | 0.47             | No (1924 $`\to`$ 04/26/1985)                      |
| Last president       | 0.62       | 0.63             | No (Fernando Collor $`\to`$ Getúlio Vargas)       |
| Solar-system planets | 0.47       | 0.44             | No (incorrect list in both)                       |

Table 3: Explicitly asking the model to admit uncertainty
(checkpoint-2000, same 4 questions, same decoding). In none of the 4
cases did the model produce an abstention response, it swapped one
confabulation for another, usually with _lower_ confidence.

In 4 of 4 cases (Table 3), explicitly instructing the model to admit
uncertainty did not produce a single abstention response, the model
swapped one confabulation for another different confabulation, with
_lower_ confidence in 3 of the 4 cases (the sharpest drop was on
Fermat’s theorem, where “Pythagorean theorem, discovered by Euclid”
turned into a nonsensical mathematical passage attributed to “Pascal’s
theorem”). This confirms, through a mechanism different from the
template test, the same boundary: the model cannot introspect and
verbalize its own epistemic uncertainty via prompt instruction, missing
factual knowledge is a property of what was seen in pre-training
(quantified in orders of magnitude in Section 4.7), not something the
prompt can work around or make the model recognize it lacks.

The result of the three tests converges on a simple rule already
reflected in this repository’s
[publish/hf-model-card.md](https://publish/hf-model-card.md): always
instantiate v4 with the exact training-time Alpaca-PT template, word for
word –

> preambulo = "Abaixo está uma instruç~ao que descreve uma  
> tarefa. Escreva uma resposta que atenda adequadamente ao pedido."  
> prompt = f"{preambulo}\n\n### Instruç~ao:\n{instrucao}\n\n###
> Resposta:\n"

_(the literal Portuguese strings above are the exact ones the model was
trained on – “Below is an instruction that describes a task. Write a
response that adequately fulfills the request.” / \### Instruction: /
\### Response: – and must not be translated or reworded when calling the
model; see the discussion below.)_

– never a _chat_ template (ChatML, Llama-2-chat, Vicuna, or similar):
the tokenizer lacks the special tokens of these formats, and the model
treats the text as structural noise, not turn signaling. The model also
has no chat_template configured in the tokenizer, anyone calling via
apply_chat_template() without adjusting this manually gets the wrong
format by default. Paraphrasing the preamble, even with synonyms
(“correctly” instead of “adequately”), does not help either: the model
learned to associate _this fixed text_, not a free-form meaning, with
the expected response format, deviating from it takes the model out of
the distribution it learned, with no accuracy gain (the word
“adequately” in the template governs adherence to the genre/format of
the request, not factual correctness, the same role “adequately” plays
in the original English Alpaca).

This fragility also shapes an architectural decision for any future web
search or RAG extension: search must always happen automatically and
outside the model’s control (search $`\to`$ inject the result into the
same standard Alpaca-PT template as always $`\to`$ the model just
summarizes/answers using the given context), rather than letting Manacá
itself decide when to search and formulate the query, that would be a
tool-calling format it has never seen, and it would fall into the same
trap ChatML/Vicuna just confirmed here.

Explicit limitation of this section: the three tests were measured on
intermediate checkpoints of instruct-2-full, _before_ safety-SFT, not on
the final v4. The finding carries over to v4 because safety-SFT
(Section 4.3) is a LoRA continuation on these same base weights, with
the same Alpaca-PT template (it never introduces a different prompt
format), but the specific confidence figures were not re-measured on the
final merged checkpoint. There was also no repetition across multiple
seeds nor more than 4–6 questions per test, this is strong
qualitative/exploratory evidence, not a statistical benchmark like the
one in Section 4.4.

### 4.2 Three DPO Attempts, All Negative

Before safety-SFT, DPO was attempted three times across the project:
twice in the first generation (results carried over, not re-run here)
and once in instruct-2, and none reversed the model’s compliance with
harmful requests. We record all three because the pattern that emerges
from them is, by itself, the project’s most important methodological
lesson.

\#1, off-policy, GigaVerbo pairs (1^(st) generation). Preference pairs
extracted from GigaVerbo’s harmful subset, with no generation by the
model itself. It did not improve; it over-optimized.

\#2, on-policy, faithful to llm-jp-dpo (1^(st) generation). Pairs
generated by the model itself (good refusal vs. the observed harmful
compliance), $`\beta=0.1`$. The reward curve reached a perfect margin
(1.0, accuracy $`\approx`$1.0), and yet free generation did not change
at all: the adapter, loaded without merging (DPO’s real, unrounded
effect), complied 0/4 even on its own training prompts.

| Round                          | Steps | rewards/chosen  | Margin           | Accuracy (training) | Held-out safety |
| ------------------------------ | ----- | --------------- | ---------------- | ------------------- | --------------- |
| LR $`5{\times}10^{-7}`$, 2 ep. | 24    | $`\approx`$0.00 | $`\approx`$0.006 | 0.56                | — (no effect)   |
| LR $`1{\times}10^{-6}`$, 3 ep. | 153   | +0.85           | 1.03             | $`\approx`$1.0      | 0/16            |

Table 4: Attempt \#2 (on-policy): two rounds with $`\beta{=}0.1`$. The
second has near-perfect training metrics, yet free generation stays at
0/16 held-out refusal, the divergence between training metric and actual
behavior that motivates this section’s lesson.

\#3, offline, third-party pairs (instruct-2, this report). On
[manaca-1b-instruct-2-full](https://manaca-1b-instruct-2-full), using
[Polygl0t/gigaverbo-v2-preferences](https://Polygl0t/gigaverbo-v2-preferences)
($`\approx`$28.4k harmless/harmful pairs, Apache-2.0) and
[nicholasKluge/reward-aira-dataset](https://nicholasKluge/reward-aira-dataset).
Same conclusion: this was not what changed the model’s safety.

The general lesson, valid beyond this project: the DPO training metric
(margin/probability ratio under _teacher-forcing_) measures the decision
over two specific sequences, not the decision of the first token in free
generation (greedy or sampled), which is what actually determines the
observed behavior. When the model’s _prior_ (here, “comply”) is strong
and the dataset is small, DPO can train perfectly and change behavior by
zero, never trust training margin/accuracy as a sign of success without
validating against real free generation.

### 4.3 Safety-SFT: Alignment Across Three Iterations

Safety again came from a dedicated safety-SFT, _AnswerCarefully_ style
\[2\]: refusal as a direct cross-entropy target, mixed with examples of
helping benign requests (so as not to become a blind refuser) and
general data (to preserve broad helpfulness). LoRA $`r{=}32`$,
$`\alpha{=}64`$, 3 epochs, on manaca-1b-instruct-2-full.

It took three actual training rounds to close the
safety$`\times`$helpfulness frontier (Table 5, small 16/34-item probes
used during iteration; v4’s final large-$`N`$ validation comes in
Section 4.4). Increasing the harmful seeds (50 $`\to`$ 465, generating
1,539 refusal examples with $`k_{\text{refuse}}{=}4`$) while keeping
N_GENERAL fixed in absolute terms (v2) nearly doubled over-refusal: the
relative share of general data in the mix fell from $`\approx`$49% to
10.6%, and the model became an over-refuser. Correcting N_GENERAL to
keep the proportion (v3) fixed most of the over-refusal, but still left
some pro-social cases (“helping a friend in crisis”) mistakenly refused;
also scaling the helpfulness side, with oversampling of gold crisis

The v2/v3/v4 points already suggested that mix composition, specifically
the fraction of general data, matters more than the absolute volume of
any single component, but this clue came from hypothesis-guided trial
and error, not a controlled experiment. We therefore decided to isolate
the question cleanly: keeping everything else fixed (1,539 deterministic
refusal examples, 280 helpful ones, the same training recipe, the same
base model), we varied only the fraction of general data in the mix from
10% to 60%, in 10-percentage-point steps, six independent training
rounds.

Figure 5 shows the result. Safety saturates at 100% up to 30% general
data and only gives way from 40% on (93.8% $`\to`$ 87.5% at 50% $`\to`$
93.8% at 60%, with $`N{=}16`$ per point, the oscillation across
40/50/60% is not conclusive on its own). Overall over-refusal drops
sharply and monotonically from 10% to 50% (32.4% $`\to`$ 5.9%), but
rises again at 60% (11.8%): the curve is not monotonic. At $`N{=}34`$
per point this is also within the range of sampling noise, but it is
consistent enough (the same pattern in both over-refusal subgroups,
benign and sensitive-legitimate) not to dismiss as pure noise. The
40–50% range dominates the extremes on both axes at once: by a combined
metric (harmonic mean of safety and $`1{-}`$ over-refusal), the three
central points sit close together, 40% (92.5), 30% (92.1), 50% (90.7),
within what this sample size can distinguish, with 40% as the
recommended point: safety near the ceiling (93.8%) with over-refusal
already low (8.8%), without the extra cost seen at 60%.

The uncontrolled v2/v3/v4 points used a different helpfulness
composition (145 examples, no crisis oversampling) and so are not
directly point-by-point comparable to this scan, but the pattern holds:
v2 (10.6% general) had 50% over-refusal, consistent with the curve’s
left extreme; v3/v4 ($`\approx`$48.5% general) fall within the knee
range identified here, which explains why v4 worked well without having
been designed with this curve in hand, the proportion landed near the
optimum through hypothesis-guided trial and error, and is now confirmed
by controlled experiment.

Explicit limitations of this scan: statistical power ($`N{=}16`$/34 per
point, the non-monotonicity at 60% could be a real effect or noise, this
sample cannot tell); a single seed/round per point (training variance
not measured, may be partly confounded with the composition effect); and
the curve was measured only on manaca-1b-instruct-2-full, it is unknown
whether the same shape (or the same knee point) holds at another model
scale. Probes of 150–300 items per category, with Wilson CIs and paired
McNemar, are the next step before claiming the knee point as a robust
result rather than merely exploratory.

### 4.5 An LLM-Judge Failure Mode: Categorical Score Refusal

While judging the mandatory core’s MT-Bench-PT, we discovered that the
LLM judge (Claude) categorically refuses to assign a score on two of the
six items in the “safety” category (safety_1, safety_2), via the API’s
stop_reason=refusal, regardless of the content of the evaluated
candidate’s response. This silently reduces that category’s effective
$`N`$ from 6 to 4: the aggregate keeps being computed over the items
that _do_ receive a score, but nothing in the original report flagged
that two of the six prompts were never actually judged, for any model.

Through a systematic search (_grep_) for this signature across every
judging report ever published in the project’s history, we verified that
this categorical refusal affects the eight historical files of
MT-Bench-PT judged up to this work, a systemic finding, not specific to
this round. We fixed the presentation (not the underlying data, which
was already correctly aggregated) in code (bench/mtbench_pt/report.py,
which now shows $`(n{=}X)`$ per cell and a per-model judge-refusal count
section) and in the already-published artifacts. As discussed in
Section 2, this specific mechanism, the judge itself categorically
refusing, is not described in the related work we reviewed.

The lesson generalizes: when an LLM judge is used to score sensitive
items (safety, potentially harmful content), the judge’s own refusal is
a possible outcome of the evaluation process, not only of the evaluated
model, and must be counted and reported as such, not silently absorbed
into the denominator. We recommend that any LLM-judge evaluation
protocol over sensitive categories explicitly record the judge’s own
refusal rate per item, not only the average score.

### 4.6 Decontamination

We found overlap in 73 items (0.49% of the total evaluated), zero on the
benchmarks of the alignment work itself (safety, over-refusal,
MT-Bench-PT, IFEval-PT). We manually audited the full text of the 73
cases and classified them into four categories: legal boilerplate
(26/73, statute name, court digest, law preamble, identical by
definition wherever cited); indirect republication of past
ENEM/OAB/BLUEX exams on prep-school websites via _web scrape_ (23/73,
several with the exam year explicit in the training text); canonical
culture/literature coincidence (5/73); and real dataset overlap at
sentence/paragraph level (19/73, FaQuAD-NLI collapses into a single
source paragraph shared with ExtraGLUE-QNLI, tested 16 different ways by
the benchmark; ASSIN2-RTE reused in extraglue_stsb with a different
label). The single most concrete case: one OAB item has the entire
hypothetical fact pattern (fictitious names) reproduced almost word for
word in a synthetic instruction, a real, if isolated, leak.

The scale is small and mostly non-informative for the benchmark’s
multiple-choice task (most synthetic training instructions ask for a
summary/explanation, not the correct option). It does not invalidate the
mandatory-core results, but it is documented as a transparent
limitation, consistent with what any LLM trained on broad Portuguese web
crawl and evaluated on ENEM/OAB/BLUEX faces.

## 5 Conclusion

Manacá-1B-Instruct-2 shows that an honest, fully reproducible safety
alignment is a useful contribution to natural language processing for
Brazilian Portuguese, even at a modest model scale. Measured with paired
statistical significance against four external baselines, the final
model dominates on safety and competes evenly on general capability with
peers of the same compute budget. The methodological findings, the
divergence between DPO’s training metric and actual behavior, the
fragility to instantiation templates outside the trained format, the
non-monotonic composition curve of safety-SFT, and the LLM judge’s
categorical score refusal, generalize beyond this specific project. We
hope the published model, the reproducible protocol, and the discipline
of also documenting what did not work help the community bring safety
evaluation in Portuguese, and in small LLMs generally, to a measurable
standard.

It is worth stating this work’s scope with the same discipline used in
every section above. Safety is not 100%: despite being statistically
robust (97.2%, $`N{=}218`$), the true measured harmful-compliance rate
is 0.46% (1/218), ambiguously phrased requests can still get through.
Over-refusal persists on a small fraction of benign requests (9.2%),
statistically indistinguishable from three of the four external
baselines. In the pro-social case, the model may refuse requests to help
someone in crisis, where the right thing is to help and point to a
crisis line (in Brazil, CVV 188), mitigated via oversampling of gold
examples, not eliminated. General capability sits below PT-BR models
with larger-scale pre-training (Section 4.7), a compute effect, not a
methodology one. The multiple-choice exams (ENEM/OAB/BLUEX) sit at
chance level, a known limit of broad factual knowledge in natively PT-BR
$`\sim`$1B models. The composition scan remains at $`N{=}16`$/34 per
point and a single round per point, treat the knee point as exploratory,
not definitive, until the sample is expanded. Validation by a second
judge from a different model family (outside the evaluated LLM) remains
blocked by API budget in this work. Research model; not a substitute for
professional, legal, or medical advice.

The natural next steps are to expand, not redesign. The composition scan
(Section 4.3) needs probes of 150–300 items per point, with paired
Wilson/McNemar, before claiming the knee point as a robust result. A
second LLM judge from a different family would close this project’s
publication checklist’s most serious remaining gap (item 2). A
literature review with full-text access (outside this work environment’s
network restrictions) would help confirm whether the categorical
judge-refusal finding (Section 4.5) is indeed novel before formally
claiming so. Finally, the same controlled scan could be repeated at
another model scale to test whether the 40–50% knee point is a property
of the method or specific to this scale.
