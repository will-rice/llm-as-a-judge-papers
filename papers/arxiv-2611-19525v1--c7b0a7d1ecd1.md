# Objectify, Don’t Delete: Complaint-Derived Evaluation of Ambient Clinical Notes

Nikita Gupta, Narayanan Unny Edakunni, Vikram Okaly,
Affiliation: Suranjana Datta,Navya Penubadi, Affiliation: Karl Eric
Swanson,Siddharth Sharma Affiliation: Suki AI, California, USA

###### Abstract

Evaluating the quality of AI-generated clinical notes presents a
fundamental challenge: standard metrics like ROUGE measure lexical
similarity to reference notes, while expert checklists encode what
committees believe matters. Neither captures what clinicians actually
complain about. Recent work has shown that evaluation criteria can be
mined from user feedback and filtered to criteria that LLM judges can
reliably enforce. We argue that this is where much of the value is lost.
The criteria that fail enforceability are disproportionately the ones
clinicians raise most often, because high-frequency complaints are
stated as several questions glued together. No yes/no judgment can grade
such composites as posed. We introduce an objectification step that
rewrites such criteria into sets of scoped, determinate decisions. We
demonstrate the approach through an evaluation pipeline that mines
criteria from complaints, objectifies them, and deploys LLM judges
against both planted defects and production notes. Across eleven defects
planted into ACI-bench reference notes, objectified judges localize
violations with median recall of 0.95, while reference metrics change by
at most 0.037. Given the complaint as one broad question, the same
judges still find every planted defect, but they also flag 70% of the
unperturbed reference notes. Splitting that complaint into one
section-pair question per call raises Youden’s $`J`$ from 0.30 to 0.72;
with prompt effort matched, the per-check gain is 0.19–0.26 on three
models, and the single broad question remains the better note-level
screen. We release ACI-Defect, a perturbation benchmark;
clinician-reviewed instance-level labels showing quality gaps in the
ACI-bench gold standard (32 of 40 notes contain hallucinations; 40 of 40
omit discussed facts); and grounding-judge verdicts. On 1,446 production
notes, mean instance-rate burden falls at every clinician-rating step
($`\rho{=}{-}0.15`$). The association is small, and a named complaint
does not track its mapped judge. Defect counts have to be kept per span
rather than collapsed into one pass/fail bit per note.

## 1 Introduction

AI-powered ambient clinical documentation systems transcribe a
patient-clinician encounter and draft a structured clinical note. They
are now deployed at scale. As with any AI-generated content, it is
essential that the quality and clinical safety of the generated notes be
rigorously monitored. Manual review of these notes does not scale, has
high inter-rater variance, and cannot be run on every production note
within a short span of time. Hence, focus has shifted to automated
methods of measuring quality, one of which is by comparing a note to a
gold note (Yim et al., 2023), termed reference-based measurement. This
form of measurement fails due to the subjectivity in constructing a gold
note. There is not just one perfect gold note. Two clinicians
documenting the same encounter produce materially different, equally
acceptable write-ups. Lexical and embedding similarity to a single
reference is the protocol followed generally in practice (as
demonstrated in ACI-bench (Yim et al., 2023) which reports ROUGE (Lin,
2004), BERTScore (Zhang et al., 2020), BLEURT (Sellam et al., 2020), and
MedCon (Van Veen et al., 2024)). However, while it penalizes legitimate
stylistic variations, it remains insensitive to clinically harmful
errors that barely change $`n`$-gram overlap, such as a fabricated
normal exam finding or an Assessment sentence leaking into the History
of Present Illness.

Alternatively, instruments such as PDQI-9 (Stetson et al., 2012),
PDSQI-9 (Croxford et al., 2025b), QNOTE (Burke et al., 2014), and
similar checklists measure the quality of notes taking into account what
is clinically relevant, and measure the impact on clinical safety.
Measuring these metrics on clinical notes often requires manual
examination to carefully assess the quality along different dimensions.
These measures tend to assess the quality at a macroscopic level,
judging the note as a whole rather than its constituents. This tends to
introduce subjectivity into the measurement. There are systems (Croxford
et al., 2025a) that try to automate the measurement of these metrics
using LLM-as-a-judge, but the resultant judge also tends to be
subjective and non-deterministic to a large extent. The other
shortcoming of these expert metrics is that they are agreed upon by a
group of experts focusing exclusively on the quality of the notes. In
practice, users care less about macroscopic scores than about specific
deficiencies that force them to edit the note, which adds to burnout.
Table 6 (Appendix A) makes the gap concrete. Recent work (Zhou et al., 2025) mines metrics from user feedback but discards many of the most
common pain points because they are composite (Table 1).

In this paper, we address these challenges with a methodology that
covers the lifecycle of automated measurement of note quality: (1) mine
the predominant user complaints; (2) break them down into metrics for
the note and its constituents (objectification); (3) judge those metrics
with an LLM; and (4) use an LLM–human adjudication loop to correct the
labels and to separate a judge error from a human error.

## 2 Related Work and Contributions

In this section, we analyze prior work and list our contributions over
and above the state of the art.

Feedback-derived evaluation criteria: Zhou et al. (2025) turn 22K
encounters of clinician free-text feedback and ratings from a deployed
ambient scribe into yes/no checklists, refined by de-duplication,
applicability tagging, LLM-enforceability unit tests, and beam-search
subset selection. They score notes with an LLM judge and train a
logistic regression on binarized ratings using checklist questions as
features. We treat this pipeline as the starting point and address a
shortcoming of their enforceability-filtering step. In their work, they
discard user feedback that cannot be turned into a yes/no question for
the LLM to judge. This forgoes the chance to create judges for
potentially impactful quality gaps. We instead break such feedback down
into criteria that can be judged objectively by an LLM judge. We call
this the _objectification_ step. Examples are shown in Figure 1. Outside
the clinical domain, SPUR (Lin et al., 2024) induces satisfaction
rubrics from user data and uses rubric scores as classifier features.
Feedback-to-Rubrics (Yoshida et al., 2026) and PARL (Qiu et al., 2026)
induce and iteratively refine rubrics from comments and user history.
Both SPUR and Zhou et al. (2025) derive features from the user’s own
words or from checklist questions about those words, and therefore
cannot score an item for which no feedback exists.

LLM-as-a-judge for clinical text: PDSQI-9 (Croxford et al., 2025b) and
its LLM-as-a-judge instantiation (Croxford et al., 2025a) report high
agreement with expert raters on summarization quality. Checklist-based
judging (CheckEval, RocketEval, HypoEval (Lee et al., 2025; Wei et al.,
2025; Li et al., 2026)) improves reliability over single holistic
scores. However, these judges mimic human evaluators on macroscopic
characteristics of quality rather than on the localized constituents of
the note. We instead score those constituents and aggregate them into a
note-level view. Evaluating the judge itself is a separate problem:
Zheng et al. (2023) show that LLM judges disagree with humans in
systematic ways, and Shankar et al. (2024) show that an LLM-written
rubric can look reliable while failing the people it is meant to stand
in for. We therefore test localization on planted spans and treat the
production rating as a weak external check, not as validation of the
judge.

Disagreement and label noise: Concord (Bliss et al., 2026) uses
inter-judge agreement for disagreement-driven triage, multi-LLM
disagreement has been used to detect human annotation errors in clinical
free-text (Wittlinger et al., 2026), and label-error detection with LLM
ensembles is an active research topic (Nahum et al., 2025). Our
observation is that an interactive LLM–human adjudication loop serves
two ends: (1) it corrects the labeled corpus where the judge is right
(Table 2), and (2) it localizes the judge’s error to a cause specific
enough to improve the prompt where the human is right. We demonstrate
the first directly and use the second to specify judge revisions (we do
not report a re-measured judge after revision).

Generation benchmarks: ACI-bench (Yim et al., 2023) is the largest
public corpus of full encounter dialogues paired with expert notes, and
the de facto generation testbed (MEDIQA-Chat / MEDIQA-SUM). PriMock57
(Papadopoulos Korfiatis et al., 2022) and MTS-Dialog (Ben Abacha et al., 2023) are related but either much smaller or snippet-level. In this
paper, we evaluate ACI-bench using human experts and AI judges and
release the labels. We also release a dataset with planted quality
defects for evaluating note-quality judges.

## 3 Method

This section describes how we go from user feedback to a set of
evaluators that judge note quality (Figure 2).

### 3.1 Rubric induction and judgeability triage

Themes are clustered from clinician comments on generated notes. We do
not claim mining as novel. Two claims matter: naive frequency is not a
ranking (Table 1), and the enforceability filter then deletes the
frequent composites. Each theme receives one of three dispositions:

- •
  Judgeable from the note, transcript, and generation context alone.
- •
  Needs context beyond the generation artifacts. For example, judging
  generated ICD codes requires billing expertise the artifacts do not
  carry.
- •
  Not a documentation defect, but a software or UI issue not
  attributable to the generator. Excluded from the rubric.

### 3.2 Objectification

Judgeable themes are then restated in a form an LLM can decide
unambiguously. We illustrate with Misplacement, one of the most common
themes, which users report as content appearing in the wrong section,
such as A&P content sitting in HPI, or the reverse. It is not monolithic
but composed of sub-metrics, so scoring it directly invites ambiguous
interpretation. Four operations split it into determinate metrics:

Reduction by logical decomposition: Misplacement is essentially content
appearing in a wrong section and either absent from the correct one, or
present. We term content present in the wrong section as Leakage. If the
content is also present in the correct section, the duplicate is
redundant; if it is absent there, it is an Omission. Misplacement is
therefore a composite of determinate checks. Cross-section duplication
is scored through leakage, so the Redundancy judge stays within one
section (scope restriction, below).

Directional sub-categorization: Leakage can be further split into
simpler sub-categories consisting of ordered section pairs (source,
destination) indicating the direction of leakage. These pairs are judged
independently, for example Plan into History or Results into Physical
Exam. This level of detailed enumeration is required since each of these
pairs has specific rules for making the judgment. For example, for A&P
in History, if a History sentence contains a definitive diagnosis or
impression, it is a violation. On the other hand, symptom narrative,
patient quotes, or brief non-decisional history are acceptable.

Scope restriction: Some metrics also need an unambiguous scope.
Redundancy means duplication within a section. Duplication across
sections is unstable across reviewers and is covered by the leakage
checks.

Preference parameterization: Even sectional complaints are sometimes
preferences rather than defects (“put the plan in the HPI” or “merge ROS
into History”). We do not treat those as leakage.

### 3.3 Evaluator battery

With the rubric fixed, we build one judge per metric by combining a
prompt with a foundation model. The prompt for each metric captures its
specific nuances, which need to be handled separately. We describe their
structure and principles but do not release the prompts (the released
labels, ACI-Defect, criterion strings, and the planted-span hit rule
support reproduction; §4). Evaluator prompts follow the construction
rules below. Deployed groups are in Table 7.

- •
  One judge per criterion, instance grain. Each criterion is judged by
  an independent agent giving structured verdicts on the constituents of
  a clinical note (sentences, case facts, or occurrences), rather than
  judging at a macroscopic level. Each verdict includes a label,
  supporting note and transcript excerpts, rationale, and severity (low,
  medium, or high clinical consequence).
- •
  Anchor-then-classify. The prompt requires the judge to first extract
  intermediate artifacts (such as note or transcript quotes) before
  making the final judgment in accordance with the principle of
  providing proof before preference (Tapwal et al., 2026).
- •
  Dual ground truth, transcript wins. LLM evaluators receive the raw
  transcript as the primary source and the distilled atomic clinical
  case facts generated by an LLM from the transcripts as a secondary
  backup, with the transcript taking priority in case of conflict. They
  also receive the encounter metadata.

## 4 Experimental Setup

Two corpora: Judges were developed on notes from a commercially deployed
ambient scribe; those notes contain PHI and are not released. ACI-bench
(207 pairs, test1 $`n{=}40`$, CC BY 4.0) is the public test. It has no
ratings or comments, so C1 aggregates and the production rating analysis
cannot run on it.

Judge model: gemini-2.5-pro, constrained structured output with schema
validation and repair-on-failure. As a check against prompt overfit, the
“History of” misuse judge was re-run unchanged on a second frontier
model and agreed with the original verdicts on over $`98\%`$ of
instances ($`n{=}100`$). The robustness runs (Table 5) also use gpt-4.1
and qwen3-next-80b, at temperature 0 with the same hit rule.

Cost: One constrained-decoding call per criterion per note, one
case-fact extraction, and one hallucination call per 20 sentences, so
about 14 calls per note for the 11 LLM judges deployed on ACI-bench. The
deterministic checks add none, and calls are independent across notes
and criteria. Objectification sets that cost. The broad complaint in
Table 4 is a single call. Each section-pair check is one call. Under
matched effort, the four directional calls cost 4.2$`\times`$ one
composite call on Gemini (USD 24.71 vs 5.90 per 1,000 notes).

Labels on gold (C2): Clinicians manually reviewed all 40 notes of the
ACI-bench MEDIQA-Chat Task B split (test1) and scored them on the
grounding dimensions. Each reviewer saw the transcript, the note, the
patient and EHR metadata, and the LLM-extracted case facts. Table 2
reports judges’ P/R against the raw independent sheet _and_ against
corrected gold. The labeling pass was blinded to judge output; the
discrepancy adjudicator was not. Adjudication covers every discrepancy
(150/150 hallucination; 280/280 omission).

ACI-Defect (C3): Generic perturbations (delete / repeat / shuffle) do
not test whether an evaluator detects the defect it was designed for,
nor whether reference metrics would have caught it. We plant one defect
at a time into the 40-note ACI-bench test1 split, leaving all other
content untouched (notes are not re-rendered). If a note lacks the
section a plant needs, we skip the plant rather than invent a section. A
hit is a judge instance whose snippet overlaps the planted span and
whose class is the plant’s target class; it is not a note-level failure
verdict. Against each perturbed note we also score ROUGE-1/2/L F1,
BERTScore-F1 (roberta-large, library default), and a UMLS-free
MedCon-proxy (gazetteer + dose/lab concept F1), all versus the original
gold, reporting drops as $`1-\text{score}`$. Recipes: Table 9.

Planted defects are cleaner than production defects. The same leakage
judges, mapped onto clinician misplacement labels rather than onto a
planted span, score 0.24 precision and 0.13 recall (Appendix B). That
mapping is not a quality claim for Table 2. It is evidence that a
surgical plant is easier than the misplacement clinicians actually mark,
which is often “plan belongs in assessment” rather than one moved
sentence.

Production notes–ratings correlation (C4): After dropping EHR/UI
comments, $`1{,}446`$ notes carrying a user rating are judged. Comments,
ratings, and user identifiers never enter the evaluator. We score
_instance rates_ (target findings / opportunities) rather than
note-level pass/fail (for example, inappropriately omitted case facts,
fabricated sentences). A comment is a family tag, not a gold span. For
each dimension we also record the note-level “at least one finding” bit,
so rate and flag are comparable on identical inputs. _Burden_ is the
mean of column-wise $`z`$-scored rates over six dimensions scored on
every note; an out-of-scope dimension is skipped, not counted as zero.

## 5 Evaluation

In this section, we evaluate the different components of the evaluation
pipeline described in Section 3 and motivate the need for each of those
components in the pipeline. We start by showing why it is necessary to
objectify user feedback into determinate metrics.

### 5.1 The enforceability filter discards half of the reported defects (C1)

The complaints an LLM judge cannot grade as posed are as common as the
ones it can. We took a year of production feedback: $`10{,}801`$ rated
notes carrying $`3{,}496`$ free-text comments, rated 1–5 (5 = most
satisfied). Table 1 assigns each unfavorable comment to exactly one
family. Composite complaints come from $`14.3\%`$ of unsatisfied users,
while complaints that are already atomic or that need an external tool
come from $`14.4\%`$. An enforceability filter deletes the first group
and keeps most of the second, so it discards about half of everything
users report as a note defect.

| --------------------- | ---- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Composite structure   | 14.3 | Unenforceable as posed; objectified into leakage, format, redundancy |
| of which misplacement | 10.0 | Four directional leakage judges                                      |
| Atomic / tool-needing | 14.4 | Judged directly, except billing codes, which need a tool             |
| Not a note defect     | 6.8  | Excluded from the rubric                                             |

### 5.2 Quality gaps in ACI-bench (C2)

In the previous section, we established the importance of objectifying
composites to be able to preserve valuable insights gained from the
feedback. In this section, we devise experiments to test the
effectiveness of the rubric derived from user feedback using ACI-bench
test1 notes.

Before we can use ACI-bench as a gold benchmark for our experiments, we
need to ensure that it does not suffer from any quality gaps as defined
by the user-feedback-derived rubric. This is inspired by anecdotal
evidence in ACI-bench. For example, in aci-D2N092 the gold documents
“Examination of the left wrist shows tenderness over the distal radius”
for an encounter whose chief complaint is right arm pain.
Figure 3(right) gives more such examples. Run on all $`40`$ notes, the
grounding judges flag hallucination and omission on almost every note,
while the other judges fire rarely (Figure 3, left).

We do not treat these flags as confirmed errors until a clinician has
ruled on them, and we do not edit ACI-bench. Note text is byte-identical
to the original corpus (Yim et al., 2023) throughout; we only change the
labels associated with each note. A label is a clinician decision on one
sentence or one case fact (supported vs not; missing vs not). It is not
an edit to the note text. The sheet design, the adjudication rules, and
the residual false positives are in Appendix B. Three clinicians
partitioned the note set and marked every sentence and every
transcript-derived case fact with judge output hidden ($`1{,}878`$
sentences; $`2{,}062`$ facts); each item carries one independent label,
and every judge-labeler discrepancy was then adjudicated. Table 2
reports judge precision and recall. Adjudication more than doubles the
hallucination positive class (44 $`\to`$ 102 sentences) and moves
omission from 334 to 368 facts. The corrected labels state the condition
of the benchmark precisely: $`102`$ of $`1{,}878`$ gold sentences
($`5.4\%`$) carry a confirmed hallucination and $`368`$ of $`2{,}062`$
case facts ($`17.8\%`$) are inappropriately missing. Every one of the
$`40`$ reference notes omits at least one discussed fact, and $`32`$ of
$`40`$ contain at least one hallucinated sentence.

A second clinician, who did not do the first pass and did not see judge
output, relabeled $`10`$ of the $`40`$ notes ($`915`$ items). Note-level
agreement is $`10/10`$ for both dimensions. Span-level Cohen’s
$`\kappa`$ is $`0.61`$ for omission ($`95\%`$ CI $`0.49`$–$`0.71`$) and
$`0.41`$ for hallucination ($`0.22`$–$`0.58`$), against first-pass
prevalence of $`11.0\%`$ and $`4.7\%`$. The second rater marked more
defects than the first, so judge precision against the first pass is a
floor. This study does not validate the adjudicated labels. We release
corrected labels rather than a cleaned corpus, because automated repair
of the reference notes would make gold an output of the system being
evaluated against it.

a single token (P1, P2, P8, P11). For scale, deleting $`15\%`$ of tokens
at random drops ROUGE-1 by $`0.081`$. The two lowest recalls are limits
of those checks rather than subtler plants: P2’s extractor does not read
past an INSTRUCTIONS header, costing 11 spans, and P8’s gazetteer lacks
six brand names.

Objectification improves discrimination, not recall: A natural objection
is that objectification is unnecessary, and we can simply give a
frontier model the complaint and it will find the defect. It does. Given
only “Content is in the wrong section of the note.”, the same judge
localizes the planted span in all 126 planted notes, beating the 109/126
of the directional-pair judges, and the criterion Zhou et al. (2025)
discard scores 53/56 on the pair it covers (Table 4). That judge is
nonetheless useless as an instrument, because it also fires on clean
notes. It flags 28 of the 40 unperturbed notes ($`70\%`$), at $`1.60`$
flagged sentences per note against $`1.68`$ on planted notes, so its
output cannot tell a note with a surgical defect from a clean one. The
directional judges flag the same notes at $`2`$–$`26\%`$. Scoring each
version by recall minus false-positive rate (Youden’s $`J`$, where
$`1.0`$ is perfect and $`0`$ is uninformative), objectification reaches
$`0.72`$ against the composite’s $`0.30`$, though this comparison also
differs in prompt effort, which Table 5 controls. Part of the
composite’s failure is scope, not capability. Half of the composite’s
clean-note flags are content the objectified rubric explicitly allows in
History: pertinent negatives that belong to Review of Systems, and past,
family and social history (Appendix D). Asked an unscoped question, the
judge enforces an idealized template of its own instead of the ordered
section pair the complaint names.

Under matched effort the gain is per check, not per note. When both
versions use the same procedure, rules, and examples, one directional
question per call still lowers the per-check clean-note false-positive
rate on all three models, and $`J`$ rises by $`0.19`$, $`0.26`$, and

HPI$`\to`$A&P shows no advantage on any model. When the checks are
combined into one note-level decision, the single composite question is
the better screen on all three models. Objectification buys a
localizable per-criterion judgment, not a better note-level alarm. Much
of the original Gemini gap ($`0.72`$ vs. $`0.30`$) was prompt effort.
Called once, without the deployed extractor, the objectified P2
predicate recalls $`28/28`$, so the $`15/28`$ in Table 4 is an extractor
limit.

The decomposition matters. Rewriting the same complaints by content
class (a management decision outside A&P, symptom narrative outside
History, a result outside Results) instead of by ordered section pair
keeps recall (pooled P2–P4: $`0.91`$, $`0.87`$, $`0.91`$ on Gemini,
GPT-4.1, Qwen) but not false-positive control (mean clean rate $`0.67`$,
$`0.54`$, $`0.39`$; $`J`$ $`0.24`$, $`0.33`$, $`0.52`$). Finding the
plant is robust to the decomposition; the low false-positive rate is
specific to the directional split.

Blind spots, found through plant misses, judge–human adjudication, and
cross-model reruns: beyond the P2 extractor window, the P8 gazetteer
gap, and the composite’s self-imposed template, cross-model runs exposed
two more: the scoped fabricated-exam predicate recalls $`0.00`$ on
GPT-4.1 and Qwen, and the objectified “history of” check flags $`57\%`$
of clean notes on Qwen.

### 5.4 Objectified rates agree with user ratings in direction (C4)

C1–C3 never use a human judgment of a whole note. For that we use the
optional 1–5 rating clinicians leave in production. Ratings, comments,
and user identifiers are hidden from every judge, and the
comment-theme-to-judge map was fixed before scoring (Appendix E).

Rates rank notes; flags do not. A note-level “at least one judge fired”
bit is useless here: it fires on $`86\%`$ of notes for hallucination and
on $`97\%`$ for both omission and diagnosis structure, so nearly every
note looks alike. The same judges scored as rates do separate the rating
bands. Averaging each note’s standardized rates over the six dimensions
scored on every note gives one _burden_ score, and mean burden falls
from $`+0.15`$ at rating 1 to $`-0.08`$ at rating 5, dropping at every
step (Figure 4, right; $`\rho{=}{-}0.15`$, $`p{<}10^{-7}`$,
$`n{=}1{,}446`$; $`\rho{=}{-}0.30`$ for overall burden on the $`229`$
named-defect notes). Five of the six dimensions are lower at rating 5
than at rating 1. The effect is small. Since no judge sees the rating,
this is convergent evidence of direction only, not a basis for routing
notes or validation of any one judge.

A note-level score cannot name the criterion. One number per note cannot
say which of $`k`$ criteria was violated. The data bear this out: when a
comment names a family, the matching instance rate is not higher, except
for bundled diagnosis headings; omission, A&P$`\to`$HPI leakage,
“history of” misuse, and hallucination show no enrichment (Table 11),
and the rating does not correlate with the mapped judge at all
($`\rho{=}{+}0.01`$, $`n{=}249`$; Figure 4, left). Rating-5 notes are
not defect-free either: they still omit about $`21\%`$ of case facts and
leave about $`6\%`$ of sentences unsupported. This is why we test
localization on planted spans: production feedback names a family, never
a span.

## 6 Conclusion

When evaluation criteria are mined from user feedback, the
enforceability filter is biased against the most common complaints.
Objectification is the alternative to deletion. Posed as a composite,
the same judge localizes every planted misplacement yet fires on 70% of
clean reference notes. With prompt effort matched, objectified checks
still lower per-check false positives on three models, but a single
composite remains the better note-level screen, so the gain is
localization per criterion. Across eleven plants, objectified judges
localize the planted span (median recall $`0.95`$, range
$`0.54`$–$`1.00`$) while ROUGE-1 drops by at most $`0.030`$. Since
production notes cannot be published under HIPAA, ACI-bench is the
public test. Because it contains quality gaps under the same rubric, we
release independent clinical review labels and grounding judges’
performance on it. On $`1{,}446`$ rated production notes, the same
objectified rates move in the right direction against a clinician rating
no judge sees, with the mean falling at every step from rating 1 to
rating 5. The association is small and is not a per-criterion
validation. That check costs no annotation but works only at the right
grain: a note-level pass/fail bit fires on most notes and ranks nothing.
Score defects per opportunity, use user feedback to decide _what_ to
measure, validate localization on planted spans, and do not treat
ACI-bench gold as a clean negative set.

## 7 Limitations

Production data are from one site, and ratings are optional and
self-selected, so $`\rho{=}{-}0.15`$ describes this feedback channel,
not clinician-perceived quality in general. We fit no classifier and do
not use burden to route notes. The ACI-bench review covers 40 test1
notes. Agreement was measured on 10 doubly labeled notes; span-level
$`\kappa`$ for hallucination is moderate. Adjudication was not blinded,
so corrected precision in Table 2 is an upper bound, and it certifies
grounding only. Objectification is an expert restatement by one
clinician. We did not measure agreement between independent rewriters;
the content-class rewrite is a proxy, and it keeps recall but not
false-positive control. Matched effort covers three models and P2–P4
only. We did not test the order or presentation of criteria within a
prompt (Shankar et al., 2024). Clean-note flags are all scored as false
positives, though some are real reference-note defects and some are
documentation convention. Plant-level recall does not transfer to
production misplacement labels (0.24 precision, 0.13 recall;
Appendix B). The 11-plant battery uses Gemini 2.5 Pro with the deployed
prompts, which are not released; the composite, matched-effort, and
content-class criterion strings are released.

## 8 Ethics

The production study uses de-identified encounter aggregates under HIPAA
policy. Notes, transcripts, and verbatim complaints are PHI and appear
in neither the paper nor the release. ACI-bench is an existing public
corpus, and ACI-Defect and the independent labels add no new patient
data.

#### Residual false positives.

Eight rows confirm a hallucination while disputing its type; under
binary scoring they are true positives for the judge (precision
$`0.52\to 0.57`$; recall unchanged). After that credit, 75 residual

is not a Table 2 quality claim.

## Appendix D Composite-vs-objectified ablation protocol

Supports Tables 4 and 5.

The composite prompt and the objectified judges see the same note and
transcript, the same judge model at temperature 0, and the same
instance-level response schema: each instance carries an exactly quoted
note sentence, the section it sits under, a class, a rationale, and a
confidence. The baseline is therefore not handicapped by a note-level
yes/no output. The criterion text is the intended difference, but the
objectified prompt is the deployed multi-step judge, so prompt effort
also differs; the matched-effort runs (Table 5) remove that difference.
The composite criterion is stated as posed, with no ordered section
pair, no scope restriction, and no inclusion/exclusion list.

#### Clean-note flags.

Buckets are assigned automatically from the composite judge’s own
rationale text and are not clinician labels: assessment/plan placement
21 flags, past/family/social history 11, Review-of-Systems and pertinent

#### Shared hit rule.

Recall for every version is scored by one rule: a hit requires a flagged
instance whose quoted span overlaps the planted span, where overlap is
containment in either direction or content-token F1 $`\geq 0.5`$. That
rule reproduces the objectified numbers in Table 3 exactly, so the two
versions are comparable.

#### Burden.

_Burden_ averages column-wise $`z`$-scored rates over the six dimensions
scored on every note: omission, hallucination, “history of” misuse,
A&P$`\to`$HPI leakage, diagnosis structure, and
subjective/results$`\to`$PE leakage. Bundled diagnosis headings are
mapped from a comment theme but are not part of that average. Judges
abstain when the phrase or section is absent, so those rates are over
in-scope notes only.
