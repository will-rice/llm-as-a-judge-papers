# The First Token Is Not the Verdict: Hidden Costs of Reading LLM Judges Without Generating

Gnaneswar Villuri Affiliation: Department of Electrical and Computer
Engineering    Hashmath Shaik Affiliation: Stony Brook University   
Alex Doboli Affiliation: Stony Brook, NY 11794, USA

###### Abstract

Reading an LLM judge’s verdict from the logits of its first generated
token is cheap, requires no generation, and is exactly what constrained
decoding and likelihood-scoring evaluation harnesses produce. We show
that this readout distorts position bias in one direction: it overstates
it in every condition we test, so figures obtained this way behave as
upper bounds. The mechanism is that judges do not always lead with a
verdict token, on $`12\%`$ to $`49\%`$ of pairs for three Qwen3 judges
and under $`3\%`$ for Llama-3.1-8B and Phi-3.5-mini, and forcing a read
on those pairs returns whichever response was shown first rather than a
judgment. Pooled over the $`924`$ pairs where a judge did not commit,
the forced read flips on $`89.7\%`$ of them when the responses are
swapped, against $`47.5\%`$ read after generation (paired difference
$`+0.422`$, $`95\%`$ CI $`[+0.365,+0.467]`$). The distortion is specific
to what is measured: it moves position bias by $`42`$ points while
moving judge accuracy by under one point in seven of ten conditions, so
it misleads whoever audits a judge rather than whoever uses one. A
second, smaller failure occurs even when the judge does lead with a
verdict token, since it sometimes opens with one letter and reasons its
way to the other, on $`0`$ to $`5.5\%`$ of pairs at a rate uncorrelated
with compliance. We recommend reporting the rate at which a judge leads
with a verdict token, which costs one forward pass and no labels,
alongside any position-bias figure.

## 1 Two protocols, one name

Position bias is measured by presenting each pair of responses in both
orders and counting how often the verdict _flips_, that is, reverses
(Zheng et al., 2023; Norman et al., 2026). Obtaining that verdict
requires choosing a protocol, and the two protocols in common use are
not the same operation.

The first protocol generates a response and parses the verdict from the
text, which is what judge audits do (Norman et al., 2026; Usami et al.,
2026; Tan et al., 2024). The second reads the verdict from the logits of
the first generated token. It is far cheaper because it requires no
generation at all, it is what restricted-likelihood evaluation harnesses
compute, and it is what constrained decoding produces by construction,
since token masking guarantees a legal verdict at the first position
(Gao et al., 2024; Willard and Louf, 2023). That guarantee is advertised
as the point: one never gets a parse error and never has to retry.

Existing practice handles a non-verdict first token in one of three
ways, and two of them are the forced read. Restricted-likelihood
harnesses score only the candidate answers against each other and never
ask what the model would have said (Gao et al., 2024). Constrained
decoding masks every illegal token, so a verdict is produced by
construction (Willard and Louf, 2023). The third way is to generate and,
on a parse failure, retry or let the judge continue, which is what
JudgeBench does (Tan et al., 2024). Only the third recovers what the
judge would have answered.

Logit-based judging is in production use rather than hypothetical.
Probability-weighted score aggregation is standard (Liu et al., 2023),
and AlpacaEval 2.0 computes its headline win-rate from the logprobs of a
single output token (Li et al., 2023). We did not identify a published
position-bias figure obtained this way, so we ask a conditional
question: if judge verdicts are obtained as this tooling obtains them,
what does that cost when the quantity measured is position bias? The
guarantee turns out to be the problem. A judge that was not going to
commit still yields a verdict, and that verdict carries position rather
than content.

## 2 Setup

A _pair_ is two responses to one instruction, shown in two
_presentations_, one per order, under a prompt ending “Answer with
exactly one character”. We take two readouts of the same judge on the
same pairs. The first-token readout takes the larger of the two
verdict-token logits at the first generated position, whether or not
either was what the judge would actually have emitted; we call it the
_forced read_, and that property is what this paper is about. The
generated readout lets the model write up to $`500`$ tokens and parses
the verdict using explicit decision patterns such as “the answer is X”,
rather than bare mentions of the letters, because the judge names both
responses repeatedly while reasoning; where none parses we regenerate
once at $`1{,}500`$ tokens. A pair is _compliant_ when the judge would
have led with a verdict token in both presentations, that is, when the
argmax over its full vocabulary is one of the two verdict tokens.

The judges are Qwen3 at $`1.7`$B, $`4`$B and $`8`$B,
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Phi-3.5-mini. The benchmarks are JudgeBench
(Tan et al., 2024), $`620`$ pairs, and LLMBar (Zeng et al., 2024),
$`419`$ pairs. Verdict tokens are X/Y, A/B and 1/2. We run the full
benchmarks. Intervals are cluster bootstraps over benchmark sources.
Qwen3 supports a hybrid thinking mode, which we disable
(enable_thinking=False) so that all judges receive comparable
single-turn prompts; the non-compliance we report is therefore not an
artefact of an explicit reasoning mode being left on.

Two properties of this design make our numbers conservative. Our prompt
asks for one character, so the generated readout measures the judge
disobeying it; audits that invite reasoning would see lower compliance
and a larger divergence. And the $`88`$ presentations that still yield
no verdict at $`1{,}500`$ tokens are excluded from both readouts; every
such pair is non-compliant, so the exclusion removes data from the arm
that produces the effect. Recovery is uneven: the second pass resolves
$`95\%`$ of the Qwen3-8B cases but $`54\%`$ for Qwen3-1.7B, which often
reaches the cap without concluding.

Compliance is a property of a model, a prompt and a task together, not
of a model alone. Holding Qwen3-8B and the benchmark fixed and changing
only the two verdict tokens moves it from $`0.506`$ to $`0.676`$.

it from $`0.506`$ to $`1.000`$, LLMBar items being short
instruction-following comparisons while JudgeBench items are technical

multiple-choice prompt, first-token validity is $`9.5\%`$ (Cappelletti
et al., 2025), against $`99.8\%`$ under ours. A compliance rate cannot
be inherited from another study.

## 4 A forced read on an undecided judge locks to a slot

Figure 1: Where the two readouts differ, for the five Qwen3–JudgeBench
conditions of Table 1. Where the judge leads with a verdict token (top)
they are identical _by construction_, so one marker is shown. Where it
does not (bottom) the forced read always lies to the right of the
generated read. Pooled over $`924`$ such pairs the difference is
$`+0.422`$ ($`p=1.0\times 10^{-99}`$).

The two readouts diverge only on pairs where the judge did not lead with
a verdict token, and there they diverge sharply. Because both readouts
are measured on the same pairs, the comparison is paired, and we pool it
across conditions. Of $`924`$ pooled non-compliant pairs, the forced
read flips on $`829`$ of them ($`0.897`$) and the generated read on
$`439`$ ($`0.475`$), a paired difference of $`+0.422`$ with a $`95\%`$
cluster-bootstrap interval of $`[+0.365,+0.467]`$ over conditions. Of
the $`420`$ pairs on which the two readouts disagree about whether a
flip occurred, $`405`$ are flips seen only under the forced read and
$`15`$ only under generation (exact McNemar $`p=1.0\times 10^{-99}`$,
which treats pairs as independent and so understates the true $`p`$; the
clustered interval above is the one we rely on).

What the forced read is measuring follows by identity rather than by
inference. With two verdict tokens and no ties, a flip occurs exactly
when the same slot position is selected in both presentations, because
the responses swap between them; we verify that this holds on all
$`5{,}720`$ pairs (the $`11{,}596`$ order-presentations counted in
Section 5 are the $`5{,}798`$ pairs before exclusion, each presented
twice). The flip rate on non-compliant pairs is therefore not evidence
of a slot lock but is itself the rate at which the forced read locked to
a slot, $`0.897`$.

The slot it locks to is almost always the first one. Of the $`829`$
locked non-compliant pairs, $`827`$ locked to the first position
($`0.998`$; exact binomial against chance $`p=1.9\times 10^{-244}`$),
and four of the five conditions lock to it without a single exception.
The lock is to a fixed position at the logit level rather than being
item-specific, and it makes the error one-sided: in all five conditions
the forced read flips more, never less, so position-bias figures
obtained from first-token logits are upper bounds on what a
generate-and-parse audit would report.

The choice of readout affects measurement rather than use. Against gold
labels the two readouts differ in accuracy by nothing at all in four
conditions and by more than a point in only three, the largest
difference being $`6.7`$ points ($`0.439`$ to $`0.648`$ overall). Flip
rate, by contrast, differs by up to $`0.220`$. Both readouts are near
chance on the non-compliant pairs that drive the divergence, because the
judge does not know the answer there under either protocol. The forced
read adds error that depends on order but not on the label, so it
affects whoever audits a judge far more than whoever uses one.

### A compliant first token is usually, but not always, the conclusion.

On $`300`$ compliant pairs per judge we compared the first token to the
parsed final verdict. Qwen3-4B disagrees on $`15`$ of $`271`$ parsed
($`5.5\%`$), Phi-3.5-mini on $`14`$ of $`257`$ ($`5.4\%`$), Qwen3-8B on
$`8`$ of $`285`$ ($`2.8\%`$) and Llama-3.1-8B on $`0`$ of $`300`$
($`0.0\%`$, Wilson $`95\%`$ CI $`[0.0\%,1.3\%]`$). The disagreements
share a form: the judge opens with X, then writes “…Therefore, Response
Y better follows the instruction.” This failure is independent of the
first: across the four judges its rate is uncorrelated with compliance
($`r=0.01`$), and Phi-3.5-mini leads with a verdict token on $`97.1\%`$
of pairs yet changes its mind on $`5.4\%`$ of them. The judge that looks
safest for a first-token readout is not the one least distorted by it.

## 5 A cheaper procedure, and what it is worth

The characterisation suggests a shortcut: read the first token, and
generate only for the orders where the judge did not lead with a
verdict. Adaptive computation of this shape is standard (Jung et al.,
2025; Gupta et al., 2024), but those cascades escalate to a stronger
model under a calibrated threshold, whereas we escalate between two
readouts of the same model and need no calibration set. The saving is
large and the fidelity is high but not perfect. Across our ten
conditions the procedure generates for $`1{,}678`$ of $`11{,}596`$
order-presentations, a reduction of $`85.5\%`$, ranging from $`58.9\%`$
on the least compliant condition to $`100\%`$ where the judge always
leads with a verdict. By Section 4 the substituted verdicts are wrong on
$`0`$ to $`5.5\%`$ of compliant pairs, so fidelity runs from about
$`0.945`$ to $`1.00`$. It is an approximation with a measured error rate
rather than a guarantee.

## 6 Limitations

The divergence is measurable in only one of the three families we test.
Compliance is so high for the other two that Phi-3.5-mini contributes
$`18`$ non-compliant pairs and Llama-3.1-8B one, too few to analyse, so
the pooled comparison is Qwen3 only and the cross-family contrast is a
difference in compliance rather than an independent replication of the
effect. We also test only open-weight judges at $`8`$B and below,
because frontier API judges do not expose logits.

Two measurement choices should be read with the result. The generated
readout uses a heuristic parser and a capped budget, so even after the
second pass it is a lower bound on how often a verdict is eventually
reached. And we tested whether the two readouts reorder judges by
position bias; the reordering was not stable under bootstrap ($`50.1\%`$
of resamples) and we do not claim it.

Our question is conditional on a practice rather than a correction of a
published number. That first-token reads fail when a model does not
commit is already stated for multiple-choice settings (Cappelletti et
al., 2025), and the readout is in production use (Li et al., 2023).
Prompts engineered for single-token output may reach compliance close to
$`1`$, and output prefilling (Cappelletti et al., 2025) is a plausible
remedy we have not tested; our point is that neither is usually verified
or reported.

## 7 Recommendations and conclusion

Three things belong in a judge deployment disclosure, all cheap and
currently absent from reported evaluations. Report the compliance rate:
it costs one forward pass per pair, needs no labels, varies from
$`0.506`$ to $`1.000`$ across our conditions, and cannot be inherited
from another paper. Rao and Callison-Burch (2026) ask the same of
abstention and tie rates in generated outputs; ours applies that
discipline one step earlier. Say which readout produced the verdict,
because a figure obtained by constrained decoding and one obtained by
generate-and-parse are not the same quantity when compliance is
incomplete. And treat the answer format as an experimental variable:
changing two characters moves compliance by $`17`$ points here, and most
papers do not report which verdict tokens they used.

The first token is a convenient place to read a judge’s verdict and an
unreliable one. It is not a verdict at all on up to $`49\%`$ of pairs,
and not its conclusion on up to $`5.5\%`$ of those where it looks like
one. Both rates are measurable in advance and neither is reported.
