###### Abstract

LLM-judge audits assess bias by comparing ratings across matched
conditions. Difference-in-differences designs take the rating difference
between two candidate responses to each item and compare it across a
manipulated attribute. Building on prior work on nonlinear
difference-in-differences, we show in closed form that when ratings are
bounded, a severity shift common to both responses produces an observed
interaction whenever the scale bounds attenuate it unequally. We then
report a pre-registered audit of 990 calls to a frozen pedagogy judge.
The registered primary analysis did not detect an effect of a stated
novice or advanced profile on scaffolding preference. The only nominally
significant secondary interaction was on the productive-struggle
sub-score ($`+0.378`$ points; $`p=0.002`$). A post-hoc construction with
zero differential preference reproduced 79 to 85% of this interaction
using the observed severity shift and the scale floor alone. The
observed interaction therefore does not identify differential preference
on the latent scale. Code is available at
[https://github.com/bydeng01/ability-levels-audit](https://github.com/bydeng01/ability-levels-audit).

## 1 Introduction

Large language models now grade other language models. Scores emitted by
an LLM judge steer leaderboards and benchmark rankings (Zheng et al.,
2023; Gu et al., 2026), and in education LLM judges already score
tutoring systems on rubrics of teaching quality (Maurya et al., 2025;
Fan et al., 2026). Whether such judges deserve that role is itself a
measurement question, and the audits that answer it share a design: hold
the item fixed, vary an attribute of its presentation or of the identity
attached to it, and read the bias off the contrast between matched
conditions (Wang et al., 2024; Howell et al., 2025). A stronger design
differences twice: a within-item contrast between two candidate
responses is differenced again across the manipulated attribute, so that
anything shifting a response’s score equally in every condition cancels.
The resulting endpoint is a difference in differences on a bounded
rating scale.

The design is trusted because additive artifacts cancel. The
interpretation relies on a further assumption: when an effect survives
the double difference, the bounded scale is treated as a conservative
nuisance, since clipping at the scale ends can attenuate a real effect
but not create one. For a single contrast that is true, because clipping
is non-expansive. Our own pre-registration asserted it for the double
difference, and there it is false. A common severity shift can produce a
nonzero interaction when the scale bounds attenuate it differently for
the two responses (§5). Econometrics has long separated the latent
interaction from the cross difference of observed outcomes, yet
LLM-judge audits report interaction effects from short bounded rubrics
without identifiability checks (e.g., the factorial interactions of
Maltbie and Raval (2026), read off a 1–10 rubric with most ratings
within two points of the floor). This risk grows with the quality of the
materials: sharper contrasts push the two responses toward opposite ends
of the scale, where a common shift is censored most unequally.

This paper demonstrates the failure from inside a pre-registered audit,
on the audit’s own data. The study put a validity question to the frozen
pedagogy judge of Fan et al. (2026), which scores candidate tutor turns
on a four-principle pedagogy rubric: does its preference for
high-scaffolding responses track the competence a learner demonstrates
in the dialogue, or does a stated learner profile move it? Each of 55
frozen rating contexts pairs a high-scaffolding with a low-scaffolding
candidate response, the two poles of that context. The judge rates both
in three arms, under no profile, a stated novice profile, and a stated
advanced profile. The registered primary endpoint, the Profile Anchoring
Gap (PAG), is the change in the judge’s scaffolding preference between
the two profiles on the weak stratum, the stimuli where the learner
visibly struggles. The materials, instrument, primary endpoint, and
registered secondary analyses were fixed before study scoring (§7).

The registered endpoint is null: against a baseline preference of
$`+2.6`$ to $`+3.2`$ points of the five-point scale, the profile moves
the preference by $`+0.085`$ (95% BCa $`[-0.167,+0.353]`$, exact
signed-rank $`p=0.684`$; §6.1). The profile did, however, affect
absolute ratings. Both poles received lower ratings under the advanced
profile, consistent with a common severity component. The scale can
convert such a non-differential shift into the audit’s one nominally
significant interaction: a $`+0.378`$ gap on the productive-struggle
sub-score ($`p=0.002`$). A construction containing zero differential
preference reproduces 79 to 85% of that gap from the observed high-pole
shift and the scale floor alone (Figure 1c). On 17 of the 30
weak-stratum stimuli the productive-struggle low pole is pinned at the
floor in every arm, and there the difference in differences is
algebraically a one-pole contrast.

This paper makes three contributions. First, an identifiability analysis
of within-item difference-in-differences endpoints on bounded rating
scales. The statistical result is known for limited-dependent-variable
models (Puhani, 2012); we show that in judge audits the endpoint
approaches a one-pole contrast as the two candidate responses are rated
further apart. Second, a pre-registered audit of profile influence on a
frozen pedagogy judge. The null primary is reported at its actual
resolution, and the secondaries show that censoring can manufacture an
effect, contrary to the audit’s own registered assumption. Third, an
identifiability check that follows from the same algebra, computable
from ratings an audit already has.

## 2 Related work

#### LLM judges and their audits.

Judge-based evaluation entered common use with MT-Bench and Chatbot
Arena, which reported high judge–human agreement alongside position,
verbosity, and self-enhancement biases (Zheng et al., 2023). Later
audits extended the catalog: order effects that flip verdicts (Wang et
al., 2024), self-preference in proportion to self-recognition
(Panickssery et al., 2024), and diverging human and model bias profiles
under controlled perturbation (Chen et al., 2024). Surveys collect both
the biases and the audit designs that detect them (Gu et al., 2026). We
examine whether difference-in-differences contrasts on bounded rating
scales identify latent differential preference.

#### Sensitivity to stated attributes.

A second line varies who the text is about or from. Sociodemographic
prompting shifts model predictions on subjective tasks, though
inconsistently across models and datasets (Beck et al., 2024); persona
variables explain a small fraction of human annotation variance (Hu and
Collier, 2024); stated institutional prestige moves simulated
peer-review outcomes for otherwise identical manuscripts (Howell et al.,
2025). These findings motivate our manipulated factor, a stated learner
profile, and suggest that its effect is likely to be small.

#### Pedagogical evaluation of tutoring systems.

The rubric principles at stake descend from scaffolding (Wood et al., 1976) and productive failure (Kapur, 2008), operationalized for LLM
tutors in dialogue benchmarks, error-remediation corpora, and evaluation
taxonomies (Tack and Piech, 2022; Macina et al., 2023; Daheim et al.,
2024; Maurya et al., 2025; LearnLM Team, 2024). The instrument we audit
is the frozen pedagogy judge of Fan et al. (2026), which found generic
helpfulness ratings judge-dependent while rubric distinctions were
stable; its corpus supplies our stimuli.

#### Interactions on bounded and ordinal scales.

The problem is old outside NLP. Censored and ordinal reporting make the
observed score a nonlinear transform of a latent quantity (Tobin, 1958;
McKelvey and Zavoina, 1975). In such models the interaction of observed
conditional means and the interaction coefficient are different
quantities (Ai and Norton, 2003), and the observed cross difference is
not the treatment effect in nonlinear difference-in-differences models
(Puhani, 2012). Identification that survives monotone rescaling of the
outcome requires the stronger assumptions of Athey and Imbens (2006),
and for ordinal outcomes the estimand is not even well defined without
latent-scale assumptions (Yamauchi, 2020). Treating ordinal ratings as
interval data misleads even for ratings well away from the scale
endpoints (Liddell and Kruschke, 2018), and lacks justification in NLG
(Amidei et al., 2019). Liddell and Kruschke (2018) further show that
metric models of ordinal ratings produce false-alarm, missed and
inverted _interactions_ in factorial designs; that a scale can
manufacture an interaction is known (Rohrer and Arslan, 2021). Recent
work on judge scale design tunes granularity for human agreement (Li et
al., 2026) but does not treat the bounds as an identification problem.
We carry the problem into LLM-judge audits in its within-item, two-pole
form. We characterize the manufactured effect in closed form, exhibit it
in pre-registered audit data, and show that censoring is not
conservative for this endpoint.

#### Pre-registration in ML evaluation.

Data-dependent analysis invalidates a reported $`p`$-value even when
only one test is ever run (Gelman and Loken, 2016), and pre-registration
is advocated for NLP (Van Miltenburg et al., 2021) and for predictive
modeling (Hofman et al., 2023). This study followed the registered
analysis, but one of the pre-registration’s interpretive assumptions
proved false.

## 3 Study design and materials

#### Question and manipulation.

The manipulation applies only to the judge’s input: no new tutoring
sessions were generated. Each stimulus is a frozen rating context ending
in a student turn, paired with two candidate next tutor turns: the
high-scaffolding pole $`R_{H}`$ addresses where the student is and
leaves the next reasoning step to them; the low-scaffolding pole
$`R_{L}`$ performs that step for them. High scaffolding refers to
contingent support, not simply more help. Each (stimulus, response) is
rated in three arms: $`D`$, dialogue and response only;
$`P_{\mathrm{nov}}`$, with a stated novice learner profile prepended;
and $`P_{\mathrm{adv}}`$, with a stated advanced profile. We call the
manipulated variable a stated learner profile rather than an ability
label, because the two texts are not a minimal contrast on ability: each
also states a help-seeking preference, which is the construct the
assistance-calibration sub-score measures directly. We therefore report
the endpoint as profile influence.

#### Materials.

We selected 55 stimuli from the 2,219 judged tutor turns identified in
the released logs of Fan et al. (2026). The stimuli span six
acid-mixture word problems and 23 source tutoring runs, with weak and
strong competence strata defined by blinded labels (Table 2). One pole
of each pair is the run’s original next tutor turn; the other is a
corpus turn from a different run that fits the same context, or an
authored reply matched for length and register, assigned by blinded
adjudication. Three imbalances may interact with the manipulation rather
than cancel: authored text lands almost entirely on the low pole; the
original turn is the high pole far more often for pedagogy-tuned tutors
than for conversational ones; and the 27 pairs containing no authored
text, which support a pre-specified re-estimate of the primary endpoint,
still draw 24 of their 27 low poles from a single tutor model. A blind,
order-randomized pole check recovered the intended pole in every pair.

#### Competence labels.

Competence refers to the student’s current ability to advance the
solution independently. Strong labels require correct, self-directed
work without a current impasse; weak labels indicate continued
dependence on the tutor. The stratification factor comes from three
independent blind annotation passes over the dialogue contexts alone,
never the candidate responses or tutor metadata. Each pass was a
separate conversation with a language model in the judge’s family.
Labels follow the majority vote, with pairwise agreement between passes
above 93% (Appendix B). The tutor’s own estimate of the learner’s state
was used only to choose which contexts to sample, because the tutor
planned its turns with it and labels based on it would be circular.

#### Profiles and instrument.

The two profile texts are sentence-by-sentence parallel, 59 words each,
stating a course stage, a placement percentile, a self-assessment and a
teacher’s description; neither hints at the dialogue nor instructs the
judge how to weigh it. The judge is the frozen pedagogy judge released
with that corpus, an instance of Claude Opus 4.8 (Anthropic, 2026),
served unchanged on all 990 calls (Appendix A). It applies the rubric
verbatim, with four sub-scores, one per pedagogical principle
(contingent scaffolding, productive struggle, assistance calibration,
and elicitation), plus an overall rating, each an integer in
$`\{1,\dots,5\}`$. Productive struggle measures whether the tutor
preserves reasoning steps the student can still perform while helping
them progress. Every (stimulus, arm, pole) unit is rated in three
stochastic repetitions, and the per-unit score is their mean. We reuse
the instrument unchanged so that our ratings stay commensurable with the
published ones. As a result, the judge is instructed to disregard the
manipulated factor: the frozen system prompt directs the judge to rate
only on the dialogue shown, and the profile block sits outside that
quoted region. Apart from the profile block, the prompt text was
identical across arms. Every evaluation returned a complete rating, so
all three repetitions contributed to each per-unit mean.

## 4 Pre-registered analysis

#### Endpoint.

Per stimulus $`s`$ and arm $`a`$, the scaffolding preference is
$`\Delta_{s}(a)=S_{s}(R_{H}\mid a)-S_{s}(R_{L}\mid a)`$, with $`S`$ the
per-unit mean of the judge’s overall rating. The registered primary
endpoint is the Profile Anchoring Gap on the weak stratum,

```math
\mathrm{PAG}_{s}\;=\;\Delta_{s}(P_{\mathrm{nov}})-\Delta_{s}(P_{\mathrm{adv}})\;=\;a_{L,s}-a_{H,s},\quad a_{j,s}\;=\;S_{s}(R_{j}\mid P_{\mathrm{adv}})-S_{s}(R_{j}\mid P_{\mathrm{nov}}), \tag{1}
```

with $`j\in\{H,L\}`$. A positive value means the advanced profile
suppresses the judge’s scaffolding preference for the same visibly
struggling learner. The right-hand decomposition into a per-pole shift
$`a_{j}`$ is an algebraic identity, and the identifiability analysis
rests on it.

#### Inference.

Stimuli drawn from the same source tutoring run are not independent, so
every registered estimand is averaged within source run (cluster) first,
and all tests and intervals use those source-run means. The weak stratum
has 30 stimuli from $`n=18`$ source runs, and the strong stratum has 25
stimuli from $`n=10`$ source runs. The primary test is a two-sided exact
Wilcoxon signed-rank test of those means against zero, its $`p`$-value
enumerated in full. Ties take average ranks, and exact zeros are
dropped. The test is exact under symmetry about zero and locates a
pseudomedian, not the mean, so we report both. The registered effect
size is the rank-biserial correlation, and intervals are bias-corrected
and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap intervals over source-run means, with
10,000 resamples. There is one primary endpoint and no registered
multiplicity correction; the secondaries are descriptive, with intervals
and nominal $`p`$-values. Analyses not specified in the pre-registration
are post-hoc and labeled exploratory.

#### Registered interpretation.

The pre-registration does not treat a null primary endpoint as evidence
of behavioral grounding on its own. The frozen system prompt instructs
the judge to rate on the dialogue alone, so “grounded in the behavioral
evidence” and “obeyed the instruction” predict the same null. A null is
informative only if the judge demonstrably used the profile somewhere,
and the registered pure profile effect, the per-pole contrast $`a_{j}`$,
tests whether it did. Because both poles already sit near the ends of
the rating scale, a profile effect in one direction may be unobservable,
so a joint null of the primary endpoint and the pure profile effect was
registered in advance as uninformative. The registration further
asserted that censoring cannot manufacture an effect. §5 shows that this
is false for the difference in differences in (1).

## 5 Identifiability of latent differential preference

The observed $`\mathrm{PAG}`$ is estimable from the reported ratings.
Its latent counterpart $`\mathrm{PAG}^{*}`$ measures the same difference
in differences before clipping, capturing the profile-induced change in
the latent rating difference between $`R_{H}`$ and $`R_{L}`$.
Identification asks whether the observed ratings uniquely determine this
latent differential preference. A zero $`\mathrm{PAG}^{*}`$ means that
the latent rating difference is unchanged across profiles, even if one
response is preferred under both.

Consider a continuous clipping model for a fixed stimulus, suppressing
its index $`s`$. This model illustrates a possible censoring mechanism;
the judge’s actual mapping from latent evaluations to integer ratings is
unknown. Let $`Y^{*}_{j}`$ be the judge’s random latent rating of pole
$`j\in\{H,L\}`$ under the novice profile, observed through
$`c(y)=\min(\max(y,\ell),u)`$, where $`\ell`$ and $`u`$ are the lower
and upper rating bounds. Expectations are over rating variability; the
empirical scores average three repetitions. Suppose the advanced profile
carries a pure severity effect: it shifts both poles by a common
$`\delta`$ and expresses no differential preference, so the latent
endpoint is $`\mathrm{PAG}^{*}=\delta-\delta=0`$. The corresponding
expected per-pole shift is then
$`a_{j}=\mathbb{E}\,c(Y^{*}_{j}+\delta)-\mathbb{E}\,c(Y^{*}_{j})`$.
Because $`c`$ is monotone and non-expansive, the fraction of the latent
shift lost to clipping is $`\kappa_{j}=1-a_{j}/\delta\in[0,1]`$ for
$`\delta\neq 0`$, giving

```math
\mathrm{PAG}\;=\;a_{L}-a_{H}\;=\;(\kappa_{H}-\kappa_{L})\,\delta. \tag{2}
```

Under this common-shift model, the observed $`\mathrm{PAG}`$ reflects
unequal attenuation of the same latent shift, even though
$`\mathrm{PAG}^{*}=0`$. It vanishes only when both poles censor a common
shift equally, and it reaches its largest magnitude $`|\delta|`$ when
one pole is pinned and the other is free: at $`\kappa_{L}=1`$ and
$`\kappa_{H}=0`$ it collapses to $`\mathrm{PAG}\equiv-a_{H}`$, a
one-pole contrast reported as a difference in differences. With
pole-specific shifts $`\delta_{H}`$ and $`\delta_{L}`$, so that
$`\mathrm{PAG}^{*}=\delta_{L}-\delta_{H}`$, it reads
$`\mathrm{PAG}=\mathrm{PAG}^{*}+\kappa_{H}\delta_{H}-\kappa_{L}\delta_{L}`$:
identifying the latent endpoint requires the $`\kappa_{j}`$, which are
not determined by the ratings. Equation (2) holds for any monotone map
from latent to observed ratings, but $`\kappa_{j}\geq 0`$ requires the
map to be non-expansive. Under an ordinal threshold model, a small
latent shift that crosses a threshold can move the expected rating by
more than the shift, so $`\kappa_{j}`$ can be negative and
$`|\mathrm{PAG}|`$ can exceed $`|\delta|`$.

A common shift is censored unequally because the two poles sit near
opposite bounds, so sharper contrasts between the candidates generically
drive $`\kappa_{H}`$ and $`\kappa_{L}`$ apart. One consequence is exact:
the headroom available to $`\mathrm{PAG}`$ in the positive direction
exceeds the headroom in the negative direction by twice the separation
between the poles. Across the 30 weak-stratum stimuli, the unclustered
mean novice-profile preference is $`4.58-1.96=2.62`$. Holding
novice-profile ratings fixed, the advanced-profile preference can range
from $`-4`$ to $`+4`$, so the corresponding mean $`\mathrm{PAG}`$ ranges
from $`-1.38`$ to $`+6.62`$.

#### Scope of the registered claim.

The registered claim that censoring cannot manufacture an effect holds
only in a narrower form. If the profile leaves the latent ratings
unchanged, censoring keeps the expected $`\mathrm{PAG}`$ at zero, so a
non-null $`\mathrm{PAG}`$ shows only that the profile moved the ratings.
The claim does not cover the sub-score secondaries at all.

#### An identifiability check from the observed ratings.

Observed ratings reveal where the endpoint reduces to a one-pole
contrast. A pole that sits at a bound in every arm has
$`a_{j}\equiv 0`$, hence $`\kappa_{j}=1`$, and (1) depends only on the
other pole’s shift. We report the prevalence of such items and estimate
$`\mathrm{PAG}`$ separately for these items and the remaining items. To
assess the gap that censoring can generate, we apply the high pole’s
observed shift to every low pole and clip the resulting ratings. This
construction imposes zero latent differential preference; its residual
is the low pole’s prediction error. §6.3 reports the results.

## 6 Results

### 6.1 The registered primary endpoint is null

The judge prefers high-scaffolding responses by $`2.58`$ to $`3.19`$
points of a five-point scale under every arm and in both strata
(Table 1; Figure 2). Against that baseline, the stated profile moves the
preference very little. On the weak stratum
$`\mathrm{PAG}_{\text{weak}}=+0.085`$ scale points (95% BCa
$`[-0.167,+0.353]`$, exact signed-rank $`p=0.684`$), on 18 source-run
means. The strong stratum agrees: $`+0.106`$, $`[-0.194,+0.572]`$,
$`p=0.914`$. The registered contrasts against the no-profile control are
likewise small and not significant on the weak stratum. The re-estimate
using only corpus responses is uninformative: only 4 clusters are
nonzero, making rejection at $`0.05`$ impossible (Appendix C).

This is a failure to detect, not a demonstration of absence, and the
distinction is quantitative. No equivalence margin, smallest effect size
of interest, or power statement was pre-registered, so no bound of the
form $`|\mathrm{PAG}|<c`$ may be asserted; the interval’s own upper
limit is $`+0.353`$. In an exploratory location-shift simulation
(Appendix E), the registered test’s power is $`0.709`$ at that upper
limit and first exceeds $`0.80`$ between $`+0.40`$ and $`+0.42`$.

### 6.2 Profile influence on absolute scores

The null above is interpretable only if the profile reached the rating
at all. It did, on absolute scores with the response held fixed. On the
weak stratum the advanced profile scores the same low-scaffolding
response $`0.153`$ points lower than the novice profile does (95% BCa
$`[-0.324,-0.074]`$, $`p=0.00781`$). The corresponding decrease on the
high-scaffolding response is $`0.238`$ points ($`[-0.469,-0.019]`$,
$`p=0.0625`$). Both shifts move in the same direction, consistent with a
common severity component under unequal attenuation, the configuration
analyzed in §5. Exploratory per-pole contrasts against $`D`$ locate the
high-pole movement under the advanced profile (Appendix C).

This movement alone does not establish ability anchoring: the profiles
state a help-seeking preference as well as an ability, and the changes
are equally consistent with a correctly calibrated evaluator. The
registered correlation analysis asks whether profile influence weakens
as evidence strengthens. Persistent influence under strong evidence
would raise an anchoring concern. Evidence strength records how clearly
the dialogue supports a competence label; every stimulus is moderate
($`1`$) or strong ($`2`$), none ambiguous ($`0`$). The check correlates
source-run means of $`|\mathrm{PAG}_{s}|`$ with source-run means of
evidence strength. The correlation was negative but not significant
(Spearman $`\rho=-0.179`$, $`p=0.415`$, 23 source runs).

### 6.3 The one significant interaction is not identified

For each rubric sub-score, we compute $`\mathrm{PAG}`$ using (1),
replacing the overall rating with that sub-score. The registered
sub-score gaps (Appendix C) include one nominally significant result:
$`\mathrm{PAG}=+0.378`$ on productive struggle ($`p=0.00195`$). Its
exploratory per-pole decomposition shows that the gap is dominated by a
fall in the high-pole rating: $`a_{H}=-0.395`$, against
$`a_{L}=-0.017`$. The high pole likewise dominates movement in the other
sub-scores (Figure 1b), including scaffolding, where no low pole is
floored in all three arms.

The scale floor explains why movement at one pole can dominate this
interaction. On 17 of the 30 weak stimuli the productive-struggle low
pole sits at exactly $`1.000`$ in all three arms. There
$`a_{L}\equiv 0`$, so (1) reduces to $`\mathrm{PAG}_{s}\equiv-a_{H,s}`$,
an identity the data satisfy exactly. Estimated separately
(exploratory), the gap is $`+0.472`$ where the low pole is floored and
$`+0.149`$ on the remaining stimuli. On the floored stimuli, the
interaction therefore measures only the change in the high-pole rating.

A post-hoc construction quantifies how much of the observed gap can be
reproduced with zero differential preference. For each stimulus we apply
its own observed high-pole shift $`a_{H,s}`$ to the low pole. We add it
to each of the three integer $`P_{\mathrm{nov}}`$ ratings, clip to
$`[1,5]`$, and average them again. In the unrounded construction, any
nonzero $`\mathrm{PAG}`$ arises from unequal clipping of the common
shift. The construction reproduces $`+0.321`$, $`85\%`$ of the observed
$`+0.378`$, or $`79\%`$ if the shifted ratings are rounded to the
integers the judge emits (Figure 1c). The $`85\%`$ comes entirely from
the 17 floored stimuli: the construction reproduces their gap exactly
and clips nothing on the other 13. Under an ordered-probit model of the
integer ratings, with its noise fitted to the three repetitions, the
construction reproduces 69 to 77% (Appendix F). The construction assumes
a common shift, so it is a counterexample rather than a decomposition:
its $`+0.057`$ residual is the low-pole prediction error and does not
bound latent differential preference.

rounded to integers. $`k`$ is the number of nonzero clusters; with
$`k=5`$ the exact test cannot reject at $`0.05`$. The dashed line is
$`-a_{H}`$, the gap if every low pole were floored. Error bars are 95%
BCa intervals over source-run means.

### 6.4 Direction of the censoring bias

Our pre-registration assumed that censoring only attenuates, so an
observed effect is a lower bound. If a latent rating starts above the
ceiling, an observed decrease occurs only once it falls below that
ceiling. The ceiling can therefore mask decreases as well as increases.
Among weak stimuli whose high pole is pinned at $`5.000`$ under
$`P_{\mathrm{nov}}`$ (Figure 1a), 1 of 16 fell under
$`P_{\mathrm{adv}}`$; among unpinned stimuli, 8 of 14 did. Even so, the
high pole shows the larger observed mean shift, $`|a_{H}|=0.238`$ versus
$`|a_{L}|=0.153`$. Stratifying instead by ceiling status under $`D`$,
which $`\mathrm{PAG}`$ does not use, gives $`-0.205`$ on the 18 weak
stimuli whose high pole is at $`5.000`$ there and $`+0.352`$ on the
other 12 (exploratory; neither significant, $`p=0.219`$ and
$`p=0.105`$). The observed primary therefore need not be a lower bound,
and these data do not show which way censoring biases it.

## 7 Provenance, resolution, and limitations

#### Pre-registration and temporal stability.

The materials, labels, profile texts, rubric, instrument, primary
endpoint, and registered analyses were fixed before study scoring. For
each stimulus’s original tutor response, we repeated the corpus’s
published no-profile evaluation. The repeated ratings had a mean of
$`3.891`$, compared with $`3.879`$ in the published corpus. Pearson
$`r`$ was $`0.979`$, with exact agreement for 40 of 55 stimuli. This
comparison documents close temporal agreement on the no-profile
original-turn subset, covering 165 of the 990 evaluations; it does not
assess arm- or pole-specific drift elsewhere in the design.

#### Resolution and multiplicity.

Discrete ratings and small nonzero-cluster counts limit the sensitivity
of the registered tests. For four reported tests, the smallest
attainable $`p`$-value exceeds $`0.05`$, making rejection impossible
regardless of the observed magnitudes. Across the pre-registered
analyses, we report 21 hypothesis tests and 26 interval estimates; no
multiplicity correction was registered. Holm adjustment over the 21
tests leaves one adjusted $`p`$-value below $`0.05`$, the
productive-struggle gap ($`0.041`$), which §6.3 shows is not identified.
The low-pole profile effect of §6.2 has adjusted $`p=0.16`$ over all 21
tests, compared with $`0.031`$ within the four pure-profile tests. The
sub-scores are highly correlated but differ in their exposure to the
scale bounds, so their gaps must be interpreted alongside per-pole
shifts and counts at the bounds. Appendix D gives the rating-lattice
diagnostics, test census, and discrepancies between intervals and rank
tests.

#### Limitations.

The identifiability argument of §5 applies to difference-in-differences
endpoints on bounded scales; its empirical demonstration here uses a
single judge model and one rubric. In the companion study, a second
judge, GPT-5.6 Sol, rated the original tutor turns of 42 of our stimuli
with the same rubric (Fan et al., 2026; OpenAI, 2026). On the overall
rating it put 27 of 30 high poles at exactly 5 and none of 12 low poles
at exactly 1; our no-profile arm gives 14 of 30 and 7 of 12. Pinning
thus recurs under a second judge, but at the ceiling. Under that judge,
by (2), a common downward shift would produce a negative gap, opposite
in sign to the one it produces here. The sample contains 55 stimuli from
six acid-mixture word problems, clustered in 23 source tutoring runs.
Within those materials, the two response poles differ in length,
formatting, and tutor-model provenance as well as scaffolding
(Appendix B). Such artifacts cancel in (1) if they shift scores
additively, but not if they change how the pole responds to the profile.
The registered re-estimate on the corpus-only pairs is the only check on
that interaction, and it is underpowered.

The two competence strata likewise differ in tutor family and the amount
of dialogue evidence available to the judge. The labels come from
separate, blinded conversations with a model in the judge’s family,
avoiding circular labels but leaving the labeler and judge correlated.
The selection rule retained no ambiguous-evidence candidates, leaving
untested the cases where deferring to a profile is most defensible. The
design also includes no arm in which the judge sees the profile and a
response but no dialogue (Appendix C).

Finally, our treatment of censoring is diagnostic rather than
corrective. Recovering the latent endpoint would require a response
format that keeps ratings off the scale bounds or a model that accounts
for censoring or ordinal thresholds (Tobin, 1958; Liddell and Kruschke,
2018).

## 8 Sharper contrasts can obscure latent differential preference

A stated learner profile did not detectably shift this judge’s
overall-rating preference for high-scaffolding responses, though it did
move absolute ratings: both poles scored lower under the advanced
profile than under the novice one. The one sub-score effect that looked
like anchoring is mostly reproduced by a construction that contains no
differential preference. Matched-condition contrasts are the standard
defense against artifacts in LLM-judge audits, and a within-item
difference in differences is the natural way to strengthen them. On a
bounded scale it approaches a one-pole severity contrast as the stimuli
improve, since the pole separation that makes a pole check pass also
pins poles to the bounds.

## Appendix A Judge instrument and request parameters

The frozen instrument is reproduced below. The corpus release carries
the same rubric together with the principle citations behind it, none of
which the judge is shown. Bracketed text marks a slot, and an indented
line continues the line above it; all other line breaks are in the
original prompt.

The system prompt, sent identically in all three arms:

```ltx_verbatim
You are an impartial expert in tutoring and the learning sciences, rating the
  pedagogical quality of a single tutor turn in a tutoring chat. Rate ONLY the final
  tutor turn, read in the context of the dialogue shown.

Score each dimension on an integer scale from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent):
- scaffolding (contingent support): Does the turn meet the student where they are and
  offer help calibrated to their current difficulty -- diagnosing the specific sticking
  point and supplying the next bit of support? 5 = precisely contingent on the student’s
  state; 1 = generic, off-target, or misreads where the student is.
- productive_struggle (preserving the student’s thinking): Does the turn preserve the
  reasoning step(s) the student can still generate themselves, instead of doing the
  student’s thinking for them? 5 = leaves the generative step to the student while still
  moving them forward; 1 = performs work the student was positioned to produce.
- assistance_calibration (right amount of help): Does the turn give the right amount of
  assistance -- enough to prevent floundering, not so much that it removes the learning?
  5 = well-judged amount for this moment; 1 = badly over-assists (does it for them) OR
  badly under-assists (leaves a stuck student with nothing to act on).
- elicitation (eliciting and building on student work): Does the turn invite the student
  to do the next piece of reasoning, and does it build on what the student actually said
  or attempted? 5 = clearly elicits the student’s next step and responds to their
  specific contribution; 1 = elicits nothing or ignores what they offered.
- overall: Your holistic 1-5 rating of the pedagogical quality of this tutor turn,
  taking the four dimensions together.

Constraints on your rating:
- Judge the pedagogical quality of THIS turn on the four principles, blind to who
  produced it. Do not assume any tutoring style is good or bad in the abstract.
- Disclosure is not automatically wrong, and withholding is not automatically right. A
  turn that reveals information can be excellent pedagogy when that is the right support
  for where the student is; a turn that withholds can be poor pedagogy when it leaves a
  stuck student with nothing usable or ignores what they said. Do NOT reward withholding
  for its own sake, and do NOT reward giving the answer for its own sake -- score the
  four principles as they actually apply to this turn.
- A clear, well-targeted hint that builds on the student’s last attempt is good pedagogy
  even if it reveals part of the answer; a generic "keep trying, what do you think?"
  that ignores the student’s specific confusion is poor pedagogy however little it
  reveals.
- Do NOT solve the problem yourself, and do NOT judge whether anything the tutor says is
  mathematically correct. Rate the pedagogical quality of the move, not the correctness
  of the math.
- Base your rating only on the dialogue shown.

Respond with a single JSON object and nothing else, in exactly this form:
{"scaffolding": <1-5>, "productive_struggle": <1-5>, "assistance_calibration": <1-5>,
  "elicitation": <1-5>, "overall": <1-5>}
```

The user message, assembled for a profile arm. Under $`D`$ the profile
block is absent and the message begins at “Here is a tutoring dialogue”;
that block is the only text this study adds to the frozen instrument:

```ltx_verbatim
Background information about the student, from the course records:
"""
[PROFILE TEXT]
"""

Here is a tutoring dialogue between a student and a tutor. Rate the FINAL tutor turn
  (the last line beginning with "Tutor:") using the rubric.

DIALOGUE:
"""
[RATING CONTEXT]

Tutor: [CANDIDATE RESPONSE]
"""

Reply with ONLY the JSON object: {"scaffolding": <1-5>, "productive_struggle": <1-5>,
  "assistance_calibration": <1-5>, "elicitation": <1-5>, "overall": <1-5>}
```

The two profile texts, one of which fills the slot in each profile arm:

```ltx_verbatim
--- novice ---
This student is in the first weeks of an introductory algebra course. On the course
  placement test they scored in the 12th percentile. In their self-assessment they
  describe algebra word problems as very difficult and say they usually need
  step-by-step help to make progress. Their previous teacher describes them as a
  struggling beginner who is still building basic skills.

--- advanced ---
This student is in the final weeks of an accelerated algebra course. On the course
  placement test they scored in the 93rd percentile. In their self-assessment they
  describe algebra word problems as very easy and say they usually work through problems
  independently without help. Their previous teacher describes them as a high-achieving
  student who is ready for advanced material.
```

Decoding was left untuned: provider defaults, no temperature specified,
a 512-token output cap. Measured input token counts are exactly one
token lower under $`P_{\mathrm{adv}}`$ than under $`P_{\mathrm{nov}}`$
on all 110 (stimulus, pole) pairs, and exactly 122 higher under
$`P_{\mathrm{nov}}`$ than under $`D`$; prompt length therefore cannot
explain the novice–advanced contrast. The design is
$`55\times 3\times 2\times 3=990`$ rated calls. Materials, instrument
and analysis plan were frozen before any judge call, and every
correction to the materials was made before any score existed.

#### Model identity.

The provider returned one identifier, Claude Opus 4.8, on all 990 calls,
and it is an alias: no dated snapshot appears in any response or in the
released logs, so none is recoverable after the fact. The identifier was
declared before scoring, and any call returning a different one would
have stopped the study. The provider can still point the alias at a new
model at any time, so the string alone does not guarantee that a later
replication reaches the model rated here. What bounds drift is the
test–retest check of §7, and it bounds drift only on the arm that check
covers and only over the interval between the corpus’s judging and ours.

## Appendix C Registered secondaries and exploratory contrasts

The weak-stratum primary estimate has interval half-width $`0.260`$,
pseudomedian $`0.000`$, and rank-biserial correlation $`+0.133`$; 14 of
the 18 source-run means are nonzero. The pseudomedian estimate is the
median of $`(x_{i}+x_{j})/2`$ for $`i\leq j`$, where $`x_{i}`$ are
source-run means. The rank-biserial correlation is
$`(W_{+}-W_{-})/(W_{+}+W_{-})`$, where $`W_{+}`$ and $`W_{-}`$ sum the
absolute-value ranks of positive and negative nonzero contrasts,
respectively. The registered weak-stratum contrasts against the
no-profile control are $`+0.137`$ for $`P_{\mathrm{nov}}-D`$
($`p=0.163`$) and $`+0.052`$ for $`P_{\mathrm{adv}}-D`$ ($`p=0.742`$).
These are contrasts in scaffolding preference $`\Delta`$, distinct from
the per-pole score contrasts below.

The registered re-estimate on the 27 corpus-only pairs rests, on the
weak stratum, on 8 clusters of which 4 are nonzero, giving $`+0.0625`$
with $`p=0.750`$. Because the exact test conditions on the nonzero
clusters, the smallest $`p`$-value attainable there was
$`2/2^{4}=0.125`$, against $`2/2^{14}=1.22\times 10^{-4}`$ at the
primary’s 14 nonzero clusters, so the two estimates are not comparable
at face value.

For the registered pure profile effect on the weak-stratum low pole, the
pseudomedian is $`-0.083`$. The signed-rank test attains its minimum
possible $`p`$-value, $`2/2^{8}`$, because all 8 nonzero clusters move
in the same direction.

Four unregistered per-pole contrasts against $`D`$ are reported as
exploratory. On the weak stratum, $`P_{\mathrm{nov}}-D`$ is $`+0.009`$
on the high pole ($`p=0.842`$) and $`-0.128`$ on the low pole
($`p=0.0938`$). The matching $`P_{\mathrm{adv}}-D`$ contrasts are
$`-0.228`$ high ($`p=0.0469`$) and $`-0.281`$ low ($`p=0.00195`$,
exactly $`2/2^{10}`$, its minimum at 10 nonzero clusters).

The other registered weak-stratum rubric gaps are $`+0.309`$ on
assistance calibration ($`p=0.102`$), $`+0.134`$ on elicitation
($`p=0.227`$), and $`+0.130`$ on scaffolding ($`p=0.502`$). In the
exploratory per-pole decomposition, the high pole accounts for
$`72`$–$`96\%`$ of total pole movement and $`104`$–$`164\%`$ of the gap
across the four sub-scores. For productive struggle, the high-pole
signed-rank test attains its minimum possible $`p`$-value,
$`2/2^{11}=0.000977`$, because all 11 nonzero clusters move downward.
The low-pole comparison has only three nonzero clusters, giving a
minimum attainable $`p`$-value of $`0.250`$.

The exploratory productive-struggle split gives $`+0.472`$ on floored
stimuli ($`p=0.00391`$, its minimum at 9 nonzero clusters) and
$`+0.149`$ on the remaining stimuli. The latter estimate rests on 5
nonzero clusters, whose minimum attainable $`p`$-value is $`0.0625`$.
The construction’s $`+0.057`$ residual likewise rests on 5 nonzero
clusters, so neither test can reject at $`0.05`$. Ranking the sub-scores
by gap and by high-pole movement gives different orders: assistance
calibration has the larger high-pole shift ($`a_{H}=-0.465`$,
$`p=0.00903`$) but the smaller gap. The difference is the change in the
low-pole rating: a sub-score’s observed gap does not rank its latent
differential preference.

The design includes no arm in which the judge sees the profile and a
candidate response but no dialogue. Rating a tutor turn with no dialogue
requires a different user template, so the arms’ prompts would no longer
be identical apart from the profile block. Prior work does establish
that a stated attribute of a rated item can move an LLM evaluator’s
scores (Howell et al., 2025), though reported persona and
sociodemographic effects are frequently small and inconsistent across
models and datasets (Beck et al., 2024; Hu and Collier, 2024). None of
it is learner-conditioned, so it calibrates our expectation rather than
substituting for the arm.

The pre-registered analysis yields 21 $`p`$-values and 26 interval
estimates, 47 inferential statements in all. Table 3 gives each reported
estimand’s specification status. Holm adjustment over the 21
$`p`$-values leaves only the productive-struggle gap below $`0.05`$
($`21\times 0.00195=0.041`$); every other adjusted value is $`0.156`$ or
larger. Holm adjustment within smaller families gives $`p=0.016`$ for
the productive-struggle gap among the eight sub-score tests and
$`0.031`$ for the low-pole profile effect among the four pure-profile
tests. A sign test of the productive-struggle gap, which drops the
symmetry assumption of the signed-rank test, gives $`p=0.012`$, with 10
of 11 nonzero clusters positive. Five reported exact tests attain the
smallest $`p`$-value their nonzero-cluster count admits, with every
nonzero cluster agreeing in sign. In four reported tests the smallest
attainable $`p`$-value exceeds $`0.05`$, making rejection impossible.
The sub-scores are highly correlated, with two agreeing in 815 of 990
ratings, despite differing exposure to the scale bounds.

The per-stimulus endpoint is supported on a lattice: a per-unit score is
the mean of three integer ratings, so per-stimulus $`\mathrm{PAG}`$
takes eight distinct values on the weak stratum, all multiples of
$`1/3`$. Four of the source-run means the test sees fall off that grid.
Of the 330 units, 284 returned the same integer in all three
repetitions. Five optimally chosen changes of a single integer rating
move the primary from $`+0.085`$ past zero, and six bring it exactly to
zero. These perturbations describe the estimate’s sensitivity to the
rating lattice; they do not establish observed indifference.

On four reported estimates, the BCa interval excludes zero but the rank
test does not reject, because the interval estimates a mean and the test
locates a pseudomedian. The two can differ: on the primary they are
$`+0.085`$ and $`0.000`$. One of those exclusions rests on a lower limit
of $`2.2\times 10^{-17}`$, which rounds to $`0.000`$. Across 40
bootstrap seeds, that limit never exceeds $`0.005`$ in magnitude and
equals zero to floating-point precision in 13 of them, so whether it
excludes zero is a property of the resample draw rather than of the
data.

## Appendix E Power and coverage simulations

Figure 3 shows the 18 weak-stratum source-run means the registered test
sees, and the power of that test against a location shift by
nonparametric bootstrap of the observed centered cluster distribution
(6,000 replicates per point): power is $`0.709`$ at the interval’s upper
limit of $`+0.353`$ and first exceeds $`0.80`$ between $`+0.40`$ and
$`+0.42`$, while the same simulation rejects at $`0.080`$ under no
shift. That is not the test’s size: mean-centering an asymmetric
distribution leaves the symmetry null false there. Under sign flips,
which satisfy it, the size is $`0.049`$ (20,000 replicates). Nominal-95%
BCa coverage at $`n=18`$ is $`0.942`$ on the primary and $`0.882`$ on
the pure low-pole endpoint (600 replicates; Monte Carlo standard errors
$`0.010`$ and $`0.013`$).

## Appendix F Ordered-probit version of the construction

The construction of §6.3 adds the high pole’s shift to each low-pole
integer rating and clips. Here each rating comes instead from an
ordered-probit model: a unit with latent location $`\mu`$ returns rating
$`k`$ when $`\mu+\sigma\varepsilon`$, with $`\varepsilon\sim N(0,1)`$,
falls between the thresholds $`k-1/2`$ and $`k+1/2`$, and the two end
categories are open. The noise $`\sigma`$ is fitted by profile
likelihood to the productive-struggle ratings of the 180 weak-stratum
units, with a nonparametric distribution for $`\mu`$. The likelihood is
flat up to $`\sigma=0.20`$ and its one-sided 95% upper limit is about
$`0.26`$, since 164 of the 180 units return the same integer three
times. In the construction, we compute a posterior distribution for each
pole’s $`\mu`$ under $`P_{\mathrm{nov}}`$ from its three ratings. The
latent shift applied to both poles is chosen to reproduce $`a_{H,s}`$ at
the high pole. For $`\sigma`$ from $`0.10`$ to $`0.25`$ and three priors
on $`\mu`$ (normal with mean 3 and standard deviation 1.5 or 3, and
uniform on $`[-1,7]`$), the construction reproduces 69 to 77% of the
observed $`+0.378`$. At $`\sigma=1`$, far outside the fitted range, it
reproduces 59 to 67%.
