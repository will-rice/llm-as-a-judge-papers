# Will the Judge Flip? Predicting Position-Sensitive LLM Judgments from Residual Stream Activations

Hashmath Shaik    Gnaneswar Villuri    Alex Doboli
Affiliation: Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering
Affiliation: Stony Brook University Affiliation: Stony Brook, NY 11794,

Email: [{hashmath.shaik,gnaneswar.villuri,alex.doboli}@stonybrook.edu](mailto:)

###### Abstract

The order in which candidate responses are presented can change an LLM
judge’s verdict. Detecting such a _position flip_ ordinarily requires
judging each pair in both orders, which doubles the number of judgments.
We investigate whether residual stream activations recorded immediately
before the initial verdict can predict a flip. We use nested grouped
cross-validation to evaluate regularized linear probes on 534 JudgeBench
pairs for three Qwen3 judges and Llama-3.1-8B. The linear probes achieve
AUROCs of .621–.850 and outperform a combined baseline that uses
verbalized confidence, verdict-label logits, response lengths, and the
judge’s initial choice by .062–.113 AUROC. Linear probes trained on
JudgeBench and then frozen achieve AUROCs of .685–.853 on 1,802 MT-Bench
comparisons without MT-Bench fitting or recalibration. These results
show that pre-verdict activations support prediction of susceptibility
to candidate order and outperform the non-activation predictors
evaluated here.

## 1 Introduction

LLM-based judges compare model responses, but their verdicts can depend
on candidate order (Zheng et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024). A _position
flip_ occurs when exchanging the candidates changes the judge’s
preferred response identity. We denote the order with A first as $`AB`$
and the swapped order with B first as $`BA`$. Such flips are associated
with less reliable judgments: for our four judges, the $`AB`$ verdict
agrees with the JudgeBench reference on 60--69% of non-flipping pairs
but only 47--51% of flipping pairs.¹¹ 1 These are post-hoc accuracies
for the $`AB`$ evaluation. On a flipping pair, the $`AB`$ and $`BA`$
verdicts necessarily select opposite response identities, so their
accuracies sum to one; this comparison does not imply that exchanging
positions reduces accuracy.

Identifying a position flip conventionally requires evaluating the same
pair twice, once in each order. This procedure detects positional
sensitivity only after the $`BA`$ judgment, which it requires for every
pair, including those whose verdict remains unchanged. We therefore ask
whether information available during the $`AB`$ evaluation can predict
which comparisons are susceptible to a flip.

Our predictor is deliberately simple: an L2-regularized linear probe
trained on a residual stream activation recorded immediately before the
$`AB`$ verdict. The linear probe receives no information from the $`BA`$
evaluation, which is used only to construct the position-flip label.

We address three research questions: RQ1: Can a residual stream
activation from the $`AB`$ evaluation predict whether the judgment will
flip? RQ2: Does this representation predict flips better than
non-activation features from the $`AB`$ evaluation, including the
judge’s $`AB`$ choice? RQ3: Does a linear probe trained on JudgeBench
transfer to MT-Bench without adaptation?

Across all four judges, the linear probes predict held-out position
flips and outperform the combined non-activation baseline. The signal
also transfers to MT-Bench without retraining or recalibration. These
results establish linear decodability, but they do not show that the
model uses the decoded information causally or explicitly estimates
positional susceptibility.

#### Related work.

Prior work measures positional bias by comparing judgments across
candidate orders. MT-Bench documents this effect (Zheng et al., 2023),
and subsequent work shows that candidate order can alter model rankings
(Wang et al., 2024). Related studies examine the consistency, fairness,
and stability of reordered judgments (Shi et al., 2025), while
CalibraEval adjusts prediction distributions to reduce disagreement
after candidate swaps (Li et al., 2025). These studies characterize or
mitigate positional sensitivity. We instead seek to predict an
individual flip from the $`AB`$ evaluation alone.

Internal representations have also been used to estimate judge
reliability. Hidden-state probes can capture correctness uncertainty
(Radharapu et al., 2025), and cross-layer representations can improve
agreement between pointwise judge scores and human ratings (Lai et al.,
2025). Recent work also recovers human-preference signals from judge
activations and examines internal pathways carrying position and length
bias (Mukherjee and Sitaram, 2026). Our target differs from correctness,
human-preference recovery, and score alignment: we predict whether the
judge’s own preferred response identity will change under an order swap.
Probe performance establishes only that a property is decodable, not
that the model uses it causally (Hewitt and Liang, 2019). We therefore
interpret our results strictly as predictive evidence.

## 2 Method and Experimental Setup

#### Position-flip target.

Each instance contains a user request $`x`$ and two responses, A and B.
We map each verdict to the selected response’s original identity,
yielding $`c_{AB},c_{BA}\in\{A,B\}`$; A and B denote response identities
rather than presentation slots in both orders. The position-flip label
and aggregate position flip rate (PFR) are

```math
y_{\mathrm{pos}}=\mathbf{1}[c_{AB}\neq c_{BA}],\qquad\mathrm{PFR}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{\mathrm{pos}}^{(i)}. \tag{1}
```

Thus, $`y_{\mathrm{pos}}=1`$ means that exchanging the candidates
changes the selected response. The label captures susceptibility to
candidate order but does not distinguish a consistent preference for the
first or second candidate from position effects specific to a particular
comparison.

#### Residual stream activations and linear probe.

The linear probe uses a residual stream activation recorded immediately
before the verdict token. The fixed prompt requests brief reasoning, an
integer confidence score from 0 to 100, and a final single-token
verdict. We use greedy decoding with a 256-token generation allowance
and disable Qwen3 thinking mode. To extract activations from the $`AB`$
evaluation, we replay the saved token sequence through the same
token-by-token KV-cache path used during generation. We record this
activation at four predeclared relative depths: 25%, 50%, 75%, and the
penultimate layer. Let $`h_{AB}`$ denote the activation at a given
layer. For every example in the shared JudgeBench analysis set, replay
reproduces the recorded verdict as the greedy next token. We train the
linear probe as an L2-regularized logistic regression classifier:

```math
\hat{p}(y_{\mathrm{pos}}=1\mid h_{AB})=\sigma(w^{\top}h_{AB}+b). \tag{2}
```

We optimize the linear probe with deterministic L-BFGS and select both
the layer and $`\lambda\in\{10^{-4},10^{-3},10^{-2},10^{-1},1,10\}`$ by
validation Brier score. The $`BA`$ evaluation contributes only to the
target in Eq. 1; it supplies no feature to the linear probe.

#### Baselines.

All baselines use only information from the $`AB`$ evaluation and are
evaluated on the same held-out folds as the linear probe. The confidence
baseline uses the judge’s verbalized confidence score. The logit-margin
baseline uses $`z_{A}-z_{B}`$ and $`|z_{A}-z_{B}|`$, where $`z_{A}`$ and
$`z_{B}`$ are the next-token verdict logits mapped to responses A and B.
The length-difference baseline uses the signed and absolute differences
between the token lengths of responses A and B. The $`AB`$-choice
baseline indicates whether the $`AB`$ verdict selects A or B. The
_combined baseline_ uses all six features: confidence; signed and
absolute logit margin; signed and absolute length difference; and $`AB`$
choice. The _baseline without $`AB`$ choice_ omits this feature, and a
no-signal predictor uses the training-fold PFR. Every learned baseline
follows the same nested fitting procedure as the linear probe.

#### Data and judges.

JudgeBench (Tan et al., 2025) is our in-domain benchmark. Our JudgeBench
analysis pool contains 620 pairs. We use a 32,768-token context budget
in both candidate orders. Of these, 534 pairs from 470 question groups
are usable for every judge, which keeps the evaluated examples identical
across judges and predictors. Appendix Table 3 reports per-judge
retention.

MT-Bench (Zheng et al., 2023) provides the out-of-domain transfer data.
Deduplication yields 2,400 comparisons across 80 questions. The
human-judgment split adds no unique comparisons, and we do not use
preference labels. Each question contains 30 comparisons, so we group
examples by question. Of these, 1,802 comparisons from 79 questions are
usable for every judge; Appendix Table 3 reports per-judge retention.
Llama follows the required three-line format less consistently, although
99.4% of its outputs reach a verdict token. Our evaluation prompt
differs from the official FastChat templates, so the experiment tests
transfer across response-pair distributions and does not yield
official-protocol PFR estimates.

We evaluate Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, and Qwen3-8B in non-thinking mode
(Yang and others, 2025), together with Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct
(Grattafiori and others, 2024) as a cross-family replication. We
generate all judgments deterministically in bfloat16 on four 48-GB
NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs. Independent reproduction additionally requires
the exact checkpoint revisions, prompts, parser rules, and split
identifiers.

#### Evaluation.

On JudgeBench, we use five outer grouped folds and three inner grouped
folds shared across all judges and predictors. Grouping keeps variants
of the same question together, while feature standardization and model
selection are performed exclusively within the relevant training folds.
We concatenate predictions from the five held-out outer folds, producing
exactly one held-out prediction per comparison.

For MT-Bench transfer, grouped cross-validation over all 534 JudgeBench
pairs selects the hyperparameters. We then refit and freeze one linear
probe and its standardization on the complete JudgeBench set. No
fitting, threshold selection, or recalibration uses MT-Bench data.

We report PFR, AUROC, AUPRC, and Brier score (Brier, 1950).
Choice-matched AUROC evaluates rankings only between flipping and
non-flipping examples with the same $`AB`$ choice. We predeclare that we
will not report discrimination metrics when PFR falls below .05 or
exceeds .95; no judge–benchmark combination meets either condition.
Percentile 95% confidence intervals use 2,000 group-bootstrap resamples,
with the underlying question as the resampling unit. Confidence
intervals for differences between predictors use paired resamples.

The repeated-$`AB`$ control measures same-order variation under
deterministic inference. For this control, we select 100 pairs before
inference using a seeded hash of the pair identifier.

## 3 Results

### 3.1 Residual stream activations predict position flips (RQ1)

Residual stream activations from the $`AB`$ evaluation predict position
flips for every judge tested. Table 1 reports one held-out prediction
for each of the 534 shared JudgeBench pairs. All four AUROC confidence
intervals exclude .5; point estimates range from .621 for Llama-3.1-8B
to .850 for Qwen3-8B.

### 3.2 Residual stream activations outperform the combined baseline (RQ2)

The linear probe outperforms a combined baseline that includes the
judge’s $`AB`$ choice and other non-activation features. We include
$`AB`$ choice because the pre-verdict activation may encode it, making
that choice a simpler potential predictor of flipping. The baseline also
includes verbalized confidence, verdict-label logit margin, and
response-length difference. Linear-probe AUROC exceeds the combined
baseline by .062–.113 across the four judges, with every paired
confidence interval excluding zero (Appendix Table 5). The paired
intervals do not establish improvements on every metric: the Qwen3-4B
AUPRC interval and the Llama-3.1-8B Brier interval include zero.

Choice-matched comparisons confirm that $`AB`$ choice alone does not
explain the probe’s discrimination. Appendix Table 4 reports
choice-matched AUROC, which compares flipping and non-flipping examples
with the same $`AB`$ choice. Under this stricter comparison, the linear
probe improves over the combined baseline by .108–.126, and every paired
confidence interval excludes zero. Thus, the probe’s discrimination is
not explained by $`AB`$ choice alone and exceeds that of the fitted
combined baseline.

### 3.3 Transfer to a different benchmark (RQ3)

Linear probes trained on JudgeBench generalize to MT-Bench without
adaptation. They achieve AUROCs of .685–.853, with every 95% confidence
interval above .5 (Table 2; Appendix C). MT-Bench PFR ranges from .172
to .379, compared with .315 to .504 on JudgeBench. AUPRC exceeds
MT-Bench flip prevalence for every judge, and Brier score is lower than
that of a constant predictor fixed to JudgeBench prevalence. Because the
MT-Bench linear probes are refit on all JudgeBench examples, differences
between the two table blocks are not paired performance changes.

#### Repeated-$`AB`$ control.

On a predeclared 100-pair subset, repeating $`AB`$ changes no verdict,
whereas $`BA`$ changes 19–47 verdicts depending on the judge (Appendix
Table 3). This control provides no evidence of same-order verdict
variation on the tested subset under the same inference settings.

## 4 Limitations

We test four relatively small open-weight judges, including three Qwen3
models, on two response-pair distributions. PFR may depend on the judge,
data, or prompt; our evaluation prompt also precludes comparison with
official MT-Bench estimates. The shared analysis sets retain 86.1% of
our JudgeBench pool and 75.1% of the MT-Bench comparisons. Because
inclusion requires valid outputs in both candidate orders for every
judge, these results are conditional on format compliance and may not
generalize to excluded comparisons.

The combined baseline does not exhaust all measures of difficulty or
uncertainty, and the confidence-before-verdict format may weaken
verbalized confidence. Outperforming the fitted linear baseline does not
exclude nonlinear predictive relationships among its features. Other
controls or elicitation protocols may therefore change the RQ2
comparison.

Finally, linear decodability does not establish causal use, and residual
stream access excludes closed models. Linear-probe scores predict
position-flip risk rather than general reliability; untested settings
still require evaluating sampled pairs in both orders. We do not
evaluate a risk-threshold policy, realized judgment savings, or net
runtime including activation extraction.

## 5 Conclusion

Pre-verdict residual stream activations predict position flips across
the four judges tested. Linear probes outperform the combined baseline
on JudgeBench and transfer to MT-Bench without adaptation. These results
demonstrate predictive signal but not causal use.
