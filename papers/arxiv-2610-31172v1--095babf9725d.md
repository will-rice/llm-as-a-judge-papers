# Prompting Strategy Effects Depend on the Judge: A Multilingual Study of LLM-as-a-Judge Robustness

Asal Mehrabi Affiliation: University of Amsterdam, Amsterdam, The
Netherlands Email: <asalm.1381@gmail.com>    Mohammad Aliannejadi
Affiliation: University of Amsterdam, Amsterdam, The Netherlands
Email: <m.aliannejadi@uva.nl>    Clemencia Siro Affiliation: Centrum

###### Abstract

LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation is increasingly applied to multilingual tasks,
yet prompting strategies such as Chain-of-Thought and Role Conditioning
are recommended based solely on English benchmarks. Whether their
effectiveness transfers across judge models and languages remains
unknown: prompt-sensitivity studies are largely English-centric, while
existing multilingual judge studies fix the prompt template. In this
work, we address this gap by measuring this interaction through a
benchmark of 9 prompting strategies, 4 open-weight judges, 27 languages,
and 4 tasks across 13 datasets, yielding over 1M pointwise scores on
gold-reference outputs, validated against human judgments. We find that
prompting strategies are not transferable: the choice of the judge model
dominates the choice of prompting strategy, strategy effects reverse
direction across judges, and stability degrades in low-resource
languages, where these effects are concealed by aggregate reporting.
These findings show that prompting effects in multilingual evaluation
are conditioned on the judge, task, and language. Consequently,
prompting strategies should be validated for the intended evaluation
setting, and cross-study comparisons should account for both judge and
prompt configuration.

## 1 Introduction

Multilingual evaluation of generated text relies on reference-based
metrics such as BLEU (Papineni et al., 2002) and ROUGE (Lin, 2004), and
on learned metrics such as BLEURT (Sellam et al., 2020) and COMET (Rei
et al., 2020). These metrics require human-annotated references in the
target language. Such references are unavailable for most languages and
costly to produce at scale. Recent work therefore employs LLMs as
evaluators, a paradigm known as LLM-as-a-judge (Zheng et al., 2023; Gu
et al., 2024b; Gu et al., 2024a). A prompt template specifies a role, an
evaluation rubric, and an output format, and the judge applies the
rubric criteria directly rather than scoring against a reference, which
extends evaluation to languages without annotated evaluation data.

A reliable multilingual judge should be language-invariant: its scores
should reflect the quality of the evaluated output and not the language
of that output. Fu and Liu (2025a) score parallel data in 25 languages
and report a mean Fleiss’ $`\kappa`$ of approximately $`0.3`$, with
lower agreement on low-resource languages. Neither multilingual
pretraining nor increased model scale improves this consistency. This
instability is distinct from the generation-quality gaps that
performance benchmarks report for low-resource languages (Ojo et al.,
2025; Li et al., 2025a): generation capability in a language does not
predict evaluation reliability in that language. The judge model and the
prompting strategy remain the configurable components of the evaluation
pipeline.

Chain-of-thought (Wei et al., 2022), few-shot exemplars,
self-consistency (Wang et al., 2023), and role conditioning (Salewski et
al., 2023) were developed for task solving and later carried over to
judging. Studies of judge prompt sensitivity perturb the paraphrasing or
format of one template in English rather than comparing distinct
strategies (Sclar et al., 2024; Bellibatlu et al., 2026). Fernandes et
al. (2023) evaluate in-context prompting for MT quality estimation
across several PaLM-2 sizes and language pairs, within a single model
family. MM-Eval (Son et al., 2024) scales meta-evaluation to more
languages and M-Prometheus (Pombal et al., 2025) trains multilingual
judges, and neither varies the prompting strategy. Chung and Freienthal
(2026) show that judge-induced system rankings shift across three
related European languages, attributing the instability to the judge’s
internal language representation rather than to prompt or judge choice,
in a setting limited to commercial judges and closely related languages.
Whether these findings extend to open-weight judges, lower-resource
languages, and different prompting strategies remains untested. We ask:
Does the effect of a prompting strategy on judge scores depend on the
judge model applying it and on the resource level of the language
evaluated?

We benchmark 9 prompting strategies across 4 open-weight judges (Aya
Expanse 32B, Gemma 3 27B, Qwen2.5 32B, Qwen2.5 72B), 27 languages, and 4
tasks (machine translation, summarisation, question answering,
mathematical reasoning) over 13 datasets, yielding 1.07M pointwise judge
scores. The strategies partition into two groups. Language-invariant
strategies, such as chain-of-thought and few-shot exemplars, hold the
prompt language constant. Language-varying strategies, such as
target-language and cross-lingual prompting, vary the prompt language
relative to the evaluated content. The 27 languages span four resource
tiers, so resource level varies alongside judge and strategy. Following
Fu and Liu (2025a), we score gold-reference outputs pointwise. Holding
generation quality fixed, attribute score variance to judge
configuration rather than to the evaluated content. A task-specific
rubric with a holistic 1–5 rating scores each judge, strategy, and
language combination (Section 3).

Judge identity accounts for $`\eta^{2}=0.087`$ of score variance,
against $`\eta^{2}=0.012`$ for prompting strategy. The judge $`\times`$ 
strategy interaction reaches $`\eta^{2}=0.056`$ and exceeds the strategy
main effect, indicating that strategy effectiveness is conditional on
the judge rather than intrinsic to the prompt. Target-language prompting
raises the mean score of Gemma 3 by $`0.83`$ and lowers that of Aya
Expanse by $`0.32`$ on identical inputs, with a per-language maximum
degradation of $`2.16`$ on Yoruba. Inter-judge rank agreement over
strategies falls from $`\rho=0.46`$ on high-resource machine translation
to $`\rho=0.04`$ on the low-resource tier, and is negative on
summarisation.

Our contributions are as follows.

1.  C1.
    We benchmark prompting-strategy transfer for LLM-as-a-judge
    evaluation across 9 strategies, 4 judges, 27 languages, 4 tasks, and
    13 datasets, and release 1.07M pointwise scores, rubric-level
    dimension scores, and prompt templates.
2.  C2.
    We decompose score variance across judge, strategy, language, and
    resource tier, showing that judge identity explains more variance
    than prompting strategy and that the judge $`\times`$  strategy
    interaction exceeds the strategy main effect.
3.  C3.
    We show that target-language prompting produces effects of opposing
    direction across judges on fixed content and that judge-averaged
    reporting conceals these differences.
4.  C4.
    We evaluate agreement with human judgements on SSA-MTE, SummEval,
    and WMT MQM, where Role Prompting attains the highest aggregate
    correlation ($`\rho=0.483`$).

## 2 Related Work

#### LLM-as-a-judge.

Zheng et al. (2023) established that capable LLM judges reach agreement
with human annotators comparable to inter-annotator agreement on
open-ended generation, motivating LLM-as-a-judge as a substitute for
reference-based metrics (Papineni et al., 2002; Lin, 2004; Sellam et
al., 2020; Rei et al., 2020). Subsequent work has shifted from
validating the paradigm to characterising its failure modes: task
complexity, reference quality, and prompt design all affect judge
reliability (Gu et al., 2024b), and judge decisions are sensitive to the
order in which candidates are presented even when their quality is
unchanged (Shi et al., 2024). In multilingual settings, Fu and Liu
(2025a) evaluate five judges across 25 languages and report a mean
Fleiss’ $`\kappa`$ of approximately $`0.3`$, with agreement
deteriorating further for low-resource languages regardless of whether
the judge was trained for multilingual use; their prompt-related
analysis, however, is confined to a single judge (GPT-4o) and does not
compare prompting strategies across architectures. We extend this line
by treating judge architecture and prompting strategy as jointly varying
factors rather than fixing one to study the other.

#### Prompt sensitivity and prompting strategies.

Few-shot exemplars (Brown et al., 2020), chain-of-thought reasoning (Wei
et al., 2022), and self-consistency decoding (Wang et al., 2023) were
developed for task-solving and later adapted to judging:
rubric-conditioned scoring (Kim et al., 2024), persona conditioning
(Salewski et al., 2023), and translate-then-evaluate pipelines
(Fernandes et al., 2023) are now common judge configurations, alongside
calibration-example prompting for translation quality estimation (Kocmi
and Federmann, 2023). Bellibatlu et al. (2026) introduce the Judge
Sensitivity Score to quantify how often a judge changes its decision
under semantically equivalent rephrasing, evaluating 13 judges across
four tasks and finding that model scale does not predict stability;
their study, like the prompting literature it builds on, is
English-only, leaving open whether prompt sensitivity itself is
language-dependent. Sclar et al. (2024) report a related finding for
task-solving LLMs, that formatting perturbations of a fixed prompt shift
performance substantially, again in English. We adapt the comparative
logic of these studies, holding content fixed and varying the prompt to
a multilingual, multi-judge setting.

#### Multilingual and low-resource NLP.

Benchmark infrastructure for low-resource languages has expanded through
Masakhane (Nekoto et al., 2020), FLORES-200 (NLLB Team et al., 2022),
and MAFAND-MT (Adelani et al., 2022), yet models continue to
underperform on typologically diverse and low-resource languages
independent of training-data volume (Teklehaymanot et al., 2026), and
cross-lingual transfer remains contingent on linguistic similarity (Qiu
et al., 2024). Within LLM-as-a-judge specifically, Chung and Freienthal
(2026) generate synthetic dialogues under matched conditions across
three Finno-Ugric languages and find that surface-level dimensions
(grammar, fluency) are stable across languages while higher-level
judgments (coherence) are not, indicating that some cross-lingual
instability originates in the evaluation process itself rather than in
the content evaluated. Chang et al. (2025) show that judge–human
alignment declines as resource level falls, consistent with Fu and Liu’s
findings. MM-Eval (Son et al., 2024) and M-Prometheus (Pombal et al., 2025) extend meta-evaluation and judge training to more languages,
respectively, but both fix the prompting protocol; neither varies it as
a factor. No existing study crosses prompting strategy with judge
architecture and language resource level simultaneously, which is the
gap we address.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Experimental Design

We examine the effects of prompting strategy and judge model on
multilingual pointwise evaluation. The benchmark comprises four
open-weight judges, nine prompting strategies, 27 languages, four tasks,
and 13 datasets. Each observation is a score assigned to a candidate
output by one judge under one prompting strategy.

All applicable judge–strategy configurations receive the same source
input, candidate output, and task-specific rubric. Except for SSA-MTE,
the candidate outputs are human-written references. We separately
evaluate alignment with human quality judgements using system outputs
from SSA-MTE, SummEval, and WMT MQM.

### 3.3 Prompting Strategies

We evaluate nine prompting strategies spanning calibration examples,
explicit reasoning, evaluator roles, and prompt language. All strategies
use the same task-specific rubric and five-point scoring scale. Table 2

#### Reasoning and calibration strategies.

Zero-shot provides the evaluation instruction, rubric, score
definitions, and instance without demonstrations. Few-shot anchored adds
high- and low-quality calibration examples (Fernandes et al., 2023;
Kocmi and Federmann, 2023). Prometheus-style prompting elicits
criterion-level assessments before the holistic score (Kim et al.,
2024). Chain-of-Thought requests an explicit stepwise assessment before
scoring (Wei et al., 2022). Role prompting assigns a task- and
language-specific evaluator role (Salewski et al., 2023).
Self-consistency samples three Chain-of-Thought evaluations and
aggregates their holistic scores (Wang et al., 2023).

#### Language-handling strategies.

Cross-Lingual prompting uses English instructions to evaluate
target-language candidates (Ahuja et al., 2023). Monolingual prompting
translates the instructions, rubric, and score definitions into the
candidate language. Trans-Eval first translates the candidate into
English and then evaluates the translated output (Fernandes et al.,
2023). Appendix C provides the complete prompt templates.

### 3.4 Evaluation Protocol

Each judge assigns task-specific criterion scores and a holistic score
from 1 to 5, with higher scores indicating better quality. We use the
holistic score for cross-task analyses and the criterion scores for
task-specific analyses. Table 3 summarises the criteria.

| Task | Criteria                                                        | Basis                                                              |
| ---- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| MT   | Adequacy, fluency, terminology                                  | MQM (Freitag et al., 2021)                                         |
| Sum. | Comprehensibility, attribution, main-idea coverage, conciseness | SEAHORSE  (Clark et al., 2023); SummEval (Fabbri et al., 2021)     |
| QA   | Relevance, completeness, passage grounding                      | MM-Eval (Son et al., 2024); Prometheus (Kim et al., 2024)          |
| Math | Answer correctness, reasoning validity, solution completeness   | Process Reward (Lightman et al., 2023); GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) |

Table 3: Task-specific evaluation criteria.

For XQuAD, PersianQA, CatalanQA, and Belebele, passage grounding
measures whether the answer is supported by the supplied passage. AfriQA
provides no passage, so its rubric contains only relevance and
completeness. For mathematical reasoning, judges assess the final
answer, validity of the derivation, and completeness of the solution.

We evaluate human-score alignment on SSA-MTE (Li et al., 2025b),
SummEval (Fabbri et al., 2021), and WMT MQM (Freitag et al., 2021).
SSA-MTE contains 12,565 multilingual system translations, SummEval
contains 1,600 English summaries, and the WMT MQM subset contains 500
English translations.

### 3.5 Judge Models and Implementation Details

We use four instruction-tuned open-weight judges: Aya Expanse 32B (Dang
et al., 2024), Gemma 3 27B (Gemma Team, 2025), Qwen 2.5 32B, and Qwen
2.5 72B-AWQ (Qwen Team, 2024).

We run batched inference with vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023) on NVIDIA A100
and H100 GPUs. All strategies except self-consistency use temperature 0
and top-$`p=1.0`$. Self-Consistency uses temperature 0.7 and three
independently sampled responses. We select the modal holistic score.
When all three scores differ, we use their arithmetic mean. We use seed
42 throughout.

Each response contains criterion-level scores, a holistic score, and a
short justification. We extract numeric scores from the specified
response schema. Outputs without a valid holistic score in the interval
$`[1,5]`$ are recorded as parsing failures and excluded from score
aggregation. The evaluation produced 1,068,528 valid scores.

### 3.6 Statistical Analysis

We compute mean holistic scores within judge–strategy–task–language
cells. The range of language-level means quantifies absolute
cross-language variation. We quantify relative cross-language dispersion
using the coefficient of variation,

```math
\mathrm{CV}_{j,p,t}=\frac{\sigma_{j,p,t}}{\mu_{j,p,t}},
```

where $`\mu_{j,p,t}`$ and $`\sigma_{j,p,t}`$ denote the mean and
standard deviation across languages for judge $`j`$, strategy $`p`$, and
task $`t`$. We compute strategy comparisons and CV separately by task.

We measure inter-judge agreement using Spearman’s $`\rho`$ for strategy
rankings and Pearson’s $`r`$ for strategy-level mean scores. We estimate
the following two-way ANOVA separately for each task:

```math
s=\mathrm{Judge}+\mathrm{Strategy}+\mathrm{Judge}\times\mathrm{Strategy}.
```

Separate one-way ANOVAs quantify variation associated with language and
resource group. We report Type-II eta squared,

```math
\eta_{f}^{2}=\frac{\mathrm{SS}_{f}}{\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{total}}},
```

where $`\mathrm{SS}_{f}`$ is the sum of squares associated with factor
$`f`$. We assess ANOVA terms at $`\alpha=0.001`$. We report pairwise
strategy comparisons descriptively rather than significance-testing
them, because ceiling effects in QA and reference-based MT compress the
score range and would inflate rejection rates.

For each task and strategy, we compute the difference between the
English mean and the low-resource-language mean. We measure the
language-specific effect of Monolingual prompting relative to Zero-Shot
as

```math
\Delta_{\mathrm{mono}}(l,j,t)=\bar{s}_{\mathrm{Monolingual},l,j,t}-\bar{s}_{\mathrm{Zero\mbox{-}Shot},l,j,t}.
```

We exclude English because both conditions use English instructions.
Languages represented in only one task are excluded from cross-task
comparisons.

Finally, we compute instance-level Spearman correlations between
holistic judge scores and human annotations on SSA-MTE
($`n\approx 12{,}579`$ per cell), SummEval ($`n=1{,}600`$), and WMT MQM
($`n=500`$). We estimate correlations separately for each judge and
applicable prompting strategy.

## 4 Results

We decompose score variance across judge, strategy, and their
interaction (§4.1), measure how strategy effects vary across judges
(§4.2) and languages (§4.3), and report agreement with human annotations
(§4.4).

Scoring gold-reference outputs yields mean scores above $`4.5`$ on QA
and reference-based MT, compressing the range over which strategies can
differ. A supplementary evaluation on AfroBench system outputs (Ojo et
al., 2025) yields wider dispersion than the gold-reference condition for
the same judges (Appendix J). Gemma 27B assigns lower Zero-Shot scores
than the other judges ($`2.29`$ QA, $`3.15`$ Summarization, $`2.90`$
Math), and its scores under the other strategies fall within the range
of the other judges (Appendix E).

### 4.1 Variance Decomposition

Table 4 reports $`\eta^{2}_{f}`$ from a two-way ANOVA over judge and
strategy, with language and resource estimated by separate one-way
ANOVAs. All terms are significant at $`p<0.001`$.

Judge identity accounts for $`\eta^{2}=0.087`$ of score variance and
prompting strategy for $`\eta^{2}=0.012`$, a ratio of approximately
seven to one. The judge $`\times`$ strategy interaction is
$`\eta^{2}=0.056`$ and exceeds the strategy main effect.

#### Per task.

Judge identity accounts for $`\eta^{2}=0.438`$ on Summarization. On QA
the interaction is $`\eta^{2}=0.247`$ against $`0.251`$ for judge
identity, and on Math $`0.297`$ against $`0.222`$. On MT, language
accounts for $`\eta^{2}=0.325`$ and judge identity for
$`\eta^{2}=0.006`$. Excluding SSA-MTE, the only MT dataset containing
system outputs with cross-lingual quality variation, gives a language
term of $`0.030`$, a judge term of $`0.048`$, and an interaction of
$`0.114`$ (Appendix H).

We also observe that Gemma 27B scores substantially lower under
Zero-Shot than the other judges but recovers under other strategies,
contributing to its distinctive per-task profile (Appendix E).

### 4.3 Strategy Effects Across Languages

Figure 1 reports mean judge score per strategy and resource tier for MT
and Summarization. CoT has the highest mean on Summarization, QA, and
Math (Appendix E). Monolingual has the lowest mean on MT and
Summarization, and Prometheus the lowest on QA and Math.

Cross-lingual ranges are wider at lower resource levels (Table 6). On MT
the mean range is $`0.13`$ on the high-resource tier and $`1.60`$ on the
low-resource tier. At the rubric level the difference is largest on
Terminology, which is $`3.93`$–$`4.59`$ on low-resource languages
against $`4.97`$–$`5.00`$ on high-resource languages, while Adequacy and
Fluency differ by at most $`0.5`$ points across tiers (Appendix G).

Mean $`\mathrm{CV}`$ ranges from $`0.036`$ for CoT to $`0.165`$ for
Prometheus, with Monolingual second highest at $`0.162`$ (Appendix D).
Under CoT and Prometheus, the Summarization Main Ideas dimension is
$`2.99`$–$`3.07`$ against $`3.48`$–$`3.80`$ under Zero-Shot, across all
four resource tiers.

Figure 2 reports $`\Delta_{\mathrm{mono}}`$ per language and judge.
Monolingual prompting raises Gemma’s mean score by $`0.83`$ and lowers
Aya’s by $`0.32`$; the two Qwen judges shift by $`-0.20`$ and $`-0.10`$.
Aya’s largest per-language decreases are on Yor ($`-2.16`$), Hau
($`-1.56`$), and Swa ($`-1.20`$). At the rubric level the decrease is
largest on Fluency, which is $`3.35`$–$`3.67`$ across all resource
tiers, while Adequacy differs by at most $`0.5`$ points (Appendix G).

Table 7 reports the English minus low-resource difference per strategy.
The overall difference ranges from $`-0.130`$ for cross-Lingual to
$`+0.206`$ for CoT, the latter with a Math component of $`+0.542`$.
Monolingual is $`+0.009`$ overall. Excluding SSA-MTE, most MT
differences are within $`0.11`$ of zero.

## 5 Discussion

We find that the effect of a prompting strategy on evaluation scores
depends on both the Judge and the target language. Judge dependence is
quantified by the Judge $`\times`$ strategy interactions, exceeding the
strategy’s effect when measured independently. This, however, comes with
an exception on Summarization, where we observe that different judges
exhibit different preferences w.r.t. the strategies.

#### Language effect.

Language dependence is quantified by $`\Delta_{\mathrm{mono}}`$, which
is positive for one judge and negative for another on identical content.
Note that we observe an-order-of-magnitude wider range of
$`\Delta_{\mathrm{mono}}`$ for low-resource languages in the
cross-lingual setting. Rescaling does not affect the nature of the
observed dependences, which is in line with Bellibatlu et al. (2026) and
extends it from within-judge instability under paraphrase to
between-judge non-transfer of complete strategies. The fact that the LLM
used as the Judge accounts for the larger share of variance is
consistent with multilingual evaluation behaviour being determined by
properties acquired in pre-training and instruction tuning (Dang et al.,
2024; Gemma Team, 2025), rather than by prompt design, though our design
does not isolate these sources from other inter-model differences.

#### Strategy effect.

The choice of Strategy has lower effects than the Judge LLM.
Nevertheless, it is still not negligible. We observe the strongest
Strategy effect for QA and Summarization tasks ($`\eta^{2}=0.059`$ &
$`0.057`$, respectively) with open-ended semantics where prompt framing
shapes which quality aspects the judge attends to. Chain-of-Thought
shows the lowest cross-lingual variability (mean CV $`=0.036`$),
consistent with prior work, suggesting structured reasoning reduces
variance by making intermediate steps explicit Wei et al. (2022); Wang
et al. (2023), though it does not consistently achieve the highest human
alignment, indicating consistency does not guarantee quality.

#### Target-language prompting

illustrates an architecture-dependent effect: it benefits Gemma
($`\Delta=+0.83`$) but harms Aya ($`\Delta=-0.32`$), with severe
degradation for low-resource African languages (e.g. Yor: $`-2.16`$,
Hau: $`-1.56`$). Since this holds on identical languages, it likely
reflects judge architecture rather than data characteristics, possibly
because Aya, trained explicitly for multilingual instruction
following Dang et al. (2024), has developed multilingual processing that
target-language prompts disrupt rather than support, while Gemma relies
more on prompt language during evaluation, supported by Monolingual
achieving Gemma’s highest human alignment ($`\rho=0.455`$) but Aya’s
lowest ($`\rho=0.219`$).

#### Resource level

is associated with increased instability throughout: cross-lingual
performance ranges widen as language resources decrease, and inter-model
rank agreement drops from moderate in high-resource MT ($`\rho=0.46`$)
to near zero in low-resource MT ($`\rho=0.04`$), consistent with the
findings of Fu and Liu (2025b) and Chung and Freienthal (2026),
extending their findings by showing instability affects strategy
_rankings_, not only absolute scores.

#### Language bias.

Strategy choice also carries fairness implications, not obvious from
mean scores: Cross-lingual is the most balanced strategy overall
($`\Delta=-0.130`$), while CoT shows the largest English bias in Maths
($`\Delta=+0.542`$), and aggregate reporting can mask this variability.
Note that Monolingual’s near-zero overall gap ($`\Delta=+0.009`$) hides
large positive and negative per-language biases.

#### Upshot.

Judge selection appears more important than prompt selection overall;
however, for some tasks the judge $`\times`$ strategy interaction
contributes as much as the Judge LLM alone, meaning a prompt optimised
for one judge does not necessarily transfer to another, and strategies
should be validated on the target judge rather than adopted from a
different architecture. Reliability should also be assessed at the
language level, since aggregate metrics can hide substantial
cross-language variation. Among evaluated strategies, Role Prompting
offers the best overall trade-off, achieving the highest human alignment
($`\rho=0.483`$) while remaining relatively stable across languages.
Improving multilingual LLM-as-a-Judge reliability therefore requires the
combined choice of judge model, prompting strategy, and target
languages, not prompt design alone.

## 6 Conclusion

We evaluated nine prompting strategies across four open-weight judges,
27 languages, and four generation tasks. Prompting effects varied across
judges: the judge–strategy interaction exceeded the strategy main
effect, judges showed limited agreement on strategy rankings, and
target-language prompting produced effects of opposing direction across
models. The strategy with the lowest cross-language dispersion also
differed from that with the highest aggregate correlation with human
judgements, indicating that score stability does not imply evaluation
validity. Prompt design should therefore be treated as part of the judge
configuration and validated for the intended judge, task, and language.
Future work should evaluate additional judge families and
system-generated outputs spanning a wider quality range, quantify the
effects of prompting on system rankings, and develop prompt-selection
methods that improve agreement with human judgements across languages.

## Limitations

#### Evaluation design.

Gold-reference evaluation isolates judge behaviour from output variation
but introduces ceiling effects in QA and reference-based MT, limiting
strategy discrimination for those tasks (Section 4.1); we confirm this
directly using AfroBench real system outputs but only for a subset of
judges, tasks, and strategies. A related consequence is that Fleiss’
$`\kappa`$ collapses toward zero on the skewed gold-reference score
distribution despite substantial raw agreement, an artefact of
chance-correction under skew rather than genuine disagreement
(Appendix J).

#### Language coverage and verification.

Target-language prompts were native-speaker-verified for only a subset
of languages; part of the observed Monolingual effect may therefore
reflect prompt quality rather than judge behaviour alone. Our language
distribution is weighted toward low-resource languages, so cross-tier
comparisons should be interpreted with caution, and our two-way ANOVA
does not capture language interactions, which we analyse separately via
one-way ANOVAs.

#### Validation and model coverage.

SummEval and WMT MQM are English-only, so SRQ4 is fully tested for MT
(via SSA-MTE) but not for non-English summarisation or reasoning. We
evaluate four open-source judges; our findings on judge-specificity may
not generalise to closed-source models, which we test only in a limited,
exploratory capacity (Appendix J).

## Ethical considerations.

Our results should not be read as grounds for excluding low-resource
languages from LLM-as-a-judge pipelines; rather, they show that judge
and strategy choice must be validated per language before deployment,
particularly where evaluation outcomes affect downstream model selection
or content moderation for these communities. All datasets used are
publicly released for research use, and we release our own scores under
the same principle to support independent verification. Our experiments
consumed substantial GPU compute (Section 3.5); we mitigate this by
releasing all 1.07M scores so that others can reuse them without
re-running the benchmark.

## Appendix B Task-Specific Rubric Definitions

All rubrics are included in the system prompt for every strategy and
judge model. Each dimension is scored independently on a 1–5 scale,
followed by a single holistic overall score used for cross-task
comparison.

### Machine Translation

MQM framework Freitag et al. (2021)

Dimension 1: Adequacy (meaning preservation)

- 5
  All meaning from the source preserved; no omissions or additions.
- 4
  Core meaning correct; minor omissions not affecting comprehension.
- 3
  General idea captured but notable information missing or inaccurate.
- 2
  Significant meaning distortion; key information missing or
  mistranslated.
- 1
  Meaning completely lost; translation unrelated to source.

Dimension 2: Fluency (naturalness in target language)

- 5
  Native-like fluency; no grammatical errors; natural tone and register.
- 4
  Mostly natural with minor awkwardness.
- 3
  Understandable but noticeably non-native phrasing.
- 2
  Difficult to read; frequent grammatical errors.
- 1
  Incomprehensible or gibberish.

Dimension 3: Terminology

- 5
  All domain-specific terms translated correctly and consistently.
- 4
  Most terms correct; minor inconsistencies.
- 3
  Some key terms wrong but meaning partially recoverable.
- 2
  Multiple terminology errors misleading the reader.
- 1
  Key terminology completely wrong or absent.

### Summarization

SEAHORSE framework Clark et al. (2023); Fabbri et al. (2021)

Dimension 1: Comprehensibility

- 5
  Perfectly clear and understandable on its own.
- 4
  Mostly clear; minor ambiguity.
- 3
  Understandable with effort; some unclear passages.
- 2
  Difficult to understand; significant clarity issues.
- 1
  Incoherent or incomprehensible.

Dimension 2: Attribution (factual grounding in source)

- 5
  Every claim traceable to the source article.
- 4
  Almost all claims supported; one minor unsupported detail.
- 3
  Most claims supported but some fabrications.
- 2
  Significant fabricated content.
- 1
  Entirely fabricated or contradicts the source.

Dimension 3: Main Ideas (coverage)

- 5
  All key points of the source captured.
- 4
  Most key points covered; one minor omission.
- 3
  Some key points covered but important information missing.
- 2
  Only peripheral details; main points missing.
- 1
  Misses all important content.

Dimension 4: Conciseness

- 5
  Optimal length; no redundancy, no critical omissions.
- 4
  Slightly verbose or brief but acceptable.
- 3
  Noticeably too long or too short.
- 2
  Very verbose with repetition, or critically short.
- 1
  Excessively long or short to be unusable.

### QA with Passage

MM-Eval framework Son et al. (2024); Kim et al. (2024). Applied to
XQuAD, PersianQA, CatalanQA, Belebele.

Dimension 1: Relevance

- 5
  Answer directly and precisely addresses the question asked.
- 4
  Mostly relevant; minor tangential content.
- 3
  Partially relevant; addresses some but not the core of the question.
- 2
  Marginally relevant; mostly off-topic.
- 1
  Completely unrelated to the question.

Dimension 2: Completeness

- 5
  Fully addresses all aspects of the question.
- 4
  Addresses the main point; minor aspects not covered.
- 3
  Partially addresses the question; key aspects missing.
- 2
  Only tangentially addresses the question.
- 1
  Does not address the question at all.

Dimension 3: Passage Grounding

- 5
  Answer is directly extractable from or fully supported by the passage.
- 4
  Answer is mostly grounded; uses minor inference beyond the passage.
- 3
  Answer is partially grounded; mixes passage content with external
  knowledge.
- 2
  Answer is largely not supported by the passage.
- 1
  Answer contradicts the passage or ignores it entirely.

### QA without Passage

MM-Eval framework Son et al. (2024). Applied to AfriQA (open-retrieval;
no passage provided). Passage Grounding is excluded by design.

Uses Relevance and Completeness only, with identical anchors to the QA
with Passage rubric above.

### Mathematical Reasoning

Process-reward framework Lightman et al. (2023); Cobbe et al. (2021).
Evaluated as text generation: reasoning quality, not only the final
answer.

Dimension 1: Answer Correctness

- 5
  Final numerical answer is exactly correct.
- 4
  Very close; minor rounding or unit issue.
- 3
  Wrong but the approach was reasonable.
- 2
  Significantly wrong.
- 1
  No answer or completely wrong.

Dimension 2: Reasoning Validity

- 5
  Every step logically sound and mathematically correct.
- 4
  Mostly sound; one minor logical gap.
- 3
  Notable gaps but shows some understanding.
- 2
  Largely flawed or incoherent reasoning.
- 1
  No reasoning or completely nonsensical.

Dimension 3: Solution Completeness

- 5
  All necessary steps shown clearly.
- 4
  Most steps shown; one minor step implicit.
- 3
  Some steps shown but important work missing.
- 2
  Only the final answer with minimal work.
- 1
  Just a number with no work shown.

of this study. GitHub Copilot and Claude Code were used as coding
assistants during the development of the evaluation pipeline and for
debugging. Additionally, LLMs (Grammarly and Claude) were used to
support the writing process, specifically for correcting spelling and
grammatical inconsistencies, improving clarity, and reducing selected
sections to comply with space constraints. Furthermore, all aspects of
the research design, methodology, analysis, and conclusions presented in
this study are the independent work of the author.
