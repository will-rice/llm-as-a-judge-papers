###### Abstract

When an LLM judge scores an output, its score distribution retains
uncertainty and disagreement information that is lost after scalar
compression. We introduce JudgeMoE, a lightweight aggregator that
assigns example-specific weights to cached judge score distributions and
fuses them before computing a final score. A protocol study shows that
score-range choice is unstable across judge–dataset settings and that
soft scoring usually outperforms hard decoding. On the original 10-cell
benchmark, JudgeMoE improves mean Spearman over uniform log pooling by
$`+0.079`$. Applying the same configuration to six additional cells
yields a $`+0.0393`$ mean gain over the strongest local single judge
across 16 cells, with positive differences in 12/16 cells and a
one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank $`p=0.0091`$. Validation-based analyses
further show that the preferred aggregation method depends on the task
and judge pool.

## 1 Introduction

Figure 1: Three levels of information retention for the same judge score
distributions. Hard-score aggregation uses only decoded labels, and
soft-score aggregation uses expected scores. JudgeMoE pools the full
score distributions before scoring, retaining confidence, entropy,
near-tie mass, and bucket-level agreement during aggregation.

When an LLM judge assigns a score, the decoded label is only a
compressed view of a richer score distribution. Two judges may both
decode to 4 while expressing different uncertainty: one may concentrate
mass on 4, while another spreads mass across 3, 4, and 5. Such
differences matter for aggregation because confidence, entropy, and
near-ties affect whether judges provide consistent or uncertain
evidence. We use _scoring protocol_ to mean the score range, extraction
rule, and prompt framing used to elicit this distribution.

LLMs are now widely used as automatic evaluators for summarization,
dialogue, reasoning, and translation (Liu et al., 2023; Zheng et al.,
2023). Their agreement with humans can change under protocol choices
such as the score range and the rule used to extract a score (Wang et
al., 2024a; Yamauchi et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026). Our protocol study
shows that these choices change the measurement produced by the judge:
across 30 dataset and model combinations where both soft and hard scores
give usable rank correlations, each of the five tested score ranges is
strongest in at least one setting, and soft-score extraction is stronger
than hard decoding in most valid runs.

These findings motivate aggregation before score compression. Scalar
averaging and scalar-gated aggregation combine decoded or expected
scores after each judge distribution has already been reduced to one
number. JudgeMoE instead pools the full judge score distributions and
computes the final scalar score after pooling. Each judge distribution
is treated as protocol-specific evidence over the benchmark’s possible
labels.

We use JudgeMoE as a lightweight testbed for this representation shift.
With a frozen pool of small local judges and cached score distributions,
the same outputs support several validation-guided deployment choices. A
DeepSets gate assigns example-specific judge weights, and a tempered
log-opinion pool fuses score buckets before final scoring. Our
experiments focus on reusable pools of small local judges, while larger
evaluators are included as standalone options in the validation-based
selection procedure. We call one dataset paired with one score-scale
condition a _cell_.

Our contributions are as follows:

- •
  A protocol study showing that score-range choice is unstable across
  judge families and datasets, and that soft scoring is typically
  stronger than hard decoding.
- •
  We introduce JudgeMoE, a lightweight conditional aggregator that
  builds on the protocol study by pooling cached judge score
  distributions before converting them to scalar scores.
- •
  A validation-guided deployment analysis showing when fixed pooling,
  full JudgeMoE, sparse repair, or a standalone expert is supported by
  validation evidence.

## 2 Related Work

#### LLM-as-a-Judge.

Recent work has established LLMs as scalable evaluators for open-ended
generation (Liu et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2023). Later studies show
that positional bias and evaluator self-preference can affect agreement
with humans (Wang et al., 2024a; Liu et al., 2024). Prompt design,
safety artifacts, decoding choices, and grading scales can also change
evaluation outcomes (Hong et al., 2026; Chen and Goldfarb-Tarrant, 2025;
Yamauchi et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026). Other work improves individual
judges through specialized evaluator models (Kim et al., 2024; Huang et
al., 2025a), self-rationalization (Trivedi et al., 2024), checklist
decomposition (Lee et al., 2025), and adaptive debiasing (Yang et al.,
2026). Much of this line studies how to make a single judge more
reliable; our work asks what can still be recovered by aggregating
across multiple judges that produce different scores for the same
example under a shared scoring protocol.

#### Evaluator ensembles and aggregation.

Multi-model evaluation can use interaction, aggregation, or
probability-level comparison across evaluators. Debate-style approaches
let agents exchange rationales before producing a final answer (Chan et
al., 2024; Du et al., 2024); jury-style approaches aggregate judgments
from several models without explicit multi-turn interaction (Verga et
al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025b); and ContrastScore uses disagreement in
model probabilities for generation evaluation (Wang et al., 2025b). Wang
et al. (2025a) study inference from the judgment distribution of a
single LLM evaluator and show that distributional information can
improve over greedy decoded judgments. JudgeMoE studies conditional
aggregation across multiple judges, using per-example score
distributions under explicit scoring protocols and example-specific
judge weights.

There is an established statistical tradition of aggregating noisy
judgments under latent reliability models, from Dawid–Skene (Dawid and
Skene, 1979) to recent LLM-specific probabilistic aggregation models
(Zhao et al., 2025a). These models infer consensus labels and judge
reliability from noisy annotations. They assume a shared discrete label
space and a single latent consensus label, whereas we treat score
distributions as protocol-dependent measurements and compare several
deployment modes over cached outputs. Relatedly, QAEval uses the phrase
“mixture of evaluators” for heterogeneous QA evaluators that mix
rule-based and learned components (Yue et al., 2025); our study focuses
on a homogeneous LLM pool and treats heterogeneous experts as optional
task-aligned additions.

#### Adjacent settings.

Pairwise preference learning provides relative supervision that can
complement direct scores (Bradley and Terry, 1952; Jeong et al., 2025).
RLHF work also uses reward-model ensembles or judge-based constraints
inside policy optimization (Zhang et al., 2024; Zhai et al., 2024; Xu et
al., 2025). Our setting is complementary: we aggregate frozen LLM judges
directly at evaluation time, so one cached set of score distributions
can support several validated aggregators without rerunning judge
inference. This reuse objective distinguishes our setting from
post-training evaluator ensembles.

## 3 Method

#### Setup and terminology.

JudgeMoE takes each judge’s probability distribution over possible
scores as the input object. We use _bucket_ as shorthand for one allowed
score label, and a _bucket vector_ is the normalized probability vector
over those labels. A _protocol_ is the score range, prompt, and
score-extraction rule that produced that vector. A _cell_ is one dataset
paired with one score scale. A _gate_ assigns an example-specific weight
to each judge. A _log pool_ fuses the weighted score distributions by
taking a weighted geometric mean over buckets. _Sparse repair_ means
removing harmful judges on validation data and retraining the same
aggregator on the selected subset. In short, JudgeMoE reads each judge’s
full distribution, decides how much to trust each judge for the current
example, pools the distributions, and then computes one expected score.

### 3.1 Judge Representations

Each judge produces a normalized probability vector over the allowed
score labels. Hard scores decode the argmax, soft scores take the
expectation, and JudgeMoE uses the distribution itself. We optionally
augment this bucket vector with scalar summaries, including hard and
soft scores, confidence, entropy, and maximum bucket probability. Scores
and targets are normalized to $`[0,1]`$ for cross-scale comparison.

This is the main distinction from score-level aggregation. Scalar
baselines aggregate decoded scores or expected scores, so the aggregator
only sees compressed judge outputs. JudgeMoE keeps the bucket vector as
the input object and lets the gate decide whether distributional
differences matter for the current example. Feature ablations in the
appendix show that much of the useful signal lies in the score
distribution itself, with scalar uncertainty features adding a smaller
gain over probabilities alone.

### 3.2 Aggregation Families

For deployment, we use validation labels to compare a small family of
gating and pooling variants. The sweep includes feed-forward and
contextual gates inspired by mixture-of-experts gating (Jacobs et al.,
1991; Jordan and Jacobs, 1994), Deep Sets-style permutation-invariant
gating (Zaheer et al., 2017), and probability fusion with log-opinion
pooling (Heskes, 1997). We use this reproducible family to test whether
aggregating judge score distributions before score compression improves
evaluation quality.

The full validation comparison is shown in Appendix Table 13. The
selected mainline uses a DeepSets gate with tempered log-opinion pooling
($`T=0.7`$). For each example $`x`$, the gate assigns an
example-specific weight to each judge from its distributional features.
The following equations define the selected DeepSets gate and tempered
log-opinion pool.

Let $`\phi^{(j)}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\phi}}`$ be the feature vector of
judge $`j`$, and let the shared encoder
$`\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\phi}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{h}}`$ map each
judge feature vector into a common hidden space,
$`h^{(j)}(x)=\psi(\phi^{(j)}(x))`$. With a shared scoring vector
$`a\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{h}}`$, the judge weights are

```math
\alpha^{(j)}(x)=\mathrm{softmax}_{j}\!\left(a^{\top}h^{(j)}(x)\right). \tag{1}
```

We use log-opinion pooling because multiplicative fusion rewards
agreement across judges. The trade-off is increased sensitivity to any
judge that assigns near-zero mass to the correct bucket: a single
near-zero probability can push a bucket down unless that judge receives
very small weight. The weights parameterize the tempered log-opinion
pool:

```math
\displaystyle q_{k}(x) \displaystyle\propto\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha^{(j)}(x)\cdot\log\!\left(p^{(j)}_{k}(x)+\varepsilon\right)\Bigr), \tag{2}
```

where $`q_{k}(x)`$ is the fused probability of score $`s_{k}`$, $`T`$ is
the pooling temperature, and $`\varepsilon`$ is a small numerical
constant added inside each judge log-probability for numerical
stability. Lower temperatures sharpen the fused distribution toward
buckets supported by the weighted pool, while higher temperatures
flatten it. The final prediction is the expected score under the pooled
distribution,

```math
\hat{y}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}s_{k}q_{k}(x). \tag{3}
```

Compared with linear pooling, this operator sharpens consensus and is
often stronger. Its brittleness motivates the sparse-repair analysis in
Section 6. In practice, the validation sweep over
$`T\in\{0.7,1.0,1.3\}`$ selected $`T=0.7`$. We use this configuration
because it gives the best validation average while maintaining a small
parameter count and judge-order invariance.

### 3.3 Training Objectives

#### Direct and auxiliary objectives.

The primary evaluator is selected on direct-score quality; pairwise
supervision is used only as an auxiliary analysis. We train primarily on
direct-score labels with Huber loss:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{direct}}=\mathrm{Huber}\bigl(\hat{y}(x),y\bigr). \tag{4}
```

In auxiliary experiments, we add a margin-ranking loss on pairwise
preference data. For a pair $`(x_{a},x_{b})`$ with human preference
label $`t\in\{-1,+1\}`$ and scalar aggregator scores $`s_{a},s_{b}`$,
this loss is

```math
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{pair}}=\max\!\left(0,m-t\,(s_{a}-s_{b})\right), \tag{5}
```

where $`m`$ is a margin hyperparameter. The joint objective is

```math
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{direct}}+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\text{pair}}. \tag{6}
```

## 4 Experimental Setup

Our experiments consist of two parts. First, the protocol study examines
how scoring protocols change judge output distributions. Second, the
aggregation benchmark fixes the candidate judges and compares
aggregation methods on direct-score cells. The original aggregation
benchmark contains 10 dataset–scale cells. After the judge pool,
architecture, feature construction, and training procedure had been
fixed, we evaluated the same configuration on six additional cells from
HelpSteer2 validation, FLASK, and WildBench. We report the original 10
cells and the six additional cells separately, and use all 16 cells for
the expanded comparison.

### 4.1 Tasks and Datasets

Separate follow-ups then test heterogeneous experts, pairwise auxiliary
training, and sparse repair. We evaluate JudgeMoE on direct-score and
pairwise benchmarks spanning summarization (SummEval (Fabbri et al.,
2021)), dialogue/helpfulness (TopicalChat-USR (Gopalakrishnan et al.,
2019; Mehri and Eskenazi, 2020) and HelpSteer2 (Wang et al., 2024b)),
reasoning/explanation quality (ROSCOE-eSNLI (Camburu et al., 2018;
Golovneva et al., 2023)), translation quality estimation (WMT
MQM (Freitag et al., 2021; Lommel et al., 2013)), and pairwise
preference evaluation (MT-Bench-human (Zheng et al., 2023) and
Summarize-from-Feedback validation sets (Stiennon et al., 2020)). These
datasets include both high-resource cells where aggregation can be
estimated reliably and smaller diagnostic cells where failure modes are
visible. For HelpSteer2, Table 1 reports the main subset used in the
original 10-cell benchmark; the held-out validation extension uses a
separate source validation split summarized in the appendix.

#### Split integrity.

To prevent leakage across train, validation, and test splits, we
preserve the original split labels for each dataset and use exact
example-ID intersections only when constructing aligned subset
comparisons. Model, judge-pool, and aggregation choices are made using
validation labels only; test labels are used only for final reporting.

### 4.2 Judge Pool

Our candidate pool includes six small judges from multiple model
families: Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Qwen et al., 2024), Qwen3.5-9B (Qwen
Team, 2026), Gemma-2-9B-IT (Gemma Team et al., 2024),
Gemma-3-12B-IT (Gemma Team et al., 2025),
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024), and
Ministral-3-8B-Instruct-2512 (Liu et al., 2026). The mainline comparison
uses all six judges. The compact 3-judge configuration retains
Gemma-2-9B-IT, Gemma-3-12B-IT, and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct; this pool is
selected once from the protocol study and then held fixed for the
aggregation benchmark. A family-diverse 3-judge control enforces three
different model families across the selected judges; it is weaker and
appears in the appendix. The pool is homogeneous in evaluator type
because all members are local instruct-tuned LLM judges; model families
vary. Heterogeneous follow-ups append one task-aligned expert at a time
to stress-test whether validation keeps, rejects, or isolates a
candidate expert.

### 4.3 Score Ranges and Prompting

We evaluate score ranges \[1–3\], \[1–5\], \[1–7\], \[1–10\], and
\[1–100\] as a diagnostic study of protocol sensitivity. The protocol
study checks whether scoring choices change the measurement; the
aggregation experiments then freeze a shared protocol to isolate pooling
from repeated scale search. The main JudgeMoE protocol uses a shared
scale across judges, with 1–5 as the default and 1–10 as the primary
ablation. These scales serve as fixed deployment settings for
aggregation comparisons. We also run a held-out protocol study over 24
dataset–model cells in which the protocol is selected on validation and
evaluated once on test.

For each allowed score label, we extract or score the corresponding
label probability, normalize across the allowed labels, and use the
expectation as the soft score; hard decoding takes the
highest-probability label. Soft-vs-hard counts exclude runs where rank
correlation is undefined, such as constant prediction or target vectors;
the appendix gives the exact filtering rule.

Prompts are task-specific but structurally aligned so the comparison
stays focused on scale and aggregation. The appendix summarizes prompt
skeletons and the shared output contract; the released artifact contains
the exact prompt strings and parsing scripts.

### 4.4 Baselines

Baselines include the best single judge, uniform average or log-opinion
pooling, covariance-aware aggregation, aligned strong single-judge
follow-ups on selected cells, and JudgeMoE variants. Unless stated
otherwise, “best single” denotes the strongest single judge within the
evaluated cell; validation-selected single-judge results are reported
with the deployment-selection diagnostics. The covariance-aware baseline
is the strongest fixed pool: it uses validation error covariance to set
non-uniform judge weights, giving less influence to judges with large or
highly correlated errors, while keeping those weights fixed across
examples.

### 4.5 Implementation Details

All aggregation experiments are script-driven and use fixed split
manifests, cached judge score distributions, and reproducible checkpoint
configurations. Appendix Tables 10 and 40 summarize the hyperparameters,
seed settings, prompt templates, runner scripts, and result files needed
to reproduce the experiments.

### 4.6 Metrics

Before aggregation and evaluation, direct-score outputs are normalized
to a common $`[0,1]`$ range to make scores comparable across datasets
with different scales. For direct-score cells from SummEval,
TopicalChat-USR, HelpSteer2, ROSCOE-eSNLI, and WMT MQM, we use Spearman
correlation with human labels as the primary metric. Pairwise preference
cells are evaluated with pairwise accuracy. We report MAE and RMSE only
when absolute score error is directly relevant, such as in calibration
or error-magnitude analyses. Main-paper tables report equal-cell
averages, where a cell is one dataset paired with one score scale and is
treated as one deployment condition. Sample-weighted summaries use the
number of aligned evaluation examples in each cell as weights. For
comparisons between aggregation methods, we report the mean cell-level
difference and apply a Wilcoxon signed-rank test to the cell-level
differences. One-sided tests evaluate whether JudgeMoE improves over the
baseline, and two-sided tests evaluate whether the differences are
nonzero. Some cells share datasets and examples across score scales, so
these tests summarize variation across evaluation conditions with
dependence between related cells.

## 5 Main Results

We first show that scoring protocols change judge measurements, then
test example-dependent weighting against fixed and single-judge
baselines. The failures identify when validation should prefer fixed
pooling, sparse repair, or a standalone expert.

### 5.1 Protocol Choices Change the Measurement

The protocol study shows that score range and extraction rule change
judge behavior. Figure 2 shows two patterns: the best score range varies
across dataset–model combinations, and soft scoring improves over hard
decoding in most valid runs. These results support aggregating score
distributions rather than scalar summaries alone. Across 24
dataset–model cells, validation-selected protocols also outperform fixed
1–5 or 1–10 soft scoring on test (Appendix Table 15).

dataset–model cells. Right: soft scoring usually improves over hard
decoding across valid runs and scales.

| ----------- | -------- | -------- | ---------- | ----------------- | --------------- | --------------- |
| Uniform log | 0.304    | 0.383    | +0.079     | \[0.039, 0.119\]  | 0.005           | 0.010           |
| Cov-log     | 0.341    | 0.383    | +0.043     | \[-0.001, 0.085\] | 0.053           | 0.105           |
| Best single | 0.341    | 0.383    | +0.042     | \[-0.007, 0.088\] | 0.053           | 0.105           |

### 5.2 Conditional Aggregation

Table 2 compares the selected configuration with equal-weight
distributional pooling, fixed scalar baselines, and the best individual
judges on the original 10-cell benchmark.

#### Gain over uniform pooling.

The most direct comparison is uniform log pooling, which uses the same
six judges with equal weights. JudgeMoE gains +0.079 mean Spearman, with
Wilcoxon signed-rank tests giving $`p=0.005`$ for the one-sided
improvement test and $`p=0.010`$ for the two-sided difference test. This
supports example-specific weighting over equal-weight distributional
pooling for this judge pool.

#### Comparison with stronger baselines.

Against covariance-aware log pooling and the best single judge, JudgeMoE
has positive mean gains, but the confidence intervals cross zero
(Table 2). We therefore treat these comparisons as inconclusive and use
validation data to choose among learned weighting, fixed pooling, and
single-judge deployment.

#### Expanded evaluation.

We applied the same JudgeMoE configuration to six additional cells from
HelpSteer2 validation, FLASK, and WildBench after the judge pool,
architecture, features, and training procedure had been fixed. Across
all 16 cells, JudgeMoE improves over the strongest single judge in the
local pool by $`+0.0393`$ mean Spearman, with positive differences in
12/16 cells and a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank $`p=0.0091`$
($`p=0.0182`$ two-sided). The mean difference from uniform log pooling
is $`+0.0601`$ with 12/16 positive cells ($`p=0.0017`$ one-sided), while
the difference from covariance-aware log pooling is $`+0.0250`$ with
9/16 positive cells ($`p=0.0964`$ one-sided). The six additional cells
are reported individually in Appendix Tables 32, 33, and 47.

#### Cell-level variation.

Appendix Table 27 shows that JudgeMoE improves over the best single
judge in most cells, but fails on both TopicalChat score ranges and WMT
MQM@1–5. Section 6 uses these failures diagnostically: TopicalChat
favors fixed pooling, while WMT MQM@1–5 motivates sparse judge selection
and retraining.

#### Supporting analyses.

The appendix gives three additional analyses. First, excluding ROSCOE,
the 8-cell average remains higher for JudgeMoE than for the best single
judge (0.418 vs. 0.384). On the original 10 cells, the sample-weighted
mean differences are $`+0.0413`$ relative to the best single judge and
$`+0.0549`$ relative to covariance-aware log pooling; excluding ROSCOE
gives $`+0.0413`$ and $`+0.0548`$, respectively (Appendix Table 26).
Second, ablations show that the compact 3-judge setting remains
competitive and that full score distributions carry most of the useful
signal (Appendix Tables 13 and 18). In a separate $`2\times 2`$
comparison using the same cells, judge manifests, feature construction,
pooling temperature, 30-epoch schedule, and three random seeds, DeepSets
with log pooling achieves the highest mean Spearman of 0.4068 (Appendix
Table 14). Third, JudgeMoE obtains lower equal-cell ECE for the final
scalar predictions than single-judge and fixed-pooling baselines (0.059
vs. 0.179–0.221; Appendix Table 28). This ECE compares normalized scalar
predictions with human scores and does not measure calibration of the
underlying score-token probabilities.

### 5.3 Focused Heterogeneous Stress Tests

For each heterogeneous follow-up, we append one task-aligned expert to
the compact homogeneous pool. Validation labels choose among the
homogeneous pool, the heterogeneous pool, a repaired subset, and the
standalone expert when applicable. Table 3 reports test Spearman
correlations and validation-selected deployment choices.

| -------------------- | ------------- | ------------- | ----------- | ------------------- |
| SummEval + BERTSc.   | 0.646         | 0.670         | 0.517       | keep expert         |
| HelpSteer2 + RM      | 0.533         | 0.545         | 0.390       | repair pool         |
| WMT + trans. BERTSc. | 0.239         | 0.133         | 0.190       | keep LLM pool       |
| WMT + COMET          | 0.247         | 0.162         | 0.4326      | use standalone      |

Table 3: Focused heterogeneous follow-ups on compact deployment
settings. All entries are test Spearman correlations with human labels;
higher is better. BERTSc. denotes BERTScore and RM denotes the reward
model. The validation decision is chosen using validation labels before
test reporting. “Keep expert” selects the heterogeneous pool, “repair
pool” selects a validation-repaired subset, “keep LLM pool” rejects the
added expert, and “use standalone” keeps the external metric outside
JudgeMoE.

Positive additions. BERTScore helps on SummEval@1–10, and a Skywork
reward model helps on held-out HelpSteer2 after train-split subset
repair. These cases show that task-aligned experts can help when
validation confirms complementary signal.

Rejected additions. Translation metrics give the stronger warning. On
WMT MQM@1–10, translation-BERTScore is rejected by subset search. COMET
is the strongest standalone evaluator at 0.4326, while mixed-pool
JudgeMoE reaches 0.1620. Validation selects COMET as the standalone
evaluator. Strong standalone performance therefore does not necessarily
translate into strong performance after adding the evaluator to the
learned pool.

## 6 Validation-Guided Deployment

### 6.1 Deployment Decision Matrix

The previous section reports the evidence used for deployment decisions:
protocol choice changes judge behavior, learned weights improve over
uniform pooling, TopicalChat favors fixed pooling, and WMT MQM@1–5
improves after pruning and retraining. Table 4 summarizes how these
validation patterns guide the deployment choice for a new judge pool.

| Observed pattern          | Choice               |
| ------------------------- | -------------------- |
| Protocol variation        | Select protocol      |
| Strong fixed pool         | Fixed pooling        |
| Learned-weight gain       | Full JudgeMoE        |
| Weak judge                | Subset + retraining  |
| Strong external evaluator | Standalone evaluator |

Table 4: Validation-guided deployment choices supported by the protocol
study, main aggregation experiments, TopicalChat and WMT analyses, and
heterogeneous-expert experiments.

Shared-error check. Judges often make similar mistakes, which leaves
less room for ensembling to help. Across the 10 direct-score cells, the
mean judge-error correlation is 0.7775 for the full 6-judge pool and
0.7071 for the strong 3-judge pool (Appendix Table 20). Values around
0.7 to 0.8 indicate that much of the remaining error is shared across
judges.

Validation selector. The shared-error check motivates a validation-based
choice among full, fixed, sparse, and single-judge modes. A thresholded
selector matches the test-time best aggregation family in 6/8 held-out
direct-score cells (Appendix Table 21). A stronger validation-only
selector reaches 0.3732 average test Spearman across the eight held-out
direct-score cells, close to the 0.3789 obtained by selecting the best
family using test performance. Its selected fixed subsets use 3.38
judges on average (Appendix Table 23).

Calibration checks also favor JudgeMoE over the strongest single and
fixed-pool baselines on dataset averages.

The focused heterogeneous follow-ups support the same conclusion.
Task-aligned experts can help when they add new evidence to the
homogeneous pool and pass train-split diagnostics, while prompt-diverse
expansions of the same base models hurt, and both translation-BERTScore
and COMET degrade the WMT mixed pool. This supports validating candidate
experts before they enter the pool.

### 6.2 Cell-Level Patterns

The cell-wise breakdown explains why deployment should be selected on
validation data. Most cells favor JudgeMoE, but TopicalChat and WMT
MQM@1–5 show different failure patterns. TopicalChat is a fixed-pooling
case: covariance-aware log pooling is already strong and residual
judge-error correlation is extremely high. WMT MQM@1–5 is different
because a weak judge contaminates the pool, so removing that judge and
retraining the aggregator improves the result. These two cells motivate
the fixed-pooling and sparse-repair rows in Table 4.

Figure 3: Per-cell pattern for the main direct-score benchmark. JudgeMoE
improves over the best single judge in most direct-score cells, but the
per-cell breakdown also identifies failures on TopicalChat and WMT
MQM@1–5 (Appendix Table 27). TopicalChat is a case where
covariance-aware fixed pooling already outperforms JudgeMoE, while WMT
MQM@1–5 motivates sparse repair: retraining JudgeMoE on the
validation-selected sparse subset improves Spearman from 0.0376 to
0.1648 (Tables 11 and 53).

### 6.3 WMT Failure and Sparse Repair

The WMT MQM diagnostic isolates a concrete failure mode: coarse score
ranges compress ranking information, weak judges can hurt log pooling,
and sparse task-specific composition can outperform all-judge fusion. At
WMT 1–5, the full-6 log pool degrades sharply and the learned mainline
only partially recovers (Table 11). Leave-one-out validation identifies
Qwen2.5-7B as a harmful weak judge. Keeping only the validation-selected
top-2 subset and retraining sparse JudgeMoE raises WMT 1–5 to 0.1648,
above both the best single judge at 0.1413 and the fixed sparse pool at
0.1473 (Table 53). The full gate already assigns negligible weight to
Qwen2.5 (Appendix Table 52). Validation and test subset ranks are
tightly aligned across all 120 WMT fixed-pool candidates, which consist
of all single judges plus all non-single subsets under uniform-log and
cov-log pooling (Appendix Table 51).

The heterogeneous translation follow-ups show the same failure mode.
Appending translation-BERTScore drops the strong 3-judge JudgeMoE
baseline from 0.2389 to 0.1328, and train-split subset search ranks the
pure \[Gemma-2, Gemma-3\] subset first. COMET is more instructive: it
becomes the strongest single expert at 0.4326 and uniform log pooling
rises to 0.3926, while mixed-pool JudgeMoE still falls to 0.1620. Adding
a strong external metric can therefore degrade the learned aggregator
when its distributional behavior mismatches the LLM judges it was
trained with. The conservative WMT recommendation is either a small
clean LLM pool or the standalone metric itself. The appendix reports the
full failure-and-recovery numbers.

The practical value comes from the reusable evidence layer as well as
the full six-judge model. Once frozen judge distributions are cached,
validation can choose among single, fixed, sparse, and gated modes
without rerunning a monolithic evaluator.

### 6.4 Implications for Evaluation Practice

The results suggest a shift in how LLM judges are reported and reused.
Individual judge quality remains important, yet the scoring protocol is
part of the measurement and belongs with the judge output. A minimal
benchmark report includes at least the score range, the score-extraction
rule, and whether expected scores were computed from token probabilities
or from decoded labels.

#### Probability access.

We also compare variants using different levels of judge output
information. Hard-score-only features obtain 0.2634 mean Spearman,
soft-score-only features obtain 0.3100, hard and soft scores obtain
0.3671, and probability-derived scalar summaries obtain 0.3776. The
difference between hard and soft scores together and hard scores alone
is $`+0.1036`$ with a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank $`p=0.0068`$, while
the additional gain from probability-derived summaries over hard and
soft scores is $`+0.0106`$ with $`p=0.0801`$. Full results appear in
Appendix Table 19.

## 7 Conclusion

LLM-as-a-judge evaluation is best understood as protocol-conditioned
measurement. The final score is only a compressed view of a distribution
shaped by the scoring protocol. Our results show that score ranges and
extraction rules change judge behavior and that example-specific
distributional aggregation improves over equal-weight log pooling on the
original 10-cell benchmark. Across the expanded 16-cell evaluation,
JudgeMoE improves over the strongest local single judge by $`+0.0393`$
mean Spearman, with positive differences in 12/16 cells and a one-sided
Wilcoxon signed-rank $`p=0.0091`$. The WMT-COMET case further shows that
validation can favor a strong standalone evaluator when adding it to the
learned pool reduces performance. The practical question is which cached
measurements to trust, pool, prune, or keep standalone.

## Limitations

The original aggregation benchmark contains 10 dataset–scale cells,
followed by six additional cells evaluated with the same JudgeMoE
configuration. The resulting 16-cell analysis covers more evaluation
conditions, while several cells still share datasets, score scales, and
judge families. Evaluation on additional datasets and criteria would
further establish how the observed gains transfer across applications.

This paper studies learned aggregation over a fixed pool of small local
judges, so results depend on candidate quality, task match, and score
resolution. Larger evaluators are included as standalone comparison
points, while aggregation over pools of larger or proprietary judges
remains for future study. Broader comparisons across code execution,
factuality checking, and other evaluation settings would also require
careful cost normalization (Huang et al., 2025b).

The feature design is intentionally conservative. Current ablations
suggest that judge distributions carry much of the useful signal.
Comparisons with hidden states, explicit cross-judge interaction
features, full natural-language explanations, and a broader
same-capacity scalar-gated control remain useful next steps for
separating representation gains from model-capacity gains.

The distributional approach also depends on access to informative judge
score probabilities. The restricted-access experiments show that
expected scores recover substantial signal beyond decoded scores, while
probability-derived summaries provide a smaller additional improvement.
The reported ECE measures the calibration of the final scalar
predictions against human scores and does not establish calibration of
the underlying score-token probabilities. In addition, the present
benchmarks provide scalar human targets; annotator disagreement and
multimodal human preferences remain outside the current scope.

## Ethics Statement

Automatic evaluation systems can influence model selection, training
feedback, and deployment decisions, so errors or biases in judge models
may propagate directly into research conclusions. Judge aggregation adds
its own risks: protocol choices can privilege some score distributions
over others, and weak-judge contamination can distort pooled rankings.
We therefore emphasize comparison against human annotations,
cross-dataset evaluation, explicit discussion of failure modes, and
clear separation between architecture selection, held-out checkpoint
expansion checks, and validation-only deployment heuristics. The
appendix summarizes benchmark provenance and split counts; the released
artifact records original license constraints and external API use where
applicable. JudgeMoE is best understood as an efficiency-oriented
evaluation aid, with careful human assessment still required in
high-stakes settings.

## Appendix A Background and Problem Setup

We consider an evaluation instance $`x`$ consisting of task-specific
context and a candidate output, with human target signal $`y\in[0,1]`$.
Judge $`j`$ produces a categorical distribution $`p^{(j)}(\cdot\mid x)`$
over a finite score range $`\mathcal{S}=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{K}\}`$, where
the score values are normalized to $`[0,1]`$ before aggregation and
evaluation.

#### Hard score.

The hard score is the decoded label:

```math
\hat{y}^{(j)}_{\mathrm{hard}}=\arg\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}p_{j}(s\mid x). \tag{7}
```

#### Soft score.

The soft score uses the expected score under the judge distribution:

```math
\hat{y}^{(j)}_{\mathrm{soft}}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}s\,p_{j}(s\mid x). \tag{8}
```

Our experiments compare both protocols across several score ranges. The
empirical motivation for JudgeMoE is that the optimal score range is
unstable across judges and tasks, while each judge carries complementary
information through its full score distribution, confidence, and
entropy. This motivates an aggregation layer that consumes score
distributions directly while preserving information that would be
discarded after decoding a single label.

#### Goal.

Given multiple judges $`\{j_{1},\dots,j_{m}\}`$, we want to learn an
aggregation function

```math
\hat{y}(x)=f\bigl(z^{(1)}(x),\dots,z^{(m)}(x)\bigr), \tag{9}
```

where each $`z^{(j)}(x)`$ includes score-distribution features and
optional metadata. The desired aggregator is lightweight, easy to train,
and compatible with both direct-score and pairwise supervision.

#### Architecture variants.

The validation sweep compares several gates for computing judge weights
$`w_{j}(x)`$. The linear gate uses

```math
w(x)=\mathrm{softmax}(W\phi(x)+b),
```

where $`\phi(x)`$ concatenates the per-judge features. The low-rank gate
factorizes the weight matrix as $`W=UV^{\top}`$ to reduce parameters.
The DeepSets gate computes permutation-invariant judge weights by
applying a shared encoder to each judge feature vector and normalizing
the resulting scores:

```math
h_{j}=\rho(\phi^{(j)}(x)),\qquad w_{j}(x)=\mathrm{softmax}_{j}(a^{\top}h_{j}).
```

For distributional variants, the final pooled distribution is computed
with tempered log-opinion pooling,

```math
p_{\mathrm{pool}}(y\mid x)\propto\prod_{j=1}^{J}p_{j}(y\mid x)^{w_{j}(x)/T}.
```

### B.7 Focused Theoretical Perspective

This section keeps only the theory that ties directly to the JudgeMoE
formulation and the empirical storyline. The propositions below explain
three concrete design and deployment choices: why score distributions
can contain more information than compressed scores, why the best
pooling temperature is often $`T{<}1`$, and when sparse repair helps a
contaminated log pool. Several small judges can still lose to a larger
judge, as the WMT failure case shows. Proposition 1 together with
Propositions 3 and 4 are retained as background facts; Propositions 6–8
are specific to the JudgeMoE setup and connect directly to the main
empirical findings.

#### Notation.

Let the ordered human score take values in
$`\{v_{1},\dots,v_{K}\}\subset\mathbb{R}`$. A judge outputs a
categorical distribution $`P=(P_{1},\dots,P_{K})`$ over these values.
For multi-judge pooling, let $`w\in\Delta^{m-1}`$ denote a pooling
weight vector. When comparing two candidate score buckets $`a`$ and
$`b`$, write the per-judge likelihood ratio as
$`r_{j}(a,b)=p_{j}(a)/p_{j}(b)`$.

#### Proposition 1 (Conditional mean minimizes squared loss).

Let the ordered human score take values in
$`\{v_{1},\dots,v_{K}\}\subset\mathbb{R}`$, let a judge output a
categorical distribution $`P=(P_{1},\dots,P_{K})`$ over these values,
and define $`m(P)=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid P]`$. Then for any measurable scalar
summary $`a(P)`$,

```math
\mathbb{E}\!\left[(Y-a(P))^{2}\mid P\right]=\mathrm{Var}(Y\mid P)+\bigl(m(P)-a(P)\bigr)^{2}. \tag{10}
```

Hence the unique minimizer of conditional squared loss is

```math
a^{\star}(P)=m(P). \tag{11}
```

If the judge distribution is calibrated with respect to the human score
distribution, so that

```math
m(P)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}v_{k}P_{k}\equiv\mu(P), \tag{12}
```

then the soft score $`\mu(P)`$ is the optimal scalar summary under
squared loss. Under the same calibration condition, the hard score
$`h(P)=v_{\arg\max_{k}P_{k}}`$ is a Bayes action under exact-class
$`0`$–$`1`$ loss.

#### Proof sketch.

Apply the bias–variance decomposition around the conditional mean
$`m(P)=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid P]`$. The variance term is independent of
$`a(P)`$, so the conditional squared loss is minimized at $`a(P)=m(P)`$.
Under score-distribution calibration, $`m(P)=\mu(P)`$.

#### Interpretation.

Expected scoring is the natural one-dimensional reduction when the judge
distribution is aligned with the human-score distribution. The empirical
protocol study examines how well this reduction works with extracted LLM
score probabilities in practice.

#### Proposition 3 (Correlated error floor for averaging).

Assume judge score errors $`\varepsilon_{j}`$ are zero mean with

```math
\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon_{j})=\sigma^{2},\qquad\mathrm{Corr}(\varepsilon_{i},\varepsilon_{j})=\rho\ \text{for}\ i\neq j,
```

where $`\rho\in[0,1)`$. For uniform averaging,

```math
\bar{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\varepsilon_{j},
```

the error variance is

```math
\mathrm{Var}(\bar{\varepsilon})=\sigma^{2}\left(\rho+\frac{1-\rho}{m}\right). \tag{13}
```

Therefore,

```math
\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathrm{Var}(\bar{\varepsilon})=\sigma^{2}\rho. \tag{14}
```

#### Proof sketch.

Expand $`\mathrm{Var}(\bar{\varepsilon})`$ into diagonal and
off-diagonal covariance terms. Under the exchangeable-correlation
assumption, the diagonal terms contribute $`\sigma^{2}/m`$ and the
off-diagonal terms contribute $`\sigma^{2}\rho(1-1/m)`$.

#### Interpretation.

Ensembling removes only the idiosyncratic component of judge noise.
Shared bias or highly correlated error remains as an irreducible floor,
which formalizes why judge diversity matters more than simply adding
more judges.

#### Proposition 4 (Weak-judge veto threshold under log pooling).

Consider a weighted log pool

```math
\displaystyle q(y) \displaystyle\propto\exp\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}\log p_{j}(y)\right), \tag{15}
```

```math
\displaystyle\alpha_{j} \displaystyle\geq 0,\qquad\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}=1.
```

For two competing score buckets $`a`$ and $`b`$,

```math
\log\frac{q(a)}{q(b)}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}\log\frac{p_{j}(a)}{p_{j}(b)}. \tag{16}
```

Hence, if one weak judge $`j=0`$ prefers $`b`$ over $`a`$ while the
remaining judges prefer $`a`$ over $`b`$, the pooled ranking flips in
favor of $`b`$ if and only if

```math
\alpha_{0}\log\frac{p_{0}(b)}{p_{0}(a)}>\sum_{j\neq 0}\alpha_{j}\log\frac{p_{j}(a)}{p_{j}(b)}. \tag{17}
```

In the homogeneous equal-weight case with $`m`$ good judges,
$`p_{j}(a)=p`$, $`p_{j}(b)=p^{\prime}`$, and one weak judge with
$`p_{0}(a)=\delta`$, $`p_{0}(b)=\delta^{\prime}`$, this reduces to

```math
\log(\delta^{\prime}/\delta)>m\log(p/p^{\prime}). \tag{18}
```

#### Proof sketch.

Take the log ratio of the pooled probabilities for $`a`$ and $`b`$; the
result is exactly the weighted sum of per-judge log-likelihood ratios.
The flip condition is simply the sign change of this quantity.

#### Interpretation.

Log pooling rewards agreement, but it also gives every judge
multiplicative influence. A weak judge that places too little mass on
the correct bucket can therefore veto the remaining judges. This is the
mechanism behind the WMT weak-judge pollution result.

#### Linear-pool contrast.

Under equal-weight linear pooling in the same homogeneous setting, the
ranking flips only when

```math
\delta^{\prime}-\delta>m(p-p^{\prime}).
```

The weak judge enters additively in probability space, so single-judge
pollution is less explosive than in log pooling.

#### Proposition 6 (Optimal pooling temperature as a calibration diagnostic).

Suppose the ground-truth posterior over $`K`$ ordered scores given
instance $`x`$ is $`p^{*}(\cdot\mid x)`$. Model each judge $`j`$ as
producing a temperature-scaled version of that posterior:

```math
p_{k}^{(j)}(x)=\frac{\bigl(p_{k}^{*}(x)\bigr)^{1/\tau_{j}}}{Z_{j}(x)},\qquad Z_{j}(x)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\bigl(p_{\ell}^{*}(x)\bigr)^{1/\tau_{j}}, \tag{19}
```

where $`\tau_{j}>0`$ is the calibration temperature of judge $`j`$
($`\tau_{j}=1`$ calibrated, $`\tau_{j}>1`$ under-confident,
$`\tau_{j}<1`$ over-confident). Under equal-weight tempered log pooling
at temperature $`T`$, the fused distribution satisfies

```math
q_{k}^{T}(x)\propto\bigl(p_{k}^{*}(x)\bigr)^{\gamma(T)},\qquad\gamma(T)=\frac{1}{T\,\bar{\tau}_{H}}, \tag{20}
```

where $`\bar{\tau}_{H}=m\big/\sum_{j=1}^{m}\tau_{j}^{-1}`$ is the
harmonic mean of the judges’ calibration temperatures. The fused
distribution recovers the true posterior ($`q^{T}=p^{*}`$) if and only
if

```math
T^{*}=\frac{1}{\bar{\tau}_{H}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\tau_{j}}. \tag{21}
```

At this temperature the expected-score predictor
$`\hat{y}^{T^{*}}\!(x)=\sum_{k}s_{k}\,q_{k}^{T^{*}}\!(x)`$ equals the
Bayes-optimal point prediction $`\mathbb{E}[y\mid x]`$, and for any
$`T\neq T^{*}`$ the conditional MSE increases by the squared bias:

```math
\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\hat{y}^{T}-y)^{2}\mid x\bigr]=\operatorname{Var}(y\mid x)+\bigl(\hat{y}^{T}(x)-\hat{y}^{T^{*}}\!(x)\bigr)^{2}. \tag{22}
```

#### Proof sketch.

The equal-weight tempered log pool gives

```math
\log q_{k}^{T}(x)=\frac{1}{T}\cdot\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\log p_{k}^{(j)}(x)+\mathrm{const.}
```

Substituting (19),

```math
\log p_{k}^{(j)}(x)=\tau_{j}^{-1}\log p_{k}^{*}(x)-\log Z_{j}(x).
```

The normalization terms are constant across $`k`$ and vanish into the
pooled normalizer, so

```math
\log q_{k}^{T}(x)=\underbrace{\frac{1}{mT}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\tau_{j}}}_{\gamma(T)}\log p_{k}^{*}(x)+\mathrm{const.}
```

Writing $`\sum_{j}\tau_{j}^{-1}=m/\bar{\tau}_{H}`$ yields (20); setting
$`\gamma(T)=1`$ gives $`T^{*}=1/\bar{\tau}_{H}`$. The MSE identity then
follows from Proposition 1 and the standard bias–variance decomposition
around $`\mathbb{E}[y\mid x]`$.

#### Interpretation.

Proposition 6 turns the pooling temperature into a calibration
diagnostic as well as an empirical hyperparameter. The empirical
preference for $`T{=}0.7`$ in Table 12 is therefore consistent with a
collectively under-confident judge pool.

#### Scope.

The temperature-scaled model above is a first-order approximation. Real
judges exhibit instance-dependent miscalibration, so Proposition 6
characterizes the pool-average calibration condition without predicting
one exact optimal $`T`$ for every example. We use it only as a
directional diagnostic for the temperature sweep in Table 12. Its
practical use is to explain why the best temperature often lies below
one and to motivate initialization of the temperature sweep from a small
calibration set.

#### Proposition 7 (Distribution–score information gap).

Let $`\mathbf{P}=(p^{(1)},\ldots,p^{(m)})\in(\Delta^{K-1})^{m}`$ be the
distributional evidence from $`m`$ judges over $`K\geq 3`$ ordered
scores, and let $`\boldsymbol{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m})`$ with
$`\mu_{j}=\sum_{k}s_{k}\,p_{k}^{(j)}`$ be the corresponding score vector
of expected scores.

Here $`\sigma(\mathbf{P})`$ denotes the information contained in the
full collection of judge distributions, while
$`\sigma(\boldsymbol{\mu})`$ denotes the coarser information retained
after each distribution is reduced to its expected score. Thus
$`\sigma(\boldsymbol{\mu})\subseteq\sigma(\mathbf{P})`$ simply
formalizes the fact that expected scores are deterministic summaries of
the full distributions.

(i) The Bayes-optimal predictors
$`f^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})=\mathbb{E}[y\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}]`$ and
$`g^{*}(\mathbf{P})=\mathbb{E}[y\mid\mathbf{P}]`$ satisfy

```math
\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\bigl[(y-f^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu}))^{2}\bigr]}_{\text{score risk}}-\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\bigl[(y-g^{*}(\mathbf{P}))^{2}\bigr]}_{\text{distribution risk}} \tag{23}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\operatorname{Var}\bigl(g^{*}(\mathbf{P})\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}\bigr)\Bigr]\geq 0.
```

(ii) For any auxiliary feature $`\phi(\mathbf{P})`$ with information
beyond $`\boldsymbol{\mu}`$, the gap admits the lower bound

```math
\Delta_{\mathrm{info}}\geq\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\!\left[\frac{\bigl(\operatorname{Cov}(y,\phi(\mathbf{P})\mid\boldsymbol{\mu})\bigr)^{2}}{\operatorname{Var}(\phi(\mathbf{P})\mid\boldsymbol{\mu})}\right]. \tag{24}
```

(iii) When $`K=2`$, $`\sigma(\boldsymbol{\mu})=\sigma(\mathbf{P})`$ and
the gap is exactly zero. When $`K\geq 3`$ and judge distributions are
generically non-degenerate,
$`\sigma(\boldsymbol{\mu})\subsetneq\sigma(\mathbf{P})`$ and the gap is
generically strict.

#### Proof sketch.

Part (i) is the law of total variance applied to
$`\sigma(\boldsymbol{\mu})\subseteq\sigma(\mathbf{P})`$:

```math
\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(y\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}) \displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\operatorname{Var}(y\mid\mathbf{P})\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}\bigr]
```

```math
\displaystyle+\operatorname{Var}\bigl(\mathbb{E}[y\mid\mathbf{P}]\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}\bigr).
```

Taking expectations yields (23). For part (ii), conditional
Cauchy–Schwarz gives

```math
\operatorname{Var}\bigl(g^{*}(\mathbf{P})\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}\bigr)\geq\frac{\bigl(\operatorname{Cov}(g^{*}(\mathbf{P}),\phi(\mathbf{P})\mid\boldsymbol{\mu})\bigr)^{2}}{\operatorname{Var}(\phi(\mathbf{P})\mid\boldsymbol{\mu})},
```

and the tower property identifies the covariance term with
$`\operatorname{Cov}(y,\phi(\mathbf{P})\mid\boldsymbol{\mu})`$.
Part (iii) follows because for $`K=2`$ the simplex is one-dimensional,
so the map from distribution to expected score is affine and invertible,
whereas for $`K\geq 3`$ it has a non-trivial kernel inside
$`\Delta^{K-1}`$.

#### Interpretation.

Proposition 7 formalizes the paper’s central formulation choice.
Distributional aggregation has a genuine information advantage whenever
judge distributions vary in ways that are unrecoverable from their
expected scores alone. This is the setting in which entropy, confidence,
and near-tie structure become useful.

#### Example.

For scores $`\{1,2,3\}`$, the distributions $`(0.5,0,0.5)`$ and
$`(0,1,0)`$ have the same expected score, 2, while encoding very
different uncertainty: the first is a polarized near-tie between low and
high scores, while the second is concentrated on the middle score. A
score-only aggregator treats them the same; a distributional aggregator
can distinguish them.

#### Concrete instantiation.

Taking $`\phi(\mathbf{P})`$ to be mean judge entropy, confidence, or
maximum-bucket probability yields a computable lower bound on the
score-to-distribution gap. Appendix Table 18 provides the corresponding
empirical signal in our setting: moving from distributions alone to the
full scalar summary bundle raises average Spearman from 0.3746 to
0.3832, suggesting that these extra distributional statistics carry
useful information beyond the expected scores.

#### Scope.

The lower bound in (24) is intentionally general. Its role is
diagnostic: it says what kind of auxiliary distributional statistic can
certify a non-trivial information gap, while making no claim that any
single statistic captures the full gain.

#### Proposition 8 (Sparse improvement condition for expected-score MSE).

Under the calibration model (19) with equal weights and pooling
temperature $`T`$, define judge precision $`r_{j}=\tau_{j}^{-1}`$, mean
pool precision $`\bar{r}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}r_{j}`$, pool
precision excess $`\Delta=m(\bar{r}-T)`$, and judge precision deviation
$`\delta_{0}=r_{0}-T`$ for the candidate judge $`j_{0}`$ to be dropped.
The effective exponent of the full pool is
$`\gamma_{\mathrm{full}}=\bar{r}/T`$, and dropping $`j_{0}`$ yields
$`\gamma_{-0}=(m\bar{r}-r_{0})/((m-1)T)`$.

Dropping judge $`j_{0}`$ moves the effective exponent strictly closer to
the optimum $`\gamma^{*}=1`$ (equivalently,
$`|\gamma_{-0}-1|<|\gamma_{\mathrm{full}}-1|`$) if and only if

```math
\displaystyle\delta_{0} \displaystyle\in\Bigl(\tfrac{\Delta}{m},\tfrac{(2m-1)\Delta}{m}\Bigr) \displaystyle\text{when }\Delta>0, \tag{25}
```

```math
\displaystyle\delta_{0} \displaystyle\in\Bigl(\tfrac{(2m-1)\Delta}{m},\tfrac{\Delta}{m}\Bigr) \displaystyle\text{when }\Delta<0.
```

Equivalently, $`\delta_{0}`$ lies strictly between $`\bar{r}-T`$ and
$`(2m-1)(\bar{r}-T)`$. When $`\Delta=0`$ (the pool is already optimally
sharpened), dropping any judge can only hurt or leave the exponent
unchanged.

#### Proof sketch.

Substitute the effective exponents into the inequality
$`|\gamma_{-0}-1|<|\gamma_{\mathrm{full}}-1|`$:

```math
\gamma_{\mathrm{full}}-1=\frac{\Delta}{mT},\qquad\gamma_{-0}-1=\frac{\Delta-\delta_{0}}{(m-1)T}.
```

This reduces the problem to

```math
\frac{|\Delta-\delta_{0}|}{m-1}<\frac{|\Delta|}{m},
```

which becomes a quadratic inequality in $`\delta_{0}`$. Solving that
inequality yields exactly the interval in (25).

#### Corollary 1 (Weak judges hurt under-sharpened pools).

When the pool is under-sharpened ($`\Delta<0`$), a very weak judge has
$`r_{0}\approx 0`$ and therefore $`\delta_{0}\approx-T`$. In this
condition, dropping such a judge moves the pooled exponent closer to the
optimum and improves expected-score aggregation. This matches the WMT
MQM result in Table 53: removing Qwen2.5-7B raises the full-pool
baseline from 0.0016 to 0.0389 at the 1–5 scale.

#### Corollary 2 (Weak judges can help over-sharpened pools).

When the pool is over-sharpened ($`\Delta>0`$), a weak judge may provide
useful flattening; dropping it can fail to help because the pool already
places too much concentrated mass on a small set of score buckets.

#### Interpretation.

Proposition 8 reveals an asymmetry that is invisible in the mode-level
veto analysis of Proposition 4. Weak judges are reliably harmful when
the pool is already too flat, but can be harmless or even useful when
the pool is too sharp. Sparse repair is therefore a calibration-aware
intervention, with no universal preference for smaller pools.

#### Scope.

The equal-weight analysis in Proposition 8 characterizes the fixed-pool
baseline that JudgeMoE’s learned gate improves upon. Its role is to
identify which judges a fixed pool excludes; the learned gate can then
adaptively reweight the surviving pool. Appendix Table 52 gives the
matching empirical check: on WMT, the full gate already assigns
negligible weight to the weak Qwen2.5 judge, but sparse retraining is
still needed at 1–5 because coarse scores leave too little coherent
ranking signal in the contaminated full pool.

#### Connecting the results.

Propositions 6–8 form a coherent diagnostic chain. Proposition 6
diagnoses the pool-level calibration condition and interprets the best
temperature. Proposition 7 justifies why JudgeMoE works with score
distributions in addition to compressed scores, and identifies the
$`K\geq 3`$ condition under which that advantage can exist.
Proposition 8 then characterizes when removing a judge improves the
pool, conditional on the calibration condition identified by
Proposition 6. Together, they support the validation-guided deployment
heuristic in Table 4: study calibration first, keep distributional
evidence whenever the score range is fine enough to support it, and use
sparse repair only when the pool diagnostics indicate contamination.

#### Scope.

Propositions 1, 3, and 4 are standard background facts stated in compact
form; Propositions 6–8 are specific to the JudgeMoE formulation and
serve as structured diagnostic results with limited scope. None of them
imply that several small judges must beat a larger single judge. Their
role is more specific: to explain why soft scores help, why temperature
tuning is interpretable, why distributional fusion can add information
beyond score compression, and why sparse repair helps only in particular
contamination conditions.
