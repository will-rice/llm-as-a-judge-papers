###### Abstract

Evaluating large language models increasingly relies on LLM-as-a-judge
protocols, but such evaluations remain costly: different judges have
different prices and reliabilities, and the difficulty of each
prompt-response pair can vary substantially. This raises a basic
allocation question: under a fixed budget, how should one distribute
evaluation queries across heterogeneous judges and instances to obtain
the most accurate score estimates? We formalize this question as
_budgeted heteroskedastic multi-judge estimation_ under independent,
unbiased score observations. Given $`K`$ prompt-response pairs and $`J`$
judges with known costs but unknown, potentially heterogeneous
query–judge variances, our goal is to estimate the underlying bounded
score vector while minimizing an $`\ell_{p}`$-error. Our first
contribution is to analyze the inverse-variance weighted estimator
(IVWE) and to derive the oracle allocation that minimizes its leading
error bound. Since this allocation depends on the unknown variances, we
then address the unknown-variance setting by proposing EST-IVWE, an
adaptive algorithm that constructs and uses optimistic variance
estimates to stabilize the empirical allocation. We prove that EST-IVWE
matches the oracle IVWE rate up to lower-order terms in the budget. Our
second and central theoretical contribution is a local minimax lower
bound matching the leading allocation-dependent rate, establishing
asymptotic instance-optimality up to logarithmic factors. An
Assouad-type construction based on local, variance-preserving
perturbations yields this bound both in expectation and with constant
probability. A complementary Fano-type change-of-measure argument
captures the logarithmic dependence on the confidence level with a
different (suboptimal) allocation geometry. Finally, we numerically
validate our approach on synthetic data and resampled HelpSteer2
evaluations, demonstrating improvements over naïve uniform allocation.

## 1 Introduction

Evaluating the quality of responses produced by large language models
(LLMs) is a central component of modern model development, particularly
for post-training (Ouyang et al., 2022; Rafailov et al., 2023; Guo et
al., 2025; Bai et al., 2022) and model selection (Fernandez et al.,
2026). Traditional evaluation methods, however, face important
limitations. Human annotation remains the gold standard for capturing
human preferences and judgments (Ouyang et al., 2022; Rafailov et al.,
2023), but it is costly, time-consuming, and difficult to scale with the
rapid pace of model development. Standardized benchmarks provide a more
scalable alternative, but they are often too rigid for emerging
applications where suitable benchmarks do not yet exist. These
limitations have motivated the growing use of the “LLM-as-a-judge”
framework, in which strong frontier models are used to evaluate model
responses across diverse tasks (Zheng et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Gu
et al., 2026).

While LLM-as-a-judge reduces reliance on costly human feedback, it still
has computational and statistical concerns. Querying large frontier
models at scale remains expensive, and different judges may have very
different costs, expertise, and reliability (Kim et al., 2024; Li et
al., 2024; Raju et al., 2024). A uniform allocation of evaluation
queries is therefore typically inefficient: a small specialized judge
may be more reliable than a costly general-purpose model on some
domains, while using a frontier model for straightforward instances may
waste budget (Fernandez et al., 2026). The difficulty is that, although
the cost of each judge is usually known, the variability of its
evaluations is not. This variability is _heteroskedastic_: it depends
both on the judge and on the intrinsic difficulty of the prompt-response
pair being evaluated. This raises the central question studied in this
work:

Given a fixed evaluation budget, how should one allocate queries across
prompt-response pairs and multiple LLM judges in order to obtain the
most accurate estimates of response quality?

We formalize this question as a new statistical learning problem,
referred to as _budgeted heteroskedastic multi-judge estimation_. This
problem can be viewed as an adaptive, cost-aware generalization of
Neyman allocation (Neyman, 1934). In classical Neyman allocation,
samples are allocated across strata with known variances. Here, the
learner must allocate evaluation queries across query–judge pairs with
known costs but unknown, heteroskedastic variances, with the goal of
estimating the full score vector as accurately as possible. Formally,
given a total budget $`B`$, a set of queries
$`k\in[K]\coloneq\{1,\ldots,K\}`$, and a set of LLM judges $`j\in[J]`$,
each judge $`j`$ has a known query cost $`c_{j}`$, while each
query–judge pair $`(k,j)\in[K]\times[J]`$ has an unknown variance
$`\sigma_{k,j}`$. The learner’s objective is to adaptively allocate
queries to judges so as to estimate the true score vector
$`{{\bm{s}}}=(s_{k})_{k\in[K]}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}`$ while minimizing the
$`\ell_{p}`$-error, for a fixed $`p\in[1,\infty]`$ specified in advance.
As we discuss later, this subsumes the recently studied budget
allocation problem for a single LLM-as-a-judge in Saha et al. (2026)
that only considered $`J=1`$ and $`p=\infty`$. Furthermore, while our
setting is motivated by the LLM-as-a-judge framework (Saha et al.,
2026), it addresses a broader class of adaptive resource allocation
problems across heterogeneous information sources under budget
constraints.

Contributions. We provide a complete theoretical and algorithmic
solution to our problem:

_(a) Optimal allocation and an adaptive algorithm._ We first analyze the
inverse-variance weighted estimator (IVWE) and to derive the oracle
allocation that minimizes its error rate (Section 3). Since this
allocation depends on the unknown variances, we then address the
practical unknown-variance setting by proposing Est-IVWE (Algorithm 1),
an adaptive algorithm that constructs and leverages _optimistically
biased_ variance estimates to stabilize the empirical allocation. We
prove that Est-IVWE matches the oracle IVWE rate up to lower-order terms
in the budget (Section 4).

_(b) Local minimax lower bounds._ We derive _local_ minimax lower bounds
matching the leading allocation-dependent rate of Est-IVWE, hence
establishing its asymptotic instance-optimality up to logarithmic
factors (Section 5). A key technical insight is that our Fano-type
change-of-measure and Assouad-type arguments capture different
allocation geometries. The former characterizes the
$`\log\frac{1}{\delta}`$ dependence, while the latter, based on local,
variance-preserving perturbations, yields the sharp oracle
allocation-dependent rate both in expectation and with constant
probability.

_(c) Empirical validation._ We numerically validate Est-IVWE on both
synthetic data and the real-world _HelpSteer2_ dataset (Wang et al., 2024) processed by Saha et al. (2026). Our results demonstrate that
Est-IVWE consistently and significantly outperforms naïve uniform
allocation (Section 7).

#### Related Work.

While we contextualize our contributions with specific literature
throughout the text, we briefly situate our structural formulation
within broader statistical frameworks. In classical statistics,
estimating a shared mean from multiple heteroskedastic sources is known
as the _common mean problem_ (Cochran, 1937; Graybill and Deal, 1959;
Norwood Jr and Hinkelmann, 1977). This line of work explores how to
optimally aggregate samples, though traditionally without budget
constraints. More recently, theoretical computer science has studied a
variant featuring a single sample per population – referred to as
_entangled mean estimation_ (Chierichetti et al., 2014; Liang and Yuan,
2020; Diakonikolas et al., 2025)—largely motivated by crowdsourcing
applications. Despite this rich literature on heteroskedastic
aggregation, incorporating a fixed budget constraint across sources with
varying costs has, to the best of our knowledge, remained unexplored
prior to our work.

## 2 Problem Setting

There are $`K`$ queries, e.g., prompt–answer pairs, to be evaluated by
$`J`$ LLM judges. Each query $`k\in[K]`$ has an unknown ground-truth
score $`s_{k}\in\mathbb{R}`$. Whenever the learner assigns query $`k`$
to judge $`j`$, she incurs a cost $`c_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}`$ and
observes a noisy score $`s_{k,j}\in[0,R]`$, independently of past
observations. We assume that $`\mathbb{E}[s_{k,j}]=s_{k}`$ and
$`\sigma_{k,j}^{2}\coloneq\operatorname{Var}(s_{k,j})>0`$. The variances
$`\sigma_{k,j}^{2}`$ are unknown, while the score upper bound $`R`$ is
known. The learner operates online: at each step, she selects a
query–judge pair based on all observations collected so far. Given a
total budget $`B\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}`$ and a known cost vector
$`{\bm{c}}=(c_{j})_{j\in[J]}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{J}`$, her goal is to
provide an estimator of the score vector
$`{\bm{s}}=(s_{k})_{k\in[K]}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}`$ as accurate as possible,
i.e., minimizing the $`\ell_{p}`$-error, for a fixed $`p\in[1,\infty]`$
specified in advance. With this, each problem instance is characterized
by a triple
$`({\bm{s}},\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}^{K\times J}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{J}_{>0}`$,
where $`\bm{\sigma}\coloneq(\sigma_{k,j})_{k\in[K],j\in[J]}`$ is the
variance profile.

To assess and compare the performance, we introduce the following notion
of learnability:

###### Definition 2.1 ($`(B,\delta,\ell_{p})`$-Budget Efficient Algorithm).

Let $`p\in[1,\infty]`$, $`B\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}`$ be a total budget, and
$`\delta\in(0,1)`$ be a confidence level. An algorithm $`{\mathcal{A}}`$
with score estimate $`\hat{{\bm{s}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}`$ is said to be
$`(B,\delta,\ell_{p})`$-budget efficient at
$`({\bm{s}},\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})`$ with error rate
$`\varepsilon_{p}>0`$ if:

```math
{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{p}\leq\varepsilon_{p}\right)\geq 1-\delta\quad\hbox{ and }\quad\sum_{j\in[J]}c_{j}\sum_{k\in[K]}N_{k,j}\leq B,\hbox{ a.s.}
```

where $`N_{k,j}`$ denotes the (random) number of times $`{\mathcal{A}}`$
queries pair $`(k,j)\in[K]\times[J]`$.

#### Discussion.

We view this model as a first step toward a statistical foundation for
budget-constrained estimation from multiple LLM judges. It captures two
key characteristics: heterogeneous per-judge costs and query-dependent
reliability through the variance profile. The $`K`$ queries are fixed
and given; the learner chooses which query–judge pairs to evaluate and
must estimate $`{\bm{s}}`$.

The shared-mean assumption idealizes judges as unbiased estimators of a
common target score, which may serve as an approximation after
calibration. Likewise, the deterministic cost $`c_{j}`$ can represent a
conservative per-judge budget charge under fixed input and output token
limits, even when the realized cost is smaller. These assumptions
simplify practical evaluation pipelines, where residual bias,
dependence, and query-dependent stochastic costs can be important. We
next discuss the boundedness assumption and the role of
heteroskedasticity in our model; further discussion of its limitations
and possible extensions is provided in Appendix I.

The boundedness assumption $`s_{k,j}\in[0,R]`$ is quite realistic as the
LLM-judges are typically required to assign scores in fixed rating
scales (e.g., binary, $`\{0,1,2,3,4\}`$, or $`[0,100]`$) based on
scoring rubrics to ensure consistent evaluation (Gu et al., 2026). As we
will see later, such a boundedness assumption necessitates fundamentally
different analysis techniques and algorithmic design compared to Saha et
al. (2026), which assumes Gaussianity for algorithm design.
Additionally, we emphasize that nonnegativity is not necessary; all
statements hold for any interval of length $`R`$.

We now comment on the heteroskedasticity of the problem setting. By
Popoviciu’s inequality on variances (Popoviciu, 1935), a trivial bound
of $`\sigma_{k,j}^{2}\leq R^{2}/4`$ holds, but we note that it can be
significantly loose; for instance, if the pair $`(k,j)`$ is
“easy-to-evaluate” (the judge has sufficient expertise to “confidently”
evaluate the query), $`\sigma_{k,j}^{2}\ll R^{2}/4`$. Accounting for
such variation in the variances is a central challenge in the
heteroskedastic learning literature (Chaudhuri et al., 2017; Kirschner
and Krause, 2018; Fontaine et al., 2021).

## 3 Oracle Allocation for Inverse-Variance Weighted Estimation

In this section, we first provide high-probability guarantees for the
$`\ell_{p}`$-error of the celebrated Inverse-Variance Weighted Estimator
(IVWE) (Cochran, 1937; Cochran, 1954) under any fixed allocation, and
then derive the allocation minimizing this error. This allocation is
referred to as the Oracle allocation, because it requires the knowledge
of the variances $`\sigma_{k,j}^{2}`$.

Consider a fixed allocation and let $`N_{k,j}`$ be the number sampled
scores obtained for query $`k`$ from judge $`j`$. Alternatively, the
allocation can be represented by the cost-weighted proportions of the
budget allocated to the $`(k,j)`$-pairs by
$`\bm{\omega}\coloneq(\omega_{k,j}\triangleq\frac{c_{j}N_{k,j}}{B})_{k,j}`$.
The budget constraint $`B`$ is satisfied if and only if
$`\bm{\omega}\in\Lambda^{KJ}:=\{\bm{\lambda}\geq 0:\sum_{k,j}\lambda_{k,j}=1\}`$.
Let $`\{s_{k,j}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N_{k,j}}`$ denote sampled scores of query
$`k`$ from the judge $`j`$. The IVWE is defined by:

```math
\hat{s}_{k}\coloneq\left(\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{N_{k,j}}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}\right)^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{N_{k,j}\hat{s}_{k,j}}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}},\quad\hat{s}_{k,j}\coloneq\frac{1}{N_{k,j}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k,j}}{s}_{k,j}^{(i)},\ \forall j\in[J], \tag{2}
```

where by convention, $`\hat{s}_{k,j}=0`$ if $`N_{k,j}=0`$. The next
proposition provides error-rate guarantees for IVWE; its full statement
and proof are given in Section A.1.

###### Proposition 3.1.

For any instance $`({\bm{s}},\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})`$ and
$`p\in[1,\infty]`$, IVWE is $`(B,\delta,\ell_{p})`$-budget efficient
with the following error rate $`\varepsilon_{p}`$:

```math
\varepsilon_{p}\approx_{B}\sqrt{\frac{2{\mathcal{A}}_{p}(\bm{\omega};\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\log\frac{2K}{\delta}}{B}},\qquad{\mathcal{A}}_{p}(\bm{\omega};\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\coloneq\left(\sum\nolimits_{k\in[K]}\left(\sum\nolimits_{j\in[J]}\frac{\omega_{k,j}}{c_{j}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{p}{2}}\right)^{\frac{2}{p}}, \tag{3}
```

where $`\approx_{B}`$ omits lower-order terms in $`B`$.

In the above result, $`{\mathcal{A}}_{p}(\cdot)`$ is derived from the
variance of IVWE. We refer to it as the _allocation objective_, since
minimizing this quantity yields the allocation that minimizes the
estimator’s error rate. The following theorem, proved in Section A.2,
characterizes this optimal allocation.

###### Theorem 3.2.

For each query $`k\in[K]`$, let
$`j^{*}(k)\coloneq\argmin_{j\in[J]}c_{j}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}`$ be the
optimal judge. For $`p\in[1,\infty]`$, consider the following optimal
allocation problem:
$`{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\coloneq\min_{\bm{\omega}\in\Lambda^{KJ}}{\mathcal{A}}_{p}(\bm{\omega};\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})`$.
The value of this problem and the corresponding optimal allocation
$`\bm{\omega}^{*}=(\omega_{k,j}^{*})_{k,j}`$ are given by

```math
{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})=\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(c_{j^{*}(k)}\sigma_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+2}}\right)^{\frac{p+2}{p}},\ \omega_{k,j}^{*}=\frac{{\mathds{1}[j=j^{*}(k)]}\left(c_{j^{*}(k)}\sigma_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+2}}}{\sum_{k^{\prime}\in[K]}\left(c_{j^{*}(k^{\prime})}\sigma_{k^{\prime},j^{*}(k^{\prime})}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+2}}}. \tag{4}
```

A few remarks are in order. First, note that when $`J=1`$ (a single
judge), $`c_{1}=1`$ (unit cost), and $`p=\infty`$, the above result
reduces exactly to Saha et al. (2026, Theorem 3). Our setting therefore
generalizes the framework studied therein. An interesting consequence of
the theorem is that, regardless of $`p`$, an optimal allocation can be
chosen to be _sparse_: for each query $`k`$, the budget is assigned to a
single optimal judge $`j^{*}(k)`$. This follows from the direct
comparison in Section A.2: for a fixed budget assigned to query $`k`$,
its contribution to the allocation objective is minimized by assigning
that budget to a judge minimizing $`c_{j}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}`$.
Intuitively, once the budget allocated to a query is fixed, the most
accurate estimate $`\hat{s}_{k}`$ is obtained by assigning all of that
budget to the judge with the best cost–variance trade-off. Allocating
any positive budget to a judge $`j`$ with
$`c_{j}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}>c_{j^{*}(k)}\sigma_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}`$ can only
worsen, or at best leave unchanged, the resulting estimate. Thus, while
the choice of $`p`$ changes how budget is distributed across queries, a
single optimal judge per query always suffices. If several judges attain
the same minimum, splitting the query’s budget among them is also
optimal.

At this stage, the optimality statement concerns the minimization of our
upper bound. In Section 5, we show that this bound is in fact minimax
optimal.

## 4 Est-IVWE: An Adaptive Algorithm and its Error Rates

In this section, we consider unknown variances, and propose an adaptive
algorithm Est-IVWE. It proceeds in two phases: the first phase estimates
the variances, while the second allocates the remaining budget according
to the empirical optimal allocation induced by these estimates. The
pseudo code of Est-IVWE is given in Algorithm 1. Before providing
error-rate guarantees for Est-IVWE, we first motivate its design and
discuss the main challenge in its analysis.

Input: Total budget $`B>0`$, Cost vector
$`{\bm{c}}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}_{+}`$, Error norm $`p\in[1,\infty]`$,
Confidence level $`\delta\in(0,1)`$, Number of forced exploration per
pair $`N_{0}\geq 2`$

// Phase I: Forced Exploration

1 for _each $`(k,j)\in[K]\times[J]`$_ do

    2 Pull arm $`(k,j)`$ exactly $`N_{0}`$ times and observe noisy
evaluation scores $`\left\{s_{k,j}^{(n)}\right\}_{n\in[N_{0}]}`$;

    3 Compute the empirical variance estimator

```math
\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}^{2} \displaystyle:= \displaystyle\frac{1}{2N_{0}(N_{0}-1)}\sum_{1\leq n\neq n^{\prime}\leq N_{0}}\left(s_{k,j}^{(n)}-s_{k,j}^{(n^{\prime})}\right)^{2}; \tag{5}
```

// Phase II: IVWE

4 Compute the optimal allocation
$`\left(\widehat{\omega}_{k,j}^{*}\right)_{k,j}`$ as in Theorem 3.2
using the remaining budget
$`B^{\prime}\triangleq B-N_{0}K\sum_{j\in[J]}c_{j}-K\max_{j\in[J]}c_{j}`$,
based on
$`\left\{\left(\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}+\tau\right)^{2}\right\}_{k,j}`$ with
$`\tau`$ as in Eqn. (6);

5 Set
$`\widehat{N}_{k,j}^{*}=\left\lceil{B^{\prime}\widehat{\omega}_{k,j}^{*}}/{c_{j}}\right\rceil`$,
and query each pair $`(k,j)`$ exactly $`\widehat{N}_{k,j}^{*}`$ times;

6 return IVWE with $`\left(\widehat{N}_{k,j}^{*}\right)_{k,j}`$ as
allocation and $`(\tau+\hat{\sigma}_{k,j})^{2}`$ as variance proxies;

Algorithm 1 Estimate-then-IVWE (Est-IVWE)

Optimistically biased variance estimates. A key challenge in analyzing
the performance of Algorithm 1 is quantifying _how accurately the
variances are estimated from Phase I_. Ideally, we seek a multiplicative
guarantee of the form
$`(1-\alpha)\sigma_{k,j}^{2}\leq\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}^{2}\leq(1+\alpha)\sigma_{k,j}^{2}`$
for some $`\alpha\in(0,1)`$. The common factors $`(1\pm\alpha)`$ then
give a direct comparison between empirical and oracle allocations
(Theorem 3.2). Under Gaussian scores, Saha et al. (2026, Lemma 6) use
such multiplicative bounds, obtained from $`\chi^{2}`$ concentration
(Laurent and Massart, 2000, Eqn. (4.4) & (4.5)). These bounds do not
apply to general bounded scores. Instead, we utilize the empirical
Bernstein inequality (Maurer and Pontil, 2009, Theorem 10), which
provides an additive guarantee of
$`|\sigma_{k,j}-\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}|\lesssim\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/N_{0}}`$
($`N_{0}`$ is the length of the first phase). Now, if we were to use
$`\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}`$ directly and require it to satisfy analogous
multiplicative guarantee, we would require
$`N_{0}\gtrsim\frac{1}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}`$. This is
problematic because we require the knowledge of $`\sigma_{k,j}`$ to set
$`N_{0}`$, and for _easy_ pairs in which $`\sigma_{k,j}`$ is near-zero,
the requirement on the number of exploratory samples $`N_{0}`$ becomes
prohibitively large.

To overcome these limitations, we use an _optimistic variance
estimator_,

```math
\bar{\sigma}_{k,j}=\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}+\tau,\qquad\text{where}\quad\tau:=R\sqrt{\frac{2}{N_{0}-1}\log\frac{4KJ}{\delta}}. \tag{6}
```

Here, the bias $`\tau`$ comes from the empirical Bernstein inequality so
that $`\sigma_{k,j}\leq\bar{\sigma}_{k,j}\leq\sigma_{k,j}+2\tau`$ with
high probability; see Lemma B.2. This optimistic correction stabilizes
the empirical allocation, especially for query–judge pairs with
near-zero variance, whose estimates are otherwise highly unstable. It
also ensures that such pairs receive a sufficient amount of budget for
reliable estimation. By tuning $`\tau`$ and $`N_{0}`$ appropriately as
functions of the total budget $`B`$, the extra cost induced by this
stabilization remains lower order relative to the leading error term.

The following theorem formalizes the resulting error guarantees.

###### Theorem 4.1 (Error Rates of Est-IVWE).

Let $`p\in[1,\infty]`$, $`B\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}`$, and
$`\delta\in(0,1)`$. Let us set

```math
\displaystyle N_{0}=\begin{cases}\max\left\{2,\left\lceil(2B)^{1/3}\left(R^{2}\log\frac{4KJ}{\delta}\right)^{2/3}\right\rceil\right\}&\hbox{ if }p\geq 2,\\
\\
\max\left\{2,\left\lceil 2^{\frac{p}{2p+2}}R^{\frac{3p+2}{2p+2}}\left(\log\frac{4KJ}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{3p+2}{4p+4}}B^{\frac{p+2}{4p+4}}\right\rceil\right\}&\hbox{ if }1\leq p<2.\end{cases} \tag{7}
```

Further suppose that
$`B\geq 2\left(N_{0}K\sum_{j\in[J]}c_{j}+K\max_{j\in[J]}c_{j}\right)`$.
Then, Est-IVWE is $`(B,\delta,\ell_{p})`$-budget efficient at any
$`({\bm{s}},\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})`$ with the following error rate
$`\varepsilon_{p}`$:

```math
\varepsilon_{p}=\sqrt{\frac{2{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{B}}+\begin{cases}{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{R^{\frac{1}{3}}{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\left(\log\frac{4KJ}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}}{B^{\frac{2}{3}}}\right)&\hbox{ if }p\geq 2,\\
{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{R^{\frac{p}{2p+2}}{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\left(\log\frac{4KJ}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{3p+2}{4p+4}}}{B^{\frac{3p+2}{4p+4}}}\right)&\hbox{ if }1\leq p<2.\end{cases} \tag{8}
```

###### Proof sketch.

We divide the $`(k,j)`$-pairs into two cases using the bias $`\tau`$ as
an implicit threshold. For “easy-to-evaluate" pairs with
$`\sigma_{k,j}\leq\tau`$, the bias stabilizes the ratio
$`(\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}+\tau)/(\sigma_{k,j}+\tau)`$, avoiding the
numerical instability of $`\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}/\sigma_{k,j}`$ when
$`\sigma_{k,j}\approx 0`$. For “hard-to-evaluate" pairs with
$`\sigma_{k,j}>\tau`$, the multiplicative guarantee between
$`\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}+\tau`$ and $`\sigma_{k,j}+\tau`$ follows, as we
choose $`\tau`$ to be the concentration radius of (Maurer and Pontil,
2009, Theorem 10). Since the selection rule
$`\hat{j}^{*}(k)=\argmin_{j}c_{j}\overline{\sigma}_{k,j}^{2}`$ in Phase
II implies
$`c_{\hat{j}^{*}(k)}\overline{\sigma}_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{2}\leq c_{j^{*}(k)}\overline{\sigma}_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}`$,
we can upper bound the error $`\varepsilon_{p}`$ of the empirical
allocation; and then we decouple the effect of additive $`\tau`$ using
Minkowski’s inequality. We then conclude by separating the impact of
initial exploration $`N_{0}`$ from the total budget $`B`$, to recover
the _same_ leading term as the error rate of IVWE. The full proof is
deferred to Appendix B. ∎

We conclude the section with two remarks on Est-IVWE and its
performance.

(1) Oracle performance without known variances. Remarkably, Theorem 4.1
shows that, for every fixed instance and all $`p\in[1,\infty]`$, the
adaptive algorithm Est-IVWE (Algorithm 1) matches the leading
$`B^{-1/2}`$ term of the oracle strategy that knows the variances in
advance (Theorem 3.2). Thus, in the sufficiently large budget $`B`$,
there is no price for not knowing the variances: a simple two-stage
procedure recovers the oracle rate.

(2) Relations to Graybill-Deal Estimator. Estimating $`s_{k}`$ from
multiple sources is an instance of the classical _common mean problem_
(Graybill and Deal, 1959; Pal et al., 2007). Our two-stage estimator is
closely related to the _Graybill–Deal_ estimator, which uses all
available samples to estimate both means and variances. Reusing samples
induces correlations between these estimates, complicating sharp
performance analysis. Even for Gaussian populations, tight variance
characterizations are technically involved (Nair, 1980; Voinov, 1984),
and admissibility is known only for certain classes of problems (Ghosh
and Sinha, 1981; Sinha and Mouqadem, 1982; Pal and Lim, 1997). We avoid
this dependence through _sample splitting_: Phase-I samples estimate the
variances $`\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}^{2}`$, while fresh Phase-II samples yield
the final IVWE estimates $`\hat{s}_{k}`$. This separates the randomness
used in the two phases and ensures that, conditioned on the estimated
variances, IVWE remains unbiased,
$`\mathbb{E}\!\left[\hat{s}_{k}\,\middle|\,\{\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}\}_{k,j}\right]=s_{k}.`$

## 5 Local Minimax Lower Bounds

We now establish fundamental local minimax lower bounds for our budgeted
heteroskedastic multi-judge estimation problem. The term _local_ means
that the lower bound holds in a small neighborhood of any fixed problem
instance, in the same spirit as local minimax results for logistic
bandits (Abeille et al., 2021, Theorem 2), online LQR (Simchowitz and
Foster, 2020, Theorem 1), and generalized trace regression (Lee et al.,
2026, Theorem 4.1). These results show that the instance-dependent
allocation quantity $`{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})`$,
initially derived from upper bounds for IVWE, is in fact the correct
information-theoretic complexity measure. A key technical insight in our
lower-bound analysis is that our two arguments capture complementary
aspects of the problem: a Fano-type change-of-measure argument
characterizes the $`\log\frac{1}{\delta}`$ dependence, while an
Assouad-type construction recovers the oracle allocation-dependent
complexity.

### 5.1 A Fano-Type High-Probability Lower Bound

We begin by stating a high-probability minimax lower bound that
characterizes the $`\log\frac{1}{\delta}`$ dependence through a
Fano-type change-of-measure argument:

###### Theorem 5.1 (High Probability Lower Bound).

Let $`p\in[1,\infty]`$, $`B>0`$, $`\delta\in(0,1/16]`$, and
$`({\bm{s}},\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})`$ be a given problem instance. Let
$`R_{k}\coloneq\min\{s_{k},R-s_{k}\}>0`$, $`R_{\min}=\min_{k}R_{k}`$,
and suppose that $`\max_{j}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}\leq\frac{R_{k}^{2}}{2}`$.
Then, any algorithm that is $`(B,\delta,\ell_{p})`$-budget efficient
with an error rate of
$`\varepsilon_{p}\in\left(0,\frac{R_{\min}}{8}\right]`$ at any
$`({\bm{s}}^{\prime},\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})`$ with
$`\left\lVert{\bm{s}}^{\prime}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{p}\leq 2\varepsilon_{p}`$¹¹
1 This in the same spirit as locally stable algorithms used for proving
local & high-probability lower bounds (Jedra and Proutière, 2023; Yun
and Proutière, 2019). must satisfy the following: for an absolute
constant $`C_{1}>0`$,

```math
\varepsilon_{p}\geq\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{C_{1}B}{{\mathcal{A}}_{\frac{2p}{2-p}}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})}}\ \ \ \text{for $1\leq p<2$},\quad\varepsilon_{p}\geq\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{C_{1}B}{{\mathcal{A}}^{*}_{\infty}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})}}\ \ \ \text{for $p\geq 2$}. \tag{9}
```

###### Proof sketch.

We suppose that the learner has knowledge of the true $`\bm{\sigma}`$.
The proof uses a Fano-type change-of-measure argument, following the
hypothesis testing framework (Jedra and Proutière, 2023) based on the
binary KL data processing inequality (Garivier et al., 2019, Lemma 1).
The main technical novelty is the construction of the prior over
$`{\bm{s}}`$ to retain the instance-specific nature. Specifically, when
constructing alternate models, one needs to ensure that $`(i)`$ its mean
stays close to $`{\bm{s}}`$, $`(ii)`$ its variance remains the _same_ as
$`\bm{\sigma}`$, and $`(iii)`$ it is bounded in $`[0,R]`$. This is done
by considering a family of _beta distributions_ (Proposition C.1). The
proof “bifurcates” at $`p=2`$: the function $`z\mapsto z^{2/p}`$ is
convex for $`p\leq 2`$ and concave for $`p\geq 2`$, and is linear at
$`p=2`$. Consequently, minimizing the quadratic KL bound at a prescribed
$`\ell_{p}`$ distance admits a sparse solution for $`p\geq 2`$ and a
dense solution for $`1\leq p<2`$ (Lemma C.2). We provide the full proof
in Appendix C. ∎

The high-probability bound matches the upper bound only in the case
$`p=\infty`$, up to a factor of $`\sqrt{\log K}`$. For $`p<\infty`$,
however, it does not recover the allocation quantity
$`{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})`$ appearing in the upper
bound of IVWE. Indeed, the lower bound only attains
$`{\mathcal{A}}_{\frac{2p}{2-p}}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})`$ for
$`1\leq p<2`$ and $`{\mathcal{A}}_{\infty}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})`$
for $`p\geq 2`$. This is _not_ the oracle allocation dependence for
finite $`p`$. Its explicit $`\log\frac{1}{\delta}`$ dependence
nevertheless remains informative, as we discuss in Section 5.3.

### 5.2 An Assouad-Type In-Expectation Lower Bound

We now recover the allocation dependence missing from the previous
Fano-type construction. Using an Assouad-type argument, we obtain a
local in-expectation lower bound that matches
$`{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})`$ for all
$`p\in[1,\infty]`$. The same construction also yields a
constant-probability lower bound, as noted below.

###### Theorem 5.2 (In-Expectation Lower Bound).

Let $`{p\in[1,\infty]}`$, $`B>0`$ and
$`({\bm{s}}^{\star},\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})`$ be any given problem
instance with $`{\bm{s}}^{\star}=(s_{k}^{\star})_{k}`$. Suppose that
$`R_{k}\coloneq\min\{s_{k}^{\star},R-s_{k}^{\star}\}{>0}`$ and
$`0<\sigma_{k,j}^{2}\leq\tfrac{R_{k}^{2}}{2}`$ for all $`(k,j)`$. Define
the “radius”
$`\xi(B)\coloneq\sqrt{\frac{{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})}{16B}}`$
and the ‘‘threshold’’²² 2 For $`p=\infty`$, the expressions are
interpreted by continuity, giving
$`B_{0}={\mathcal{A}}_{\infty}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\max_{k}R_{k}^{-2}`$.
as
$`B_{0}\coloneq\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(c_{j^{*}(k)}\sigma_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+2}}\right)\max_{k\in[K]}\frac{\left(c_{j^{*}(k)}\sigma_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{2}{p+2}}}{{R_{k}^{2}}}`$.
Then, there exists a universal constant $`C_{3}>0`$ such that for
$`B\geq B_{0}`$,

```math
\inf_{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}\sup_{{\bm{s}}:\left\lVert{\bm{s}}-{\bm{s}}^{\star}\right\rVert_{p}\leq\xi(B)}\mathbb{E}^{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}_{{\bm{s}}}\left[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{p}\right]\geq C_{3}\sqrt{\frac{{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})}{B}}, \tag{10}
```

and furthermore for $`1\leq p<\infty`$ and $`B\geq pB_{0}`$,

```math
\inf_{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}\sup_{{\bm{s}}:\left\lVert{\bm{s}}-{\bm{s}}^{\star}\right\rVert_{p}\leq\sqrt{p}\xi(B)}\mathbb{E}^{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}_{{\bm{s}}}\left[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}\geq 4^{1-\frac{1}{p}}C_{3}\sqrt{\frac{p{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})}{B}}. \tag{11}
```

###### Proof sketch.

The full proof is deferred to Appendix D. We follow the standard
nonparametric lower bound framework based on the seminal Assouad’s Lemma
(Assouad, 1983; Yu, 1997) combined with the Bretagnolle-Huber inequality
(Bretagnolle and Huber, 1979). We first construct a hypercube of
$`2^{K}`$ hard instances, and each vertex corresponding to the same beta
distribution construction as in the proof of Theorem 5.1
(Proposition C.1).

For $`p>1`$,
$`\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{p}]`$ is
_not_ decomposable coordinate-wise, hindering the direct application of
Assouad’s lemma. For finite $`p`$, we bypass this using a secant
bounding argument (leveraging the concavity of $`x\mapsto x^{1/p}`$),
decoupling the expectation to establish a lower bound proportional to
$`\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}\right\rVert_{p}`$. For $`p=\infty`$, we use
equal perturbations and lower bound the probability of at least one
incorrect sign. For finite $`p`$, the $`p`$-th moment is coordinate-wise
decomposable, so we can directly apply Assouad’s Lemma. Finally, by
tuning the adversarial shifts $`\bm{\Delta}`$ under KL-divergence
constraints and minimizing the bound over all valid algorithm budget
allocations, we recover the optimal rate
$`{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})`$. ∎

#### Beyond In-Expectation.

For finite $`p`$, the same Assouad construction also gives a
constant-probability lower bound via the reverse Markov inequality; see
Ma et al. (2026) for a general conversion from local minimax risk to
minimax quantiles. (All proofs here are deferred to Appendix D.) Under
the assumptions of Theorem 5.2, with the same $`C_{3}`$, for every
$`\delta\in(0,4C_{3})`$,

```math
\inf_{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}\sup_{{\bm{s}}:\left\lVert{\bm{s}}-{\bm{s}}^{\star}\right\rVert_{p}\leq\xi(B)}{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}}^{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}\!\left(\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{p}\geq\xi(B)\left(\frac{4C_{3}-\delta}{1-\delta}\right)^{1/p}\right)\geq\delta. \tag{12}
```

Taking $`\delta=2C_{3}`$ recovers the allocation-dependent rate with
constant probability, but the threshold stays bounded as
$`\delta\downarrow 0`$: this conversion does not recover the
$`\log\frac{1}{\delta}`$ dependence of Theorem 5.1. For $`p=\infty`$,
the construction directly gives
$`{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}}^{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}(\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\geq\xi(B))\geq 4C_{3}`$
at some vertex in the same local neighborhood. Both arguments are given
in Appendix D.

We make two further remarks concerning our lower bounds:

(1) Geometry of Constrained Lower Bounds. Optimizing over algorithms
subject to a budget constraint is reminiscent of techniques used in
statistical estimation under communication constraints (Zhu and
Lafferty, 2014; Chen et al., 2021; Chen and Özgür, 2024). In such
settings, restricting the algorithm class naturally gives rise to an
optimal allocation problem. Our analysis reveals a related phenomenon,
with different allocation quantities governing the bounds obtained from
our Fano/Assouad-type arguments. We discuss their complementary roles in
the next subsection.

(2) In-Expectation Upper Bounds. The constant-probability consequence
above permits comparison with the high-probability upper bounds in
Theorems 3.2 and 4.1 at sufficiently small fixed $`\delta`$. For known
variances and fixed $`1\leq p<\infty`$, a standard moment argument based
on Bernstein’s inequality (Bernstein, 1927) yields an oracle
expected-error bound with leading rate
$`\sqrt{{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})/B}`$, without
logarithmic factors and up to constants depending on $`p`$. In
particular, at the oracle allocation, independence gives
$`\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}]={\mathcal{A}}_{2}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})/B`$;
Gaussian scores further permit exact finite-$`p`$ moment calculations
(Theorem F.7). For $`p=\infty`$, $`\log K`$ dependencies may remain.

### 5.3 Different Allocation Geometries for Assouad and Fano

Our two lower-bound arguments capture complementary aspects of the
estimation problem. The Fano-type change-of-measure argument in
Theorem 5.1 yields an explicit $`\log\frac{1}{\delta}`$ dependence, but
its allocation quantity differs from $`{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}`$. The
Assouad-type construction in Theorem 5.2 recovers
$`{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}`$ both in expectation and with constant
probability, but lacks the $`\log\frac{1}{\delta}`$ factor.

The difference comes from how the two constructions aggregate errors
across queries. Our Fano-type change-of-measure argument searches for an
alternative with small KL divergence at a prescribed $`\ell_{p}`$
distance. The resulting quadratic optimization admits a dense solution
for $`1\leq p<2`$ and a sparse solution for $`p\geq 2`$ (Lemma C.2),
leading to $`{\mathcal{A}}_{2p/(2-p)}^{*}`$ and
$`{\mathcal{A}}_{\infty}^{*}`$, respectively. The Assouad-type argument
instead _first_ lower bounds the weighted sign errors coordinate-wise,
_then_ aggregates them, and _finally_ optimizes over the algorithm’s
budget allocation. Its hypercube of perturbations retains the
simultaneous contribution of all queries. Both constructions preserve
the same variance profile; the distinction concerns the aggregation of
coordinate errors.

Then one may wonder if the correct lower bound should be of the form
$`\sqrt{{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}\log(1/\delta)/B}`$. We argue that this may
not be the case. Consider the Gaussian setting with one unit-cost judge,
variance $`\sigma^{2}`$, and $`p=2`$. With $`B/K`$ samples per query,
assumed integer for simplicity, the sample mean satisfies
$`{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{B}}\left(K+\sqrt{2K\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)\right)\geq 1-\delta`$;
see Eqn. (169) in Appendix F. Here,
$`{\mathcal{A}}_{2}^{*}=\sigma^{2}K^{2}`$ and
$`{\mathcal{A}}_{\infty}^{*}=\sigma^{2}K`$: the first term reflects the
error accumulated across all queries, while the second reflects the
additional cost of demanding higher confidence. Thus, the two terms
naturally involve different allocation quantities. In particular, a
lower bound of order $`\sqrt{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}^{*}\log(1/\delta)/B}`$
would not hold uniformly in $`K`$ and $`\delta`$ even in this simple
Gaussian example.

A similar decomposition is established by Ma et al. (2026, Proposition 10) for Gaussian mean estimation: the minimax squared-error quantile has
a trace term measuring the overall estimation difficulty and an
operator-norm term multiplied by $`\log\frac{1}{\delta}`$. Their proof
combines an Assouad risk bound converted to a probability statement with
a separate two-point confidence argument. This supports viewing our two
lower bounds as complementary, but we remark that a sharp
characterization jointly in the variance profile, $`p`$, and $`\delta`$
remains open in our budgeted setting.

## 6 Beyond Boundedness: Gaussian Scores

We briefly describe how the results presented so far extend to _Gaussian
scores_, as considered in (Saha et al., 2026), namely
$`s_{k,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(s_{k},\sigma_{k,j}^{2})`$. In this setting, all
results carry over, and in some cases take a sharper form. Technically,
the Gaussian case is of independent interest because it allows the use
of tools that exploit distribution-specific structure. For the interest
of space, we only provide an overview, highlighting the key technical
ingredients. Full details are deferred to Appendix F.

#### Upper Bounds.

For known variances, we replace Bernstein’s inequality with the standard
Gaussian tail bound applied separately for each query $`k`$. This yields
a cleaner upper bound, with no lower-order term from Bernstein’s
inequality. For unknown variances, we can exploit
$`\chi^{2}`$-concentration for the Gaussian sample variance (Laurent and
Massart, 2000, Eq. (4.4)). This allows us to use the IVWE directly,
without introducing the additional optimistic bias $`\tau`$.

#### Lower Bounds.

Here, we consider global rather than local lower bounds. The
high-probability lower bound is essentially unchanged, except that we
use Gaussian constructions instead of Beta distributions, which affects
only constants. The in-expectation lower bounds, for both
$`\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\|_{p}]`$ and
$`\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\|_{p}^{p}]^{1/p}`$, are
substantially different and technically more interesting, which we
sketch here. We place a Gaussian prior over the instances and use a
convexity argument to show that the posterior mean minimizes the
expected $`\ell_{p}`$-risk. Since the posterior distribution is Gaussian
with inverse Fisher information as the covariance, rearranging yields
the same optimal allocation in the lower bound. Remarkably, for
$`\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\|_{p}^{p}]^{1/p}`$, this lower
bound is optimal up to universal constants.

## 7 Experiments

We now numerically evaluate the performance of our algorithms on
synthetic and real-world datasets. The codes are available in our
[GitHub repository](https://github.com/sanghwakim96/llm-judge).

#### Settings.

For the synthetic experiment, we set $`(K,J)=(1000,10)`$ and consider
the Beta distribution for each query–judge pair’s score distribution
($`R=1`$). The instance, characterized by
$`({\bm{s}},\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})`$, is randomly sampled from an
appropriate prior distribution, detailed in Section H.1.

We conduct real-world experiments following Saha et al. (2026), in which
queries from HelpSteer2 (Wang et al., 2024) are evaluated by three LLM
judges — Llama-3-8B (Llama Team, 2024), GPT-4 (OpenAI Team, 2024), and
Qwen (Qwen Team, 2024) — along four axes: complexity, correctness,
helpfulness, and verbosity. For each axis, we utilize the pre-generated
dataset from Saha et al. (2026), where each query is repeatedly
evaluated with each judge. During the experiments, we simulate each
judge by sampling with replacement from the generated dataset. We set
the judge costs uniformly to $`0.1`$ for simplicity, and we consider
$`p=2`$ for the error metric. Further details of the preprocessing
procedure are provided in Section H.2.

#### Baselines.

We compare Est-IVWE (Bounded) (Algorithm 1) and its practical Gaussian
variant, Est-IVWE (Gaussian) (Algorithm 2), against two baselines:
Uniform, which assigns each query–judge pair an equal number of times
subject to the budget constraint, and Oracle, which is IVWE with the
optimal allocation $`\bm{\omega}^{*}`$ under known variances
(Theorem 3.2). In implementing Est-IVWE (Algorithm 1) and Est-IVWE
(Gaussian) (Algorithm 2), the exploration budget $`N_{0}`$ and the bias
$`\tau`$ are set to the theoretical values as in Theorems 4.1 and F.4,
rather than being heuristically tuned.

#### Results and Discussions.

The results for synthetic and HelpSteer2 datasets are shown in Figure 1.
First, Est-IVWE and Oracle both outperform the Uniform baseline,
confirming the efficacy of adaptive allocation under the
heteroskedasticity of the query–judge pairs. Amongst the adaptive
baselines, we note that Est-IVWE rapidly approaches the performance of
the Oracle as the budget increases, validating our theoretical results.
Notably, in both settings, Est-IVWE (Gaussian) outperforms or performs
on par with Est-IVWE (Bounded), despite the fact that the score
distributions are strictly bounded. This shows that the Gaussian variant
may be more effective in practice, although it lacks a rigorous
theoretical guarantee. On HelpSteer2, the error curves appear to plateau
at non-zero values, possibly reflecting differences between the mean
judge scores and the consensus scores $`s_{k}`$. Additional results from
different $`p`$ and different datasets are provided in Sections H.1
and H.2. Furthermore, in Section H.3, we provide an additional
cost-sensitivity analysis on synthetic data to demonstrate that our
algorithm remains robust to different choices of the cost vector.

## 8 Conclusion and Future Work

This paper introduces the problem of _budgeted heteroskedastic
multi-judge estimation_, motivated by budget-constrained evaluation via
multiple LLM judges of varying costs. Our main algorithmic template is
the inverse-variance weighted estimator (IVWE), whose error rate we
analyze to derive the optimal oracle allocation. Building on this, we
develop Est-IVWE for unknown variances via a two-stage approach: Stage
I, the key ingredient, computes _optimistic_ variance estimates, and
Stage II applies IVWE with those estimates. We establish the asymptotic
_instance-wise_ optimality of our algorithm up to logarithmic factors by
proving a local minimax lower bound matching its leading
allocation-dependent rate. Interestingly, our Fano-type
change-of-measure and Assouad-type arguments capture different
allocation geometries: the former captures the $`\log\frac{1}{\delta}`$
dependence, while the latter recovers the sharp allocation-dependent
quantity; this distinction may be of independent interest. We
numerically validate IVWE and Est-IVWE on synthetic and HelpSteer2
datasets, demonstrating improvements over naïve uniform allocation.

Our work’s practical applicability is limited by the modeling
assumptions introduced in Section 2. In Appendix I, we clarify their
scope and practical justification and discuss concrete directions for
future work. Two further directions are worth highlighting. On the
theoretical side, as discussed in Section 5, sharply characterizing the
joint dependence on the variance profile, $`p`$, and
$`\log\frac{1}{\delta}`$ remains open. Empirically, extending to
production-scale LLM-as-a-judge pipelines is an important next step.

### A.1 Error Rate of IVWE

###### Proposition A.1.

Let $`p\in[1,\infty]`$, $`B\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}`$ be a total budget, and
$`\delta\in(0,1)`$ be a target confidence level. Also, let
$`(N_{k,j})_{k,j}`$ be any given allocation. Then, IVWE is
$`(B,\delta,\ell_{p})`$-budget efficient at any
$`({\bm{s}},\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})`$ with the following error rate
$`\varepsilon_{p}`$:

```math
\varepsilon_{p}=\sqrt{\frac{2{\mathcal{A}}_{p}(\bm{\omega};\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\log\frac{2K}{\delta}}{B}}+\frac{R{\mathcal{B}}_{p}(\bm{\omega};\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\log\frac{2K}{\delta}}{3B}, \tag{13}
```

where
$`\bm{\omega}\coloneq\left(\omega_{k,j}\triangleq\frac{c_{j}N_{k,j}}{B}\right)_{k,j}`$,
the allocation objective
$`{\mathcal{A}}_{p}(\bm{\omega};\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})`$ is defined as³³
3 Note that the allocation objective’s limit as $`p\rightarrow\infty`$
is well defined as
$`\max_{k\in[K]}\left(\sum_{j\in[J]}\frac{\omega_{k,j}}{c_{j}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}\right)^{-1}.`$

```math
{\mathcal{A}}_{p}(\bm{\omega};\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\coloneq\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(\sum_{j\in[J]}\frac{\omega_{k,j}}{c_{j}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{p}{2}}\right)^{\frac{2}{p}}, \tag{14}
```

and
$`{\mathcal{B}}_{p}(\bm{\omega};\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\coloneq\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\max_{j:\omega_{k,j}>0}\left(\sigma_{k,j}^{2}\sum_{j^{\prime}\in[J]}\frac{\omega_{k,j^{\prime}}}{c_{j^{\prime}}\sigma_{k,j^{\prime}}^{2}}\right)^{-p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}`$.

###### Proof.

Let $`W_{k}\coloneq\sum_{j\in[J]}\frac{N_{k,j}}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}`$, and
an allocation $`(N_{k,j})_{k,j}`$ be given. Then, first note that

```math
\hat{s}_{k}-s_{k}=W_{k}^{-1}\sum_{j\in[J]}\frac{1}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k,j}}(s_{k,j}^{(i)}-s_{k})=\sum_{j\in[J]}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k,j}}\underbrace{\frac{s_{k,j}^{(i)}-s_{k}}{W_{k}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}}_{\triangleq X_{j}^{(i)}} \tag{15}
```

Note that $`\mathbb{E}[X_{j}^{(i)}]=0`$,
$`|X_{j}^{(i)}|\leq\frac{R}{W_{k}\min_{j:\omega_{k,j}>0}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}`$,
and
$`\mathrm{Var}[X_{j}^{(i)}]=\frac{\mathrm{Var}[s_{k,j}^{(i)}-s_{k}]}{W_{k}^{2}\sigma_{k,j}^{4}}=\frac{1}{W_{k}^{2}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}.`$

We now utilize the Bernstein’s inequality for bounded random variables
(Bernstein, 1927) to the aggregated $`\hat{s}_{k}`$: with probability at
least $`1-\delta`$, the following holds for for any $`k\in[K]`$:

```math
\displaystyle\left|\hat{s}_{k}-s_{k}\right| \displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\left(\sum_{j,i}\frac{1}{W_{k}^{2}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}\right)\log\frac{2K}{\delta}}+\frac{R\log\frac{2K}{\delta}}{3W_{k}\min_{j:\omega_{k,j}>0}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}} \tag{16}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\log\frac{2K}{\delta}}W_{k}^{-\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{R\log\frac{2K}{\delta}}{3}\left(W_{k}\min_{j:\omega_{k,j}>0}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}\right)^{-1}. \tag{17}
```

We then apply Minkowski’s inequality for $`\ell_{p}`$-norm as follows:

```math
\displaystyle\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{p} \tag{18}
```

```math
\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(\sqrt{2\log\frac{2K}{\delta}}W_{k}^{-\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{R\log\frac{2K}{\delta}}{3}\left(W_{k}\min_{j:\omega_{k,j}>0}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}\right)^{-1}\right)^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}} \tag{19}
```

```math
\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(\sqrt{2\log\frac{2K}{\delta}}W_{k}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}+\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(\frac{R\log\frac{2K}{\delta}}{3}\left(W_{k}\min_{j:\omega_{k,j}>0}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}\right)^{-1}\right)^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\log\frac{2K}{\delta}}\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}W_{k}^{-\frac{p}{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}+\frac{R\log\frac{2K}{\delta}}{3}\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(W_{k}\min_{j:\omega_{k,j}>0}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}\right)^{-p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}} \tag{20}
```

```math
\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{B}\log\frac{2K}{\delta}}\underbrace{\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{\omega_{k,j}}{c_{j}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{p}{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}}_{\triangleq\sqrt{{\mathcal{A}}_{p}(\bm{\omega};\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})}}+\frac{R\log\frac{2K}{\delta}}{3B}\underbrace{\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\max_{j:\omega_{k,j}>0}\left(\sigma_{k,j}^{2}\sum_{j^{\prime}\in[J]}\frac{\omega_{k,j^{\prime}}}{c_{j^{\prime}}\sigma_{k,j^{\prime}}^{2}}\right)^{-p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}}_{\triangleq{\mathcal{B}}_{p}(\bm{\omega};\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})}, \tag{21}
```

where in the last inequality, we reparametrize as a ratio of budget
allocated to $`(k,j)`$-pair,
$`\omega_{k,j}\coloneq\frac{c_{j}N_{k,j}}{B}`$, which is invariant to
the amount of budget $`B`$. ∎

### A.2 Proof of Theorem 3.2: Optimal Allocation

For notational simplicity, let

```math
g_{k}\coloneq c_{j^{*}(k)}\sigma_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}=\min_{j\in[J]}c_{j}\sigma_{k,j}^{2},\qquad b_{k}\coloneq\sum_{j\in[J]}\omega_{k,j}. \tag{22}
```

Here, $`b_{k}`$ is the fraction of the budget allocated to query $`k`$.
Since $`g_{k}>0`$, any allocation with finite objective must have
$`b_{k}>0`$ for every $`k`$. Increasing all allocations by a common
factor decreases the objective, so the budget is fully used at an
optimum: $`\sum_{k}b_{k}=1`$.

For a fixed budget $`b_{k}`$ assigned to query $`k`$, we have

```math
\sum_{j\in[J]}\frac{\omega_{k,j}}{c_{j}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}\leq\frac{b_{k}}{g_{k}}. \tag{23}
```

Equality holds when the budget is assigned only to judges minimizing
$`c_{j}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}`$. In particular, we may assign all of $`b_{k}`$
to a single optimal judge $`j^{*}(k)`$; if several judges attain the
minimum, splitting the budget among them also gives equality. It remains
to optimize the budget fractions across queries.⁴⁴ 4 The KKT analysis
yields the same results but is more complicated.

#### Optimal Allocation minimizing $`{\mathcal{A}}_{\infty}`$

Using the preceding comparison and $`\sum_{k}b_{k}=1`$, we obtain

```math
{\mathcal{A}}_{\infty}(\bm{\omega};\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\geq\max_{k\in[K]}\frac{g_{k}}{b_{k}}\geq\sum_{k\in[K]}b_{k}\frac{g_{k}}{b_{k}}=\sum_{k\in[K]}g_{k}. \tag{24}
```

Equality is attained by setting
$`b_{k}=g_{k}/\sum_{k^{\prime}}g_{k^{\prime}}`$ and assigning each
query’s budget to an optimal judge. Thus,

```math
\omega_{k,j}^{*}=\mathds{1}[j=j^{*}(k)]\frac{g_{k}}{\sum_{k^{\prime}}g_{k^{\prime}}},\qquad{\mathcal{A}}_{\infty}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})=\sum_{k}g_{k}. \tag{25}
```

#### Optimal Allocation minimizing $`{\mathcal{A}}_{p}`$

For $`1\leq p<\infty`$, the same comparison gives

```math
{\mathcal{A}}_{p}(\bm{\omega};\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\geq\left(\sum_{k}\left(\frac{g_{k}}{b_{k}}\right)^{p/2}\right)^{2/p}. \tag{26}
```

By Hölder’s inequality with conjugate exponents $`(p+2)/2`$ and
$`(p+2)/p`$,

```math
\displaystyle\sum_{k}g_{k}^{\frac{p}{p+2}}=\sum_{k}\left[\left(\frac{g_{k}}{b_{k}}\right)^{p/2}\right]^{\frac{2}{p+2}}b_{k}^{\frac{p}{p+2}}\leq\left(\sum_{k}\left(\frac{g_{k}}{b_{k}}\right)^{p/2}\right)^{\frac{2}{p+2}}\left(\sum_{k}b_{k}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+2}}\leq{\mathcal{A}}_{p}(\bm{\omega};\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})^{\frac{p}{p+2}}. \tag{27}
```

Rearranging yields

```math
{\mathcal{A}}_{p}(\bm{\omega};\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\geq\left(\sum_{k}g_{k}^{\frac{p}{p+2}}\right)^{\frac{p+2}{p}}. \tag{28}
```

Equality in Hölder’s inequality holds when
$`b_{k}=g_{k}^{p/(p+2)}/\sum_{k^{\prime}}g_{k^{\prime}}^{p/(p+2)}`$.
Combining this with the optimal judge choice gives

```math
\omega_{k,j}^{*}=\mathds{1}[j=j^{*}(k)]\frac{g_{k}^{\frac{p}{p+2}}}{\sum_{k^{\prime}}g_{k^{\prime}}^{\frac{p}{p+2}}},\qquad{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})=\left(\sum_{k}g_{k}^{\frac{p}{p+2}}\right)^{\frac{p+2}{p}}. \tag{29}
```

This recovers Theorem 3.2, with the convention $`p/(p+2)=(p+2)/p=1`$
when $`p=\infty`$. ∎

### B.1 Dealing with Variance Mismatch during Aggregation

To show the effect of $`N_{0}`$ and $`\tau`$, we will tune them as we go
along. Let $`\overline{\sigma}_{k,j}\coloneq\tau+\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}`$.
Then the algorithm computes the allocation
$`\widehat{\omega}_{k,j}^{*}`$ as

```math
\widehat{\omega}_{k,j}^{*}=\frac{\mathds{1}[j=\hat{j}^{*}(k)]\left(c_{\hat{j}^{*}(k)}\overline{\sigma}_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+2}}}{\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(c_{\hat{j}^{*}(k)}\overline{\sigma}_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+2}}}, \tag{30}
```

where
$`\hat{j}^{*}(k)\coloneq\argmin_{j\in[J]}c_{j}\overline{\sigma}_{k,j}^{2}`$,
and allocates each query $`k`$ to $`\hat{j}^{*}(k)`$ for
$`\widehat{N}_{k}\coloneq\left\lceil\frac{B^{\prime}\widehat{\omega}_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{*}}{c_{\hat{j}^{*}(k)}}\right\rceil`$
times. Here, $`B^{\prime}=B-CN_{0}-Kc_{\max}`$ with
$`C:=K\sum_{j}c_{j}`$. Since the budget allocated to Phase-II is at most
$`B^{\prime}+\sum_{k}c_{\hat{j}^{*}(k)}\leq B-CN_{0}`$, the total budget
constraint is satisfied under this allocation. Conditional on Phase I,
the allocation is fixed and Phase-II samples are independent and fresh.
All expressions for $`p=\infty`$ are interpreted by taking limits.

We first follow the proof of Proposition A.1 while accounting for the
variance mismatch: the empirical variance used in Est-IVWE does not
match the true variance. Applying the Bernstein’s inequality to
$`\hat{s}_{k}-s_{k}=\frac{1}{\widehat{N}_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{\widehat{N}_{k}}\left(s_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{(i)}-s_{k}\right)`$,
the following holds with probability at least $`1-\delta`$: for all
$`k\in[K]`$,

```math
|\hat{s}_{k}-s_{k}|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{2}\log\frac{2K}{\delta}}{\widehat{N}_{k}}}+\frac{2R\log\frac{2K}{\delta}}{3\widehat{N}_{k}}. \tag{31}
```

By Minkowski’s inequality, we then have

```math
\displaystyle\varepsilon_{p} \displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\log\frac{2K}{\delta}}\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(\frac{\widehat{N}_{k}}{\sigma_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{p}{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}+\frac{2R\log\frac{2K}{\delta}}{3}\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\widehat{N}_{k}^{-p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}} \tag{32}
```

```math
\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\log\frac{2K}{\delta}}{B^{\prime}}}\underbrace{\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(\frac{\widehat{\omega}_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{*}}{c_{\hat{j}^{*}(k)}\sigma_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{p}{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}}_{(a)}+\frac{2R\log\frac{2K}{\delta}}{3B^{\prime}}\underbrace{\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(\frac{\widehat{\omega}_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{*}}{c_{\hat{j}^{*}(k)}}\right)^{-p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}}_{(b)}. \tag{33}
```

We bound each $`(a)`$ and $`(b)`$ separately. We utilize the useful
confidence bound on variance estimates given by Maurer and Pontil (2009,
Theorem 10). By applying a union bound across all query-judge pairs, we
obtain the following result based on the empirical Bernstein inequality:

###### Lemma B.1 (Theorem 10 of Maurer and Pontil (2009)).

With $`\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}^{2}`$ computed in Phase I of Algorithm 1,

```math
{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left|\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}-\sigma_{k,j}\right|\leq R\sqrt{\frac{2\log\frac{2KJ}{\delta}}{N_{0}-1}},\quad\forall(k,j)\in[K]\times[J]\right)\geq 1-\delta. \tag{34}
```

We then derive the following lemma that sandwiches our optimistic biased
estimates $`\overline{\sigma}_{k,j}`$’s with the true
$`\sigma_{k,j}`$’s, up to $`\tau`$: We then derive the following lemma
that sandwiches our optimistic biased estimates
$`\overline{\sigma}_{k,j}`$’s with the true $`\sigma_{k,j}`$’s, up to
$`\tau`$:

###### Lemma B.2.

Suppose that
$`\tau\geq R\sqrt{\frac{2}{N_{0}-1}\log\frac{2KJ}{\delta}}`$. Then,

```math
{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sigma_{k,j}\leq\overline{\sigma}_{k,j}\leq\sigma_{k,j}+2\tau,\ \forall(k,j)\in[K]\times[J]\right)\geq 1-\delta. \tag{35}
```

###### Proof.

By Lemma B.1 and the bound on $`\tau`$, with probability at least
$`1-\delta`$ we have $`|\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}-\sigma_{k,j}|\leq\tau`$
simultaneously for all pairs. On this event,

```math
\sigma_{k,j}\leq\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}+\tau=\overline{\sigma}_{k,j}\leq\sigma_{k,j}+2\tau, \tag{36}

which proves the claim. ∎

From the above lemma, we choose
$`\tau=R\sqrt{\frac{2}{N_{0}-1}\log\frac{2KJ}{\delta}}`$ before
rescaling $`\delta`$ to $`\delta/2`$ below.

```

```math
\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(c_{\hat{j}^{*}(k)}\overline{\sigma}_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+2}}\right)\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(\frac{c_{\hat{j}^{*}(k)}}{\overline{\sigma}_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{p}}\right)^{\frac{2p}{p+2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}} \tag{48}
```

```math
\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(c_{j^{*}(k)}\overline{\sigma}_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+2}}\right)\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(\frac{c_{j^{*}(k)}\overline{\sigma}_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}}{\overline{\sigma}_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{p+2}}\right)^{\frac{2p}{p+2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}} \hat{j}^{*}(k)
```

```math
\displaystyle\leq\tau^{-2}\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(c_{j^{*}(k)}\overline{\sigma}_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+2}}\right)\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(c_{j^{*}(k)}\overline{\sigma}_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{2p}{p+2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}} \overline{\sigma}_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}\geq\tau
```

```math
\displaystyle\leq\tau^{-2}\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(c_{j^{*}(k)}\overline{\sigma}_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+2}}\right)\left(\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(c_{j^{*}(k)}\overline{\sigma}_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+2}}\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}} \sum_{k}y_{k}^{2}\leq(\sum_{k}y_{k})^{2} y_{k}\geq 0
```

```math
\displaystyle=\left\{\tau^{-1}\underbrace{\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(c_{j^{*}(k)}\overline{\sigma}_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+2}}\right)^{\frac{p+2}{2p}}}_{\text{same as Eqn.\penalty\ \eqref{eqn:empirical-A}!}}\right\}^{2}. \tag{49}
```

Thus, borrowing our computations from bounding $`(a)`$ and using
$`(a+b+c)^{2}\leq 3(a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2})`$, we have that

```math
(b)\leq 3\left({\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\tau^{-2}+(C_{p,1}^{*})^{2}+\mathds{1}[1\leq p<2](C_{p,2}^{*})^{2}\tau^{\frac{2p-4}{p+2}}\right). \tag{50}
```

### B.4 Combining Everything and the Large-Budget Regime

We now combine the preceding bounds and specify the choice of $`N_{0}`$.
Recall that $`C=K\sum_{j}c_{j}`$ and

```math
\tau=R\sqrt{\frac{2}{N_{0}-1}\log\frac{4KJ}{\delta}}, \tag{51}
```

where we have rescaled $`\delta`$ to $`\delta/2`$. With this rescaling,
the variance-concentration in Phase I fails with probability at most
$`\delta/2`$, and, conditional on Phase I, the estimation concentration
in Phase II fails with probability at most $`\delta/2`$. Thus, by a
union bound, both events hold simultaneously with probability at least
$`1-\delta`$.

Suppose that

```math
B\geq 2\left(CN_{0}+K\max_{j\in[J]}c_{j}\right), \tag{52}
```

then we have $`B^{\prime}\geq B/2`$. Combining the bounds on $`(a)`$ and
$`(b)`$ gives, with probability at least $`1-\delta`$,

```math
\displaystyle\varepsilon_{p}\leq{} \displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{B^{\prime}}}\left(\sqrt{{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})}+C_{p,1}^{*}\tau+\mathds{1}[1\leq p<2]C_{p,2}^{*}\tau^{q}\right)
```

```math
\displaystyle+\frac{2R\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{B^{\prime}}\left({\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\tau^{-2}+(C_{p,1}^{*})^{2}+\mathds{1}[1\leq p<2](C_{p,2}^{*})^{2}\tau^{2q-2}\right), \tag{53}
```

where $`q=\frac{2p}{p+2}`$, with $`q=2`$ when $`p=\infty`$.

To separate the oracle term from the cost of learning the variances,
note that by Lemma B.3 and (52),

```math
\displaystyle\varepsilon_{p}\leq{} \displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{B}}
```

```math
\displaystyle+\frac{\left(CN_{0}+K\max_{j\in[J]}c_{j}\right)\sqrt{2{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}}{B^{3/2}}
```

```math
\displaystyle+2\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{B}}\left(C_{p,1}^{*}\tau+\mathds{1}[1\leq p<2]C_{p,2}^{*}\tau^{q}\right)
```

```math
\displaystyle+\frac{4R\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{B}\left({\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\tau^{-2}+(C_{p,1}^{*})^{2}+\mathds{1}[1\leq p<2](C_{p,2}^{*})^{2}\tau^{2q-2}\right). \tag{54}
```

The first term is exactly the leading oracle error rate. The remaining
terms capture the loss from the initial exploration, the error in
estimating the variances, and the lower-order Bernstein term. We now
choose $`N_{0}`$ so that these terms decay strictly faster than
$`B^{-1/2}`$.

#### Case I: $`p\geq 2`$.

In this case, the terms involving $`C_{p,2}^{*}`$ vanish. Among the
lower-order terms in Eqn. (54), the two dominant terms depending on
$`N_{0}`$ are

```math
2C_{p,1}^{*}\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{B}}\,\tau=O\left(\frac{R\log\frac{4KJ}{\delta}}{\sqrt{BN_{0}}}\right), \tag{55}
```

where we used $`\log(4K/\delta)\leq\log(4KJ/\delta)`$, and

```math
\frac{4R\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{B}{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\tau^{-2}=O\left(\frac{{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})N_{0}}{RB}\right). \tag{56}
```

Since
$`N_{0}=2\vee\left\lceil(2B)^{1/3}\left(R^{2}\log\frac{4KJ}{\delta}\right)^{2/3}\right\rceil`$,
we have $`N_{0}=\Theta(B^{1/3})`$, and $`\tau=\Theta(B^{-1/6})`$
ignoring factors independent of $`B`$. Thus, the two terms above are
both of order $`B^{-2/3}`$. The remaining lower-order terms decay faster
as

```math
\frac{CN_{0}+K\max_{j}c_{j}}{B^{3/2}}=O(B^{-7/6}) \tag{57}
```

up to fixed instance-dependent factors. Therefore, every term after the
leading oracle term in Eqn. (54) is of order at most $`B^{-2/3}`$. More
precisely, using $`\log(4K/\delta)\leq\log(4KJ/\delta)`$, we finally
obtain

```math
\varepsilon_{p}=\sqrt{\frac{2{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{B}}+{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{R^{1/3}\mathcal{A}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\left(\log\frac{4KJ}{\delta}\right)^{2/3}}{B^{2/3}}\right). \tag{58}
```

#### Case II: $`1\leq p<2`$.

Recall that $`q=\frac{2p}{p+2}<1`$. Among the lower-order terms in Eqn.
(54), the two dominant terms depending on $`N_{0}`$ are

```math
2C_{p,2}^{*}\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{B}}\,\tau^{q}=O\left(\frac{R^{q}\left(\log\frac{4KJ}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{q+1}{2}}}{\sqrt{B}N_{0}^{q/2}}\right), \tag{59}
```

where we used $`\log(4K/\delta)\leq\log(4KJ/\delta)`$, and

```math
\frac{4R\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{B}{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\tau^{-2}=O\left(\frac{{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})N_{0}}{RB}\right). \tag{60}
```

The choice of
$`N_{0}=2\vee\left\lceil 2^{\frac{p}{2p+2}}R^{\frac{3p+2}{2p+2}}\left(\log\frac{4KJ}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{3p+2}{4p+4}}B^{\frac{p+2}{4p+4}}\right\rceil`$
balances the orders of these two terms to the order of
$`B^{-\frac{3p+2}{4p+4}}`$. The remaining lower-order terms in Eqn. (54)
decay strictly faster:

```math
B^{-1/2}\tau=O\left(B^{-\frac{5p+6}{8p+8}}\right),\qquad\frac{\tau^{2q-2}}{B}=O\left(B^{-\frac{5p+2}{4p+4}}\right), \tag{61}
```

and

```math
\frac{CN_{0}+K\max_{j}c_{j}}{B^{3/2}}=O\left(B^{-\frac{5p+4}{4p+4}}\right),\qquad\frac{1}{B}=O(B^{-1}). \tag{62}
```

Therefore, every term after the leading oracle term is of order at most
$`B^{-\frac{3p+2}{4p+4}}`$. Finally, we obtain

```math
\varepsilon_{p}=\sqrt{\frac{2{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{B}}+{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{R^{\frac{p}{2p+2}}\mathcal{A}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\left(\log\frac{4KJ}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{3p+2}{4p+4}}}{B^{\frac{3p+2}{4p+4}}}\right). \tag{63}
```

We choose

```math
N_{0}=\max\left\{2,\,\left\lceil 2^{\frac{p}{2p+2}}R^{\frac{3p+2}{2p+2}}\left(\log\frac{4KJ}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{3p+2}{4p+4}}B^{\frac{p+2}{4p+4}}\right\rceil\right\}. \tag{64}
```

Here the dominant variance-estimation term is $`B^{-1/2}\tau^{q}`$,
which scales as $`B^{-1/2}N_{0}^{-q/2}`$. Balancing this term with the
exploration loss $`N_{0}/B`$ gives the above choice of $`N_{0}`$. It
follows that

```math
N_{0}=\Theta\left(B^{\frac{p+2}{4p+4}}\right),\qquad\tau=\Theta\left(B^{-\frac{p+2}{8p+8}}\right), \tag{65}
```

and every remainder term in (54) is of order at most
$`B^{-\frac{3p+2}{4p+4}}`$. Therefore,

```math
\varepsilon_{p}=\sqrt{\frac{2{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{B}}+{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{R^{\frac{p}{2p+2}}\mathcal{A}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\left(\log\frac{4KJ}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{3p+2}{4p+4}}}{B^{\frac{3p+2}{4p+4}}}\right). \tag{66}
```

For either choice of $`N_{0}`$, we have $`N_{0}=o(B)`$; hence the
condition (52) is satisfied for all sufficiently large $`B`$. For
$`p\geq 2`$, the remainder is $`O(B^{-2/3})`$, while for $`1\leq p<2`$
it is $`O(B^{-(3p+2)/(4p+4)})`$. Both decay strictly faster than the
$`B^{-1/2}`$ oracle term. Therefore, for every fixed instance with
$`{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})>0`$, the ratio of the
remainder to the leading oracle term vanishes as $`B\to\infty`$.
Consequently, there exists a finite instance-dependent budget $`B_{0}`$
beyond which the oracle term dominates the remainder.

Thus, beyond a finite instance-dependent budget $`B_{0}`$, the oracle
term dominates the remainder, yielding the leading-order
instance-optimality of Theorem 4.1. This threshold is neither needed by
the learner nor used to set $`N_{0}`$, and need not be uniform over
variance profiles: it may become large as
$`{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})`$ approaches zero. ∎

We conclude with the proof of the simple algebraic inequality that we
have utilized:

###### Lemma B.3.

For $`x\in[0,1/2]`$, it holds that $`(1-x)^{-1/2}\leq 1+x`$.

###### Proof.

Let’s define a function $`f(x)=1+x-(1-x)^{-1/2}`$ on $`(-\infty,1)`$,
then $`f(0)=0`$ and $`f(0.5)>0`$. Its derivatives are

```math
f^{\prime}(x)=1-\frac{1}{2}(1-x)^{-3/2}\quad\text{and}\quad f^{\prime\prime}(x)=-\frac{3}{4}(1-x)^{-5/2}, \tag{67}
```

which imply that $`f`$ is concave, increasing at $`x=0`$
($`f^{\prime}(0)>0`$), and decreasing at $`x=0.5`$
($`f^{\prime}(0.5)`$). Considering the shape of the function $`f`$,
which has a single mode (maximum) in $`(0,1/2)`$, $`f(0)=0`$ and
$`f(0.5)>0`$ imply that $`f>0`$ on $`[0,1/2]`$. ∎

## Appendix C Proof of Theorem 5.1: Fano-Type High-Probability Lower Bound

Our Fano-type change-of-measure argument relies on the binary KL data
processing inequality (Garivier et al., 2019), as utilized in Jedra and
Proutière (2023) to derive a $`\log\frac{1}{\delta}`$-dependent minimax
lower bound.⁵⁵ 5 This is somewhat reminiscent of Birgé’s refinement of
Fano’s inequality (Tsybakov, 2009; Gerchinovitz et al., 2020). The proof
“bifurcates” at whether $`1\leq p<2`$ or $`p\geq 2`$. For the former, we
construct a pair of dense perturbations, while for the latter, we
construct a pair of 1-sparse perturbations for each query and combine
their information constraints using the budget.

Throughout, we assume that the learner knows the given cost vector
$`{\bm{c}}`$ and the true variance profile $`\bm{\sigma}`$ with
$`\max_{j}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}\leq R_{k}^{2}/2\text{ for every }k\in[K]`$.

#### Construction of Alternate Models.

Let $`{\mathcal{A}}`$ be any $`(B,\delta,\ell_{p})`$-budget efficient
algorithm (Definition 2.1) with error rate $`\varepsilon_{p}`$,
_potentially adaptive_. $`{\mathcal{A}}`$ interacts with the true
environment $`{\bm{s}}^{\star}`$ sequentially⁶⁶ 6 Even though the
algorithm is batched, one could view it as sequentially observing i.i.d.
samples. Note that this includes adaptive algorithms. as follows at each
timestep $`t`$, when the learner selects query $`k_{t}`$ and judge
$`j_{t}`$, they observe a score $`\tilde{s}_{t}`$. Let us denote
$`N_{k,j}(T)\coloneq\sum_{t}\mathds{1}[k_{t}=k,j_{t}=j]`$ as the number
of times $`{\mathcal{A}}`$ queries the pair $`(k,j)`$ throughout the
interaction. We note that the knapsack constraint is in place as
follows:

```math
\sum_{j}c_{j}\sum_{k}N_{k,j}(T)\leq B. \tag{68}
```

Let $`({\bm{s}}^{\star},\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})`$ be given and fixed, as
we aim for an instance-wise lower bound. The following proposition,
whose proof is deferred to Appendix E, provides the desired construction
of hypotheses while satisfying the necessary properties:

###### Proposition C.1.

There exists a family of probability measures
$`{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}+\bm{\Delta}}=\left({\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}+\bm{\Delta},k,j}\right)_{k,j}`$
indexed by perturbation $`\bm{\Delta}=(\Delta_{k})_{k\in[K]}`$ with
$`|\Delta_{k}|\leq\frac{R_{k}}{4}`$ that satisfies the following: for
each $`(k,j)\in[K]\times[J],`$

1.  $`(i)`$
    $`\mathbb{E}[X]=s_{k}^{\star}+\Delta_{k}`$ and
    $`\mathrm{Var}[X]=\sigma_{k,j}^{2}`$ for
    $`X\sim{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}+\bm{\Delta},k,j}`$, and
    $`\mathrm{supp}({\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}+\bm{\Delta},k,j})\subseteq[0,R]`$,
2.  $`(ii)`$
    $`D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star},k,j},{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}+\bm{\Delta},k,j}\right)\vee D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}+\bm{\Delta},k,j},{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star},k,j}\right)\leq C_{1}\frac{\Delta_{k}^{2}}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}`$,
    and
3.  $`(iii)`$
    $`D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}+\bm{\Delta},k,j},{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}-\bm{\Delta},k,j}\right)\vee D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}-\bm{\Delta},k,j},{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}+\bm{\Delta},k,j}\right)\leq C_{2}\frac{\Delta_{k}^{2}}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}`$.

where $`C_{1},C_{2}>0`$ are absolute constants.

The construction is rather tedious as it is based on appropriate family
of beta distributions (which ensures boundedness) while making sure that
the variance remains invariant across perturbations, and we need to make
sure that the KL-divergence is quadratically bounded as above. (Without
the boundedness assumption, one can simply utilize location family of
Gaussian distributions.) For this high-probability lower bound proof, we
will primarily utilize $`(i)`$ and $`(ii)`$, and later for the
in-expectation lower bound, we will utilize $`(ii)`$ and $`(iii)`$.

We consider the canonical bandit model (See Lattimore and Szepesvári
(2020, Section 4.6)) over the observations, and let
$`{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}}`$ and
$`{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}\triangleq{\bm{s}}^{\star}+\bm{\Delta}}`$ be the
two probability measures induced by the true and alternate instance,
respectively. The adversary’s choice of perturbation $`\bm{\Delta}`$
will be optimized for later.

#### KL-Divergence Decomposition.

By the Divergence Decomposition Lemma (Lattimore and Szepesvári, 2020,
Lemma 15.1), the KL divergence between the probability measures
$`{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}}`$ and $`{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}}`$ is
given by

```math
\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}({\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}},{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}}) \displaystyle=\sum_{k\in[K]}\sum_{j\in[J]}\mathbb{E}^{\mathcal{A}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}}[N_{k,j}(T)]D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star},k,j},{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}},k,j}\right) \tag{69}
```

```math
\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\sum_{k\in[K]}\underbrace{\sum_{j\in[J]}\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\mathcal{A}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}}[N_{k,j}(T)]}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}}_{\triangleq w_{k}}(s_{k}-s_{k}^{\star})^{2}. (ii)
```

We first minimize the RHS over perturbations with
$`\left\lVert{\bm{s}}-{\bm{s}}^{\star}\right\rVert_{p}\geq 2\varepsilon_{p}`$.
The minimizers below lie on the boundary and satisfy
$`|s_{k}-s_{k}^{\star}|\leq 2\varepsilon_{p}\leq R_{k}/4`$, so both
signs of each perturbation are valid. This is characterized in the next
lemma, whose proof is deferred to Section C.1:

###### Lemma C.2.

Let $`w_{k}>0`$ and $`{\bm{s}}^{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}`$ be fixed.
Let $`{\bm{s}}=(s_{k})_{k\in[K]}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}`$ be an optimal
solution of the following constrained optimization:

```math
V^{\star}=\min_{{\bm{s}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}}\sum_{k}w_{k}(s_{k}-s_{k}^{\star})^{2},\text{ subj. to }\left\lVert{\bm{s}}-{\bm{s}}^{\star}\right\rVert_{p}\geq 2\varepsilon_{p}. \tag{70}
```

Then,

1.  1.  Case I. $`1\leq p<2`$:

    ```math
    s_{k}=s_{k}^{\star}+2\varepsilon_{p}\frac{w_{k}^{-\frac{1}{2-p}}}{\left(\sum_{k}w_{k}^{-\frac{p}{2-p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}},\quad V^{\star}=\frac{4\varepsilon_{p}^{2}}{\left(\sum_{k}w_{k}^{-\frac{p}{2-p}}\right)^{\frac{2-p}{p}}}. \tag{71}
    ```

2.  2.  Case II. $`p\geq 2`$: denoting $`k^{\star}\in\argmin_{k}w_{k}`$,

    ```math
    {\bm{s}}={\bm{s}}^{\star}+2\varepsilon_{p}{\bm{e}}_{k^{\star}},\quad V^{\star}=4\varepsilon_{p}^{2}\min_{k}w_{k}. \tag{72}
    ```

In both cases, note that as
$`\varepsilon_{p}\leq\frac{\min_{k}R_{k}}{8}`$ (as given in the theorem
statement), $`{\bm{s}}`$ satisfies $`{\bm{s}}\in[0,R]^{K}`$ and leads to
a valid probability distribution.

#### Optimal Hard Instances.

Now the idea is to construct a set of optimal hard instances and utilize
the Bernoulli KL-variant of the data processing inequality (Garivier et
al., 2019, Lemma 1), which we recall here:

###### Lemma C.3 (Data Processing Inequality; Lemma 1 of Garivier et al. (2019)).

Consider a measurable space $`(\Gamma,{\mathcal{G}})`$ equipped with two
probability measure $`{\mathbb{P}}_{1}`$ and $`{\mathbb{P}}_{2}`$. Then,
we have that

```math
D_{\mathrm{KL}}({\mathbb{P}}_{1},{\mathbb{P}}_{2})\geq\sup_{Z}\mathrm{kl}(\mathbb{E}_{1}[Z],\mathbb{E}_{2}[Z]), \tag{73}
```

where $`\sup_{Z}`$ is over all possible $`{\mathcal{G}}`$-measurable
random variable $`Z:\Omega\rightarrow[0,1]`$.

First, let $`\bm{\Delta}`$ satisfy
$`\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}\right\rVert_{p}=2\varepsilon_{p}`$ and consider
the two instances $`{\bm{s}}^{\pm}:={\bm{s}}^{\star}\pm\bm{\Delta}`$.
Both lie in the prescribed neighborhood and satisfy
$`|\Delta_{k}|\leq 2\varepsilon_{p}\leq R_{k}/4`$. Define their success
events by

```math
{\mathcal{E}}_{\pm}:=\{\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}^{\pm}\right\rVert_{p}\leq\varepsilon_{p}\}. \tag{74}
```

These events are disjoint, since
$`\left\lVert{\bm{s}}^{+}-{\bm{s}}^{-}\right\rVert_{p}=4\varepsilon_{p}>2\varepsilon_{p}`$.
Consequently, for at least one sign $`\eta\in\{+,-\}`$,

```math
{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}}({\mathcal{E}}_{\eta})\leq\tfrac{1}{2},\qquad{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\eta}}({\mathcal{E}}_{\eta})\geq 1-\delta. \tag{75}
```

The data processing inequality and monotonicity of binary KL give

```math
C_{1}\sum_{k}w_{k}\Delta_{k}^{2}\geq D_{\mathrm{KL}}({\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}},{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\eta}})\geq\mathrm{kl}\left(\frac{1}{2},1-\delta\right). \tag{76}
```

For $`\delta\leq 1/16`$, we have

```math
\mathrm{kl}\left(\frac{1}{2},1-\delta\right)=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1}{4\delta(1-\delta)}\geq\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1}{\delta}-\log 2\geq\frac{1}{4}\log\frac{1}{\delta}. \tag{77}
```

If any $`w_{k}=0`$, applying Eqn. (76) to
$`\bm{\Delta}=2\varepsilon_{p}{\bm{e}}_{k}`$ gives a contradiction.
Hence every budget-efficient algorithm considered here has $`w_{k}>0`$.

#### Case I. $`1\leq p<2`$.

Choose $`\bm{\Delta}`$ from Case I of Lemma C.2. Applying Eqn. (76) to
the corresponding pair of instances yields

```math
\frac{4C_{1}\varepsilon_{p}^{2}}{\left(\sum_{k}w_{k}^{-\frac{p}{2-p}}\right)^{\frac{2-p}{p}}}\geq\frac{1}{4}\log\frac{1}{\delta}. \tag{78}
```

Relaxing the expected counts to all nonnegative allocations satisfying
the budget, we obtain

```math
\displaystyle\varepsilon_{p}^{2} \displaystyle\geq\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{16C_{1}}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}N_{k,j}\geq 0\\
\sum_{k,j}c_{j}N_{k,j}\leq B\end{subarray}}\left(\sum_{k}\left(\sum_{j}\frac{N_{k,j}}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{p}{2-p}}\right)^{\frac{2-p}{p}}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{16C_{1}B}\underbrace{\left(\sum_{k}(c_{j^{*}(k)}\sigma_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2})^{\frac{p}{2}}\right)^{\frac{2}{p}}}_{={\mathcal{A}}_{\frac{2p}{2-p}}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})}, \tag{79}
```

where the last step follows from the allocation optimization in
Section A.2.

#### Case II. $`p\geq 2`$.

For each $`k`$, apply Eqn. (76) to the pair
$`{\bm{s}}^{\star}\pm 2\varepsilon_{p}{\bm{e}}_{k}`$. This gives

```math
4C_{1}\varepsilon_{p}^{2}w_{k}\geq\frac{1}{4}\log\frac{1}{\delta},\qquad\forall k\in[K]. \tag{80}
```

By the budget constraint and the definition of $`j^{*}(k)`$,

```math
\displaystyle\sum_{k}c_{j^{*}(k)}\sigma_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}w_{k} \displaystyle=\sum_{k,j}\mathbb{E}^{\mathcal{A}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}}[N_{k,j}(T)]\frac{c_{j^{*}(k)}\sigma_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}
```

```math
\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k,j}c_{j}\mathbb{E}^{\mathcal{A}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}}[N_{k,j}(T)]\leq B. \tag{81}
```

Multiplying the coordinatewise information constraints by
$`c_{j^{*}(k)}\sigma_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}`$ and summing therefore yields

```math
\varepsilon_{p}^{2}\geq\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{16C_{1}B}\sum_{k}c_{j^{*}(k)}\sigma_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}=\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{16C_{1}B}{\mathcal{A}}_{\infty}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}}). \tag{82}
```

This argument also covers $`p=\infty`$; it requires disjointness only
between the two opposite perturbations of a given coordinate. Absorbing
numerical factors into the universal constants proves the theorem. ∎

###### Remark 1 (Why $`{\mathcal{A}}_{\infty}^{*}`$ appears for $`p\geq 2`$).

For $`p\geq 2`$, each alternative used above perturbs only one
coordinate. The resulting constraints give

```math
\varepsilon_{p}^{2}\geq\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{32C_{1}\min_{k}w_{k}}. \tag{83}
```

Thus, minimizing this lower-bound expression over feasible allocations
amounts to maximizing $`\min_{k}w_{k}`$. Let
$`a_{k}\coloneq c_{j^{*}(k)}\sigma_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}`$. Since the budget
implies $`\sum_{k}a_{k}w_{k}\leq B`$, the associated continuous
relaxation satisfies

```math
\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{k}\geq 0\ \forall k\\
\sum_{k}a_{k}w_{k}\leq B\end{subarray}}\min_{k}w_{k}=\frac{B}{\sum_{k}a_{k}}. \tag{84}
```

Indeed, $`\min_{k}w_{k}\leq B/\sum_{k}a_{k}`$, with equality when all
$`w_{k}`$ are equal. This allocation is realized in the continuous
relaxation by assigning query $`k`$ the budget fraction
$`a_{k}/\sum_{\ell}a_{\ell}`$ and using only a judge minimizing
$`c_{j}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}`$. Substitution yields the factor
$`\sum_{k}a_{k}={\mathcal{A}}_{\infty}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})`$,
explaining why this factor appears for every $`p\geq 2`$.

The equalizing allocation minimizes the lower-bound expression obtained
from these single-coordinate tests. The tests provide necessary
conditions only, so they do not establish the optimal allocation for
finite-$`p`$ estimation. In particular, the allocation minimizing the
aggregate objective $`{\mathcal{A}}_{p}`$ in Theorem 3.2 assigns query
budgets proportional to $`a_{k}^{p/(p+2)}`$ for finite $`p`$.

### C.1 Proof of Lemma C.2: Optimal Hard Instance

For notational simplicity, let
$`{\bm{x}}\coloneq{\bm{s}}-{\bm{s}}^{\star}`$. By the symmetry of the
objective function and the constraint, we can assume without loss of
generality that $`x_{k}\geq 0`$ for all $`k\in[K]`$. For $`p=\infty`$,
the constraint requires $`\max_{k}|x_{k}|\geq 2\varepsilon_{p}`$, so the
minimum is $`4\varepsilon_{p}^{2}\min_{k}w_{k}`$, attained at
$`{\bm{x}}=2\varepsilon_{p}{\bm{e}}_{k^{\star}}`$ for
$`k^{\star}\in\argmin_{k}w_{k}`$. Henceforth assume $`p<\infty`$. Then
our optimization becomes:

```math
V^{\star}=\min_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}^{K}}\left\{F({\bm{x}})\triangleq\sum_{k}w_{k}x_{k}^{2}\right\},\text{ subj. to }\ \sum_{k}x_{k}^{p}\geq(2\varepsilon_{p})^{p}. \tag{85}
```

Scaling any feasible $`{\bm{x}}`$ with
$`\left\lVert{\bm{x}}\right\rVert_{p}>2\varepsilon_{p}`$ down to the
boundary strictly decreases $`F({\bm{x}})`$. Thus, the minimum is
attained where $`\sum_{k}x_{k}^{p}=(2\varepsilon_{p})^{p}`$.

We introduce a (bijective) change of variables $`y_{k}=x_{k}^{p}`$ for
$`y_{k}\geq 0`$, which maps the non-convex $`L_{p}`$ exterior into a
standard linear simplex (a convex set). Thus, our optimization becomes:

```math
V^{\star}=\min_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}^{K}}\left\{\tilde{F}({\bm{y}})\triangleq\sum_{k}w_{k}y_{k}^{\frac{2}{p}}\right\},\text{ subj. to }\ \sum_{k}y_{k}=(2\varepsilon_{p})^{p}. \tag{86}
```

Note that the geometric properties of this optimization, and thus the
nature of the optimal solution, depend entirely on the curvature of
$`\widetilde{F}({\bm{y}})`$, which bifurcates at $`p=2`$.

#### Case I: $`1\leq p<2`$.

In this regime, as the exponent of $`\widetilde{F}(\cdot)`$ satisfies
$`\frac{2}{p}>1`$, $`\widetilde{F}({\bm{y}})`$ is a _strictly convex_
function. Minimizing a strictly convex function subject to a linear
equality and non-negativity constraints (which trivially satisfies
Slater’s condition) guarantees that strong duality holds (Boyd and
Vandenberghe, 2004, Section 5.2.3).

We thus introduce Lagrangian multiplier vectors
$`\bm{\lambda}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}_{\geq 0}`$ and $`\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}`$,
and the corresponding Lagrangian

```math
{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{y}},\bm{\lambda},\nu)\coloneq\tilde{F}({\bm{y}})-\bm{\lambda}^{\top}{\bm{y}}+\nu\left(\mathbf{1}^{\top}{\bm{y}}-(2\varepsilon_{p})^{p}\right). \tag{87}
```

The Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) stationarity condition with respect to
$`y_{k}`$ requires:

```math
\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial y_{k}}=\frac{2}{p}w_{k}y_{k}^{\frac{2-p}{p}}-\lambda_{k}+\nu=0. \tag{88}
```

First, we establish that $`\nu`$ must be strictly negative. Because the
primal constraint requires $`\sum_{k}y_{k}=(2\varepsilon_{p})^{p}>0`$,
there must be at least one index $`i`$ where $`y_{i}>0`$. By
complementary slackness, the corresponding dual variable is
$`\lambda_{i}=0`$, which implies that
$`\nu=-\frac{2}{p}w_{i}y_{i}^{\frac{2-p}{p}}`$. As $`w_{i}>0`$,
$`y_{i}>0`$, and for $`1\leq p<2`$ the exponent $`\frac{2-p}{p}>0`$, it
follows that $`\nu<0`$.

Next, we prove that the solution must be strictly interior (i.e.,
$`y_{k}>0`$ for all $`k`$). Assume for contradiction that there exists
some index $`j`$ such that $`y_{j}=0`$. Because $`\frac{2-p}{p}>0`$, the
$`j`$-th stationarity condition becomes

```math
0-\lambda_{j}+\nu=0\Longrightarrow\lambda_{j}=\nu<0, \tag{89}
```

a contradiction, i.e., any solution must be strictly interior. Again, by
complementary slackness, we then must have that $`\lambda_{k}=0`$ for
all $`k`$, which implies that

```math
y_{k}=\left(\frac{-p\nu}{2w_{k}}\right)^{\frac{p}{2-p}},\quad\forall k\in[K]. \tag{90}
```

Denoting $`C\coloneq(-\frac{p\nu}{2})^{\frac{p}{2-p}}>0`$, we can
rewrite the solution as $`y_{k}=C\cdot w_{k}^{-\frac{p}{2-p}}`$. To
determine the constant $`C`$, we apply the primal equality constraint
$`\sum_{k}y_{k}=(2\varepsilon_{p})^{p}`$:

```math
\sum_{k}C\cdot w_{k}^{-\frac{p}{2-p}}=(2\varepsilon_{p})^{p}\implies C=\frac{(2\varepsilon_{p})^{p}}{\sum_{k}w_{k}^{-\frac{p}{2-p}}}. \tag{91}
```

Substituting $`C`$ back into our expression for $`y_{k}`$ yields the
optimal coordinates in the transformed space:

```math
y_{k}^{\star}=(2\varepsilon_{p})^{p}\frac{w_{k}^{-\frac{p}{2-p}}}{\sum_{i}w_{i}^{-\frac{p}{2-p}}}. \tag{92}
```

Converting back to our original variables via
$`x_{k}^{\star}=(y_{k}^{\star})^{\frac{1}{p}}`$, we recover the dense
allocation:

```math
x_{k}^{\star}=2\varepsilon_{p}\frac{w_{k}^{-\frac{1}{2-p}}}{\left(\sum_{i}w_{i}^{-\frac{p}{2-p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}}. \tag{93}
```

Finally, we substitute $`x_{k}^{\star}`$ into the original objective
$`F({\bm{x}})`$ to find the minimum value $`V^{\star}`$:

```math
V^{\star}=\sum_{k}w_{k}(x_{k}^{\star})^{2}=4\varepsilon_{p}^{2}\sum_{k}w_{k}\frac{w_{k}^{-\frac{2}{2-p}}}{\left(\sum_{i}w_{i}^{-\frac{p}{2-p}}\right)^{\frac{2}{p}}}=4\varepsilon_{p}^{2}\frac{\sum_{k}w_{k}^{-\frac{p}{2-p}}}{\left(\sum_{i}w_{i}^{-\frac{p}{2-p}}\right)^{\frac{2}{p}}}=\frac{4\varepsilon_{p}^{2}}{\left(\sum_{k}w_{k}^{-\frac{p}{2-p}}\right)^{\frac{2-p}{p}}}. \tag{94}
```

Restoring back to $`{\bm{s}}^{\star}`$ as
$`s^{\star}_{k}=s_{k}^{\prime}\pm x_{k}^{\star}`$ yields Case I.

#### Case II: $`p\geq 2`$.

In this regime, as the exponent of $`\widetilde{F}(\cdot)`$ satisfies
$`\frac{2}{p}\leq 1`$, $`\widetilde{F}({\bm{y}})`$ is a _concave_
function (strictly concave if $`p>2`$). Also note that the domain
$`{\mathcal{Y}}`$ is a simplex defined as follows:

```math
{\mathcal{Y}}:=\mathrm{conv}\left(\left\{(2\varepsilon_{p})^{p}{\bm{e}}_{1},\cdots,(2\varepsilon_{p})^{p}{\bm{e}}_{K}\right\}\right),\quad\mathrm{Ext}({\mathcal{Y}})=\left\{(2\varepsilon_{p})^{p}{\bm{e}}_{1},\cdots,(2\varepsilon_{p})^{p}{\bm{e}}_{K}\right\}. \tag{95}
```

Bauer’s maximum principle (Bauer, 1958) then states that
$`\min_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\widetilde{F}({\bm{y}})`$ is attained
at at least one extreme point of $`{\mathcal{Y}}`$.

At each vertex $`(2\varepsilon_{p})^{p}{\bm{e}}_{k}`$, we have that

```math
\tilde{F}\left((2\varepsilon_{p})^{p}{\bm{e}}_{k}\right)=w_{k}\left((2\varepsilon_{p})^{p}\right)^{\frac{2}{p}}=4\varepsilon_{p}^{2}w_{k}. \tag{96}
```

To find the global minimum across all vertices, we simply choose the
index that minimizes the RHS, i.e., the minimum is obtained at
$`k^{\star}\in\argmin_{k}w_{k}`$. Converting back to $`{\bm{x}}`$, the
optimal solution is the following 1-sparse vector:

```math
{\bm{x}}^{\star}=2\varepsilon_{p}{\bm{e}}_{k^{\star}},\quad V^{\star}=4\varepsilon_{p}^{2}\min_{k}w_{k}. \tag{97}
```

Restoring back to $`{\bm{s}}^{\star}`$ as
$`s^{\star}_{k}=s_{k}^{\prime}\pm x_{k}^{\star}`$ yields Case II. ∎

## Appendix D Proof of Theorem 5.2: Assouad-Type In-Expectation Lower Bound

By Yao’s minimax principle (Yao, 1977), for any prior $`\mu`$ over
$`\left\{{\bm{s}}:\left\lVert{\bm{s}}-{\bm{s}}^{\star}\right\rVert_{p}\leq\xi(B)\right\}`$,

```math
\inf_{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}\sup_{{\bm{s}}:\left\lVert{\bm{s}}-{\bm{s}}^{\star}\right\rVert_{p}\leq\xi(B)}\mathbb{E}^{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}_{{\bm{s}}}\left[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{p}\right]\geq\inf_{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}\mathbb{E}^{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}_{{\bm{s}}\sim\mu}\left[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{p}\right], \tag{98}
```

and analogously for
$`\mathbb{E}^{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}_{{\bm{s}}\sim\mu}\left[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}`$
when $`1\leq p<\infty`$. We will thus lower bound the RHS (Bayes error)
with an appropriate choice of $`\mu`$.

For both in-expectation lower bounds, we utilize the Assouad’s lemma
(Assouad, 1983; Yu, 1997)⁷⁷ 7 This is derived by applying the Le Cam’s
Two-Point Method (Le Cam, 1973) coordinate-wise to the hypercube: see Yu
(1997) and Tsybakov (2009, Chapter 2) for more detailed discussions.
combined with the Bretagnolle-Huber inequality (Bretagnolle and Huber,
1979), which we recall here:

###### Lemma D.1 (Assouad’s lemma).

Let $`{\mathcal{F}}_{K}=\{-1,1\}^{K}`$ be the hypercube,
$`\{{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{v}}}:{\bm{v}}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{K}\}`$ be a family
of $`2^{K}`$ probability measures indexed by
$`{\bm{v}}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{K}`$, and $`w_{k}>0`$ be some fixed weights.
Then, for any estimator $`\hat{{\bm{v}}}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{K}`$,

```math
\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{v}}\sim\mathrm{Unif}({\mathcal{F}}_{K})}\left[\sum_{k\in[K]}w_{k}\mathds{1}[\hat{v}_{k}\neq v_{k}]\right]\geq\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k\in[K]}w_{k}e^{-\min\left\{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{{\mathbb{P}}}_{+,k},\bar{{\mathbb{P}}}_{-,k}),D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{{\mathbb{P}}}_{-,k},\bar{{\mathbb{P}}}_{+,k})\right\}}, \tag{99}
```

where
$`\bar{{\mathbb{P}}}_{i,k}\coloneq\frac{1}{2^{K-1}}\sum_{{\bm{v}}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{K}:v_{k}=i}{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{v}}}`$
for $`i\in\{-,+\}`$ is the marginal distribution for $`v_{k}=i`$.

Throughout, we denote
$`\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{v}}}=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{v}}\sim\mathrm{Unif}({\mathcal{F}}_{K})}`$.

#### Construction.

Suppose that $`(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})`$ is given and fixed, and let
$`{\bm{s}}^{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}`$ be a given score vector. To
apply Assouad’s lemma, we define the prior $`\mu`$ over the score
vectors as the uniform distribution over the following hypercube:

```math
\mu=\mathrm{Unif}\left(\left\{{\bm{s}}({\bm{v}})\triangleq{\bm{s}}^{\star}+{\bm{v}}\odot\bm{\Delta}\ :\ {\bm{v}}\in\{-1,+1\}^{K}\right\}\right), \tag{100}
```

where
$`\bm{\Delta}\coloneq(\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{K})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}^{K}`$
is chosen a-priori as described for each lower bound.

We utilize the same construction as in Proposition C.1, with
$`R_{k}\coloneq\min\{s_{k}^{\star},R-s_{k}^{\star}\}>0`$,
$`\max_{j}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}\leq R_{k}^{2}/2`$, and
$`\Delta_{k}\leq R_{k}/4`$. We set
$`{\mathbb{P}}_{\bm{v}}={\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}+{\bm{v}}\odot\bm{\Delta}}`$.

We, again, consider the canonical bandit model (Lattimore and
Szepesvári, 2020, Section 4.6) over the observations, as the estimator
may be adaptive. Then, the following lemma, whose proof is provided in
Section D.3, rigorously establishes the adaptive extension of the
Assouad’s lemma:

###### Lemma D.2.

With $`{\mathbb{P}}_{\bm{v}}`$’s as defined and $`\bar{N}_{k,j}`$ being
the number of times $`(k,j)`$ has been sampled, averaged over
$`{\bm{v}}\sim\mathrm{Unif}({\mathcal{F}}_{K})`$, we have that

```math
\min\left\{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{{\mathbb{P}}}_{+,k},\bar{{\mathbb{P}}}_{-,k}),D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{{\mathbb{P}}}_{-,k},\bar{{\mathbb{P}}}_{+,k})\right\}\leq C_{2}\sum_{j\in[J]}\frac{\bar{N}_{k,j}\Delta_{k}^{2}}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}, \tag{101}
```

satisfying $`\sum_{k,j}\bar{N}_{k,j}c_{j}\leq B.`$

From hereon and forth, we will denote
$`V_{k}\coloneq\sum_{j\in[J]}\frac{\bar{N}_{k,j}}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}},`$
which is an algorithm-dependent quantity.

### D.1 Lower Bound for Expected $`\ell_{p}`$-Error

We first consider $`1\leq p<\infty`$ and describe our a priori choice of
$`\bm{\Delta}`$:

```math
\Delta_{k}=\frac{1}{4\sqrt{V_{k}^{\star}}},\quad V_{k}^{\star}\coloneq\frac{B\left(c_{j^{*}(k)}\sigma_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{2}{p+2}}}{\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(c_{j^{*}(k)}\sigma_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+2}}}. \tag{102}
```

Then it is easy to check that
$`\left\lVert\Delta\right\rVert_{p}=\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\frac{{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})}{B}}=\xi(B)`$.
Also, we have that $`\Delta_{k}\leq\frac{R_{k}}{4}`$ for all $`k`$’s if

```math
B\geq\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(c_{j^{*}(k)}\sigma_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+2}}\right)\max_{k\in[K]}\frac{\left(c_{j^{*}(k)}\sigma_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{2}{p+2}}}{R_{k}^{2}}, \tag{103}
```

as given in the statement.

Here, as
$`\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{v}}}\left[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}({\bm{v}})\right\rVert_{p}\right]`$
is not coordinate-wise decomposable for $`p>1`$, we cannot directly
utilize Assouad’s lemma (Lemma D.1).⁸⁸ 8 Keen readers may wonder if
Jensen’s inequality may do the trick, but then would probably despair
after finding out that the direction is not as we want. Instead, we
utilize a concavity argument as follows.

For an arbitrary estimator $`\hat{{\bm{s}}}`$, let $`\hat{v}_{k}=+1`$ if
$`\hat{s}_{k}\geq s_{k}^{\star}`$ and $`\hat{v}_{k}=-1`$ otherwise. The
following lemma relates the two:

###### Lemma D.3.

$`\left|\hat{s}_{k}-({\bm{s}}({\bm{v}}))_{k}\right|\geq\Delta_{k}\mathds{1}[\hat{v}_{k}\neq v_{k}]`$
for any $`k\in[K]`$ and $`{\bm{v}}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{K}.`$

###### Proof.

W.l.o.g. suppose that $`v_{k}=+1`$. If $`\hat{v}_{k}=v_{k}=+1`$, then
the bound trivially holds, and thus suppose that
$`\hat{v}_{k}=-1\neq v_{k}`$. Then,

```math
\left|\hat{s}_{k}-{\bm{s}}({\bm{v}})\right|=\left|(\hat{s}_{k}-s_{k}^{\star})-\Delta_{k}\right|=-(\hat{s}_{k}-s_{k}^{\star})+\Delta_{k}\geq\Delta_{k}. \tag{104}
```

∎

By Lemma D.3,
$`\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}({\bm{v}})\right\rVert_{p}\geq S^{1/p}`$,
where
$`S\coloneq\sum_{k\in[K]}(\Delta_{k})^{p}\mathds{1}[\hat{v}_{k}\neq v_{k}]`$
satisfies $`0\leq S\leq\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}`$. By
Assouad’s lemma (Lemma D.1), its expectation is bounded as follows:

```math
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S]=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{v}}}\left[\sum_{k\in[K]}(\Delta_{k})^{p}\mathds{1}[\hat{v}_{k}\neq v_{k}]\right] \displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k\in[K]}(\Delta_{k})^{p}\exp\left(-C_{2}\Delta_{k}^{2}V_{k}\right)
```

```math
\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\underbrace{\sum_{k\in[K]}\frac{(\Delta_{k})^{p}}{\sum_{k}(\Delta_{k})^{p}}\exp\left(-C_{2}\Delta_{k}^{2}V_{k}\right)}_{(*)}. \tag{105}
```

Note that from our choice of $`\Delta_{k}`$’s,

```math
q_{k}\coloneq\frac{(\Delta_{k})^{p}}{\sum_{k}(\Delta_{k})^{p}}=\frac{(V_{k}^{\star})^{-p/2}}{\sum_{k}(V_{k}^{\star})^{-p/2}},\quad\Delta_{k}^{2}V_{k}=\frac{1}{16}\frac{V_{k}}{V_{k}^{\star}}. \tag{106}
```

One important observation is that

```math
\displaystyle\sum_{k\in[K]}q_{k}\frac{V_{k}}{V_{k}^{\star}} \displaystyle=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{k\in[K]}c_{j^{*}(k)}\sigma_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}V_{k}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{k\in[K]}\sum_{j\in[J]}\bar{N}_{k,j}\frac{c_{j^{*}(k)}\sigma_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}} \tag{107}
```

```math
\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{B}\sum_{k\in[K]}\sum_{j\in[J]}\bar{N}_{k,j}c_{j}\leq 1. \tag{108}
```

By Jensen’s inequality, we have that

```math
(*)\geq\exp\left(-\frac{C_{2}}{16}\sum_{k\in[K]}q_{k}\frac{V_{k}}{V_{k}^{\star}}\right)\geq\exp\left(-\frac{C_{2}}{16}\right). \tag{109}
```

Now, we present the concavity argument. For $`p\geq 1`$, the function
$`x\mapsto x^{\frac{1}{p}}`$ is concave on $`[0,\infty)`$ and
$`f(0)=0`$, i.e., geometrically,⁹⁹ 9 For concave functions, any secant
line lies below the function. we have that for any $`S\in[0,S_{\max}],`$

```math
S^{\frac{1}{p}}\geq\frac{S_{\max}^{\frac{1}{p}}-0^{\frac{1}{p}}}{S_{\max}-0}S=S_{\max}^{\frac{1}{p}-1}S. \tag{110}
```

Plugging in $`S_{\max}=\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}`$, we
have that

```math
\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{v}}}\left[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}({\bm{v}})\right\rVert_{p}\right]\geq\mathbb{E}\left[S^{\frac{1}{p}}\right]\geq\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}\right\rVert_{p}^{1-p}\mathbb{E}[S]\geq\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{C_{2}}{16}\right)}{4}\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}\right\rVert_{p}=\underbrace{\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{C_{2}}{16}\right)}{16}}_{\triangleq C_{3}}\sqrt{\frac{{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})}{B}}. \tag{111}
```

#### Constant-Probability Lower Bound.

For $`1\leq p<\infty`$, the preceding argument gives
$`0\leq S\leq S_{\max}=\xi(B)^{p}`$ and
$`\mathbb{E}[S]\geq 4C_{3}S_{\max}`$. For any $`0<t<4C_{3}`$, the
reverse Markov inequality follows directly from

```math
4C_{3}S_{\max}\leq\mathbb{E}[S]\leq tS_{\max}+(1-t)S_{\max}{\mathbb{P}}(S\geq tS_{\max}), \tag{112}
```

and hence $`{\mathbb{P}}(S\geq tS_{\max})\geq(4C_{3}-t)/(1-t)`$. Setting
$`t=(4C_{3}-\delta)/(1-\delta)`$ yields the probability bound stated in
Section 5, since
$`\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}({\bm{v}})\right\rVert_{p}\geq S^{1/p}`$.
This probability averages over the hypercube and observations, so at
least one fixed vertex satisfies the same bound for each estimator. The
conversion is consistent with the local-risk-to-quantile approach of Ma
et al. (2026); it requires no independence between the coordinate
errors.

#### The case $`p=\infty`$.

We use the same hypercube construction with equal perturbations

```math
\Delta_{k}=\xi(B)=\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\frac{{\mathcal{A}}_{\infty}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})}{B}},\qquad k\in[K]. \tag{113}
```

If
$`B\geq{\mathcal{A}}_{\infty}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\max_{k}R_{k}^{-2}`$,
then $`\Delta_{k}\leq R_{k}/4`$ for every $`k`$, so the beta
construction remains valid, and every vertex lies in the
$`\ell_{\infty}`$-ball of radius $`\xi(B)`$ around $`{\bm{s}}^{\star}`$.

For this case, take

```math
q_{k}:=\frac{c_{j^{*}(k)}\sigma_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}}{{\mathcal{A}}_{\infty}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})},\qquad S:=\sum_{k}q_{k}\mathds{1}[\hat{v}_{k}\neq v_{k}], \tag{114}
```

using the same sign decoder as above. Since
$`{\mathcal{A}}_{\infty}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})=\sum_{k}c_{j^{*}(k)}\sigma_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}`$,
we have $`\sum_{k}q_{k}=1`$ and $`0\leq S\leq 1`$. The budget constraint
gives

```math
\xi(B)^{2}\sum_{k}q_{k}V_{k}=\frac{1}{16B}\sum_{k}c_{j^{*}(k)}\sigma_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}V_{k}\leq\frac{1}{16}. \tag{115}
```

Applying Lemmas D.1 and D.2 and Jensen’s inequality, with expectation
over the uniform hypercube and observations, yields

```math
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S] \displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k}q_{k}\exp\left(-C_{2}\xi(B)^{2}V_{k}\right) \tag{116}
```

```math
\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\exp\left(-C_{2}\xi(B)^{2}\sum_{k}q_{k}V_{k}\right)\geq\frac{1}{4}\exp\left(-\frac{C_{2}}{16}\right)=4C_{3}. \tag{117}
```

A single incorrect sign forces $`\ell_{\infty}`$-error at least
$`\xi(B)`$, so

```math
S\leq\mathds{1}[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}({\bm{v}})\right\rVert_{\infty}\geq\xi(B)]. \tag{118}
```

Consequently, for every budget-feasible estimator, at least one fixed
vertex $`{\bm{s}}={\bm{s}}({\bm{v}})`$ satisfies

```math
{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}}^{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}\left(\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\geq\xi(B)\right)\geq 4C_{3}. \tag{119}
```

At the same vertex,

```math
\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{s}}}^{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}\left[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\right]\geq 4C_{3}\xi(B)=C_{3}\sqrt{\frac{{\mathcal{A}}_{\infty}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})}{B}}. \tag{120}
```

This proves the expected-error lower bound for $`p=\infty`$ and gives
its constant-probability counterpart directly.

### D.2 Lower Bound for $`p`$-th Moment of the Error

For $`1\leq p<\infty`$, we choose $`\bm{\Delta}`$ slightly differently
as $`\Delta_{k}=\frac{\sqrt{p}}{4\sqrt{V_{k}^{\star}}}`$ instead of
$`\Delta_{k}=\frac{1}{4\sqrt{V_{k}^{\star}}}`$, with the same
$`V_{k}^{\star}`$ as previous. This satisfies
$`\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}\right\rVert_{p}=\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\frac{p{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})}{B}}=\sqrt{p}\xi(B)`$.
Under the given assumption on $`B`$, we also have that
$`\Delta_{k}\leq\frac{R_{k}}{4}`$.

Let $`\hat{{\bm{s}}}`$ be any estimator, with the same sign decoder
$`\hat{v}_{k}`$ defined above. Then the absolute error of
$`\hat{s}_{k}`$ to $`({\bm{s}}({\bm{v}}))_{k}`$ with the sign-error is
closely related as described in Lemma D.3.

As
$`\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{v}}}\left[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}({\bm{v}})\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\right]`$
is coordinate-wise decomposable, applying Lemmas D.3 and D.1 and similar
Jensen argument as previous, we have that

```math
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{v}}}\left[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}({\bm{v}})\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\right]\geq\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{v}}}\left[\sum_{k\in[K]}(\Delta_{k})^{p}\mathds{1}[\hat{v}_{k}\neq v_{k}]\right] \displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\exp\left(-\frac{C_{2}p}{16}\right) \tag{121}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{C_{2}p}{16}\right)}{4^{1+p}}\left(\frac{p{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})}{B}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}} \tag{122}
```

Taking the $`1/p`$-th power on both sides, we have that

```math
\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{v}}}\left[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}({\bm{v}})\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\right]^{1/p}\geq\underbrace{\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{C_{2}}{16}\right)}{4^{1+\frac{1}{p}}}}_{=4^{1-\frac{1}{p}}C_{3}}\sqrt{\frac{p{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})}{B}}, \tag{123}
```

and we are done. ∎

### D.3 Proof of Lemma D.2: Adaptive Extension of Assouad’s Lemma

For $`{\bm{u}}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{K-1}`$ and $`i\in\{-,+\}`$, let us
denote $`{\mathbb{P}}_{(i,{\bm{u}}),k}`$ as the probability measure
indexed by $`{\bm{v}}`$ defined as $`v_{k}=i`$ and
$`{\bm{v}}_{-k}={\bm{u}}`$. Also, let $`{\mathbb{P}}_{i,k,j}`$ be the
probability measure for observations of $`(k,j)`$ when $`v_{k}=i`$.

By the joint convexity of KL and the Divergence Decomposition Lemma
(Lattimore and Szepesvári, 2020, Lemma 15.1), we have that

```math
\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{{\mathbb{P}}}_{+,k},\bar{{\mathbb{P}}}_{-,k}) \displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2^{K-1}}\sum_{{\bm{u}}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{K-1}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}({\mathbb{P}}_{(+,{\bm{u}}),k},{\mathbb{P}}_{(-,{\bm{u}}),k}) \tag{124}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in[J]}\underbrace{\frac{1}{2^{K-1}}\sum_{{\bm{u}}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{K-1}}\mathbb{E}_{(+,{\bm{u}}),k}[N_{k,j}]}_{\triangleq\bar{N}_{+,k,j}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}({\mathbb{P}}_{+,k,j},{\mathbb{P}}_{-,k,j}) \tag{125}
```

```math
\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\sum_{j\in[J]}\bar{N}_{+,k,j}\frac{\Delta_{k}^{2}}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}
```

and similarly,

```math
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{{\mathbb{P}}}_{-,k},\bar{{\mathbb{P}}}_{+,k})\leq C_{2}\sum_{j\in[J]}\bar{N}_{-,k,j}\frac{\Delta_{k}^{2}}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}. \tag{126}
```

Thus,

```math
\displaystyle\min\left\{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{{\mathbb{P}}}_{+,k},\bar{{\mathbb{P}}}_{-,k}),D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{{\mathbb{P}}}_{-,k},\bar{{\mathbb{P}}}_{+,k})\right\} \displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{{\mathbb{P}}}_{+,k},\bar{{\mathbb{P}}}_{-,k})+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{{\mathbb{P}}}_{-,k},\bar{{\mathbb{P}}}_{+,k})\right\} \tag{127}
```

```math
\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\Delta_{k}^{2}\sum_{j\in[J]}\frac{\bar{N}_{+,k,j}+\bar{N}_{-,k,j}}{2}\frac{1}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}. \tag{128}
```

Note that

```math
\frac{\bar{N}_{+,k,j}+\bar{N}_{-,k,j}}{2}=2^{-K}\sum_{{\bm{v}}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{K}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{v}}[N_{k,j}]=\bar{N}_{k,j}, \tag{129}
```

which satisfies the budget constraint

```math
\sum_{k,j}\bar{N}_{k,j}c_{j}=2^{-K}\sum_{{\bm{v}}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{K}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{v}}\left[\sum_{k,j}N_{k,j}c_{j}\right]\leq B. \tag{130}
```

∎

## Appendix E Proof of Proposition C.1: Construction of Beta Distributions

### E.1 Preliminaries and Construction

#### Beta Distribution.

For given $`\alpha,\beta>0,`$ the probability density of
$`X\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)`$ is given as

```math
f_{X}(x;\alpha,\beta)=\frac{1}{B(\alpha,\beta)}x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1},\quad x\in[0,1], \tag{131}
```

where
$`B(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}`$
is the beta function, and $`\Gamma(\cdot)`$ is the gamma function. We
note that its mean and variance are characterized in closed-form as
follows:

```math
\mathbb{E}[X]=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta},\quad\mathrm{Var}[X]=\frac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha+\beta)^{2}(\alpha+\beta+1)}. \tag{132}
```

#### Construction.

Recall that $`R_{k}=\min\{s_{k}^{\star},R-s_{k}^{\star}\}`$. Let
$`{\bm{s}}^{\star},\bm{\Delta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}`$ be given, with
$`|\Delta_{k}|\leq R_{k}/4`$. Then, for each
$`{\bm{s}}={\bm{s}}^{\star}+\bm{\Delta}`$, we define the probability
measure
$`{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}}=({\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}},k})_{k\in[K]}`$ as
follows. For each $`k`$, define

```math
q_{k,j}\coloneq\frac{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}{4R_{k}^{2}},\quad d_{k}(\bm{\Delta})\coloneq\frac{\Delta_{k}}{2R_{k}}, \tag{133}
```

and define

```math
\alpha_{k}(\bm{\Delta})\coloneq\frac{1-4d_{k}(\bm{\Delta})^{2}-4q_{k,j}}{8q_{k,j}}(1+2d_{k}(\bm{\Delta})),\quad\beta_{k}(\bm{\Delta})\coloneq\frac{1-4d_{k}(\bm{\Delta})^{2}-4q_{k,j}}{8q_{k,j}}(1-2d_{k}(\bm{\Delta})). \tag{134}
```

Note that under the assumptions that $`|\Delta_{k}|\leq R_{k}/4`$ and
$`\max_{j}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}\leq R_{k}^{2}/2`$, we have that
$`\alpha_{k}(\bm{\Delta}),\beta_{k}(\bm{\Delta})>0`$, and thus, the beta
distribution
$`Y_{k}(\bm{\Delta})\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_{k}(\bm{\Delta}),\beta_{k}(\bm{\Delta}))`$
is well-defined. With this, $`{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}},k}`$ is defined as
the measure of the random variable
$`X_{k}(\bm{\Delta})\coloneq s_{k}^{\star}-R_{k}+2R_{k}Y_{k}(\bm{\Delta})`$.
One can immediately verify that
$`X_{k}(\bm{\Delta})\in[s_{k}^{\star}-R_{k},s_{k}^{\star}+R_{k}]\subseteq[0,R].`$

### E.2 Proof of $`(i)`$: Shifted Mean, Same Variance

#### Mean.

By direct computation,

```math
\mathbb{E}[Y_{k}(\Delta)]=\frac{\alpha_{k}(\bm{\Delta})}{\alpha_{k}(\bm{\Delta})+\beta_{k}(\bm{\Delta})}=\frac{1}{1+\frac{\beta_{k}(\bm{\Delta})}{\alpha_{k}(\bm{\Delta})}}=\frac{1}{1+\frac{1-\frac{\Delta_{k}}{R_{k}}}{1+\frac{\Delta_{k}}{R_{k}}}}=\frac{\Delta_{k}+R_{k}}{2R_{k}}, \tag{135}
```

and thus,

```math
\mathbb{E}[X_{k}(\bm{\Delta})]=s_{k}^{\star}-R_{k}+2R_{k}\mathbb{E}[Y_{k}(\Delta)]=s_{k}^{\star}+\Delta_{k}. \tag{136}
```

#### Variance.

By direct computation,

```math
\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[Y_{k}(\Delta)] \displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{k}(\bm{\Delta})\beta_{k}(\bm{\Delta})}{(\alpha_{k}(\bm{\Delta})+\beta_{k}(\bm{\Delta}))^{2}(\alpha_{k}(\bm{\Delta})+\beta_{k}(\bm{\Delta})+1)} \tag{137}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\left(\left(1+\frac{\beta_{k}(\bm{\Delta})}{\alpha_{k}(\bm{\Delta})}\right)\left(1+\frac{\alpha_{k}(\bm{\Delta})}{\beta_{k}(\bm{\Delta})}\right)\left(1+\alpha_{k}(\bm{\Delta})+\beta_{k}(\bm{\Delta})\right)\right)^{-1} \tag{138}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\left(\left(\frac{2R_{k}}{R_{k}+\Delta_{k}}\right)\left(\frac{2R_{k}}{R_{k}-\Delta_{k}}\right)\left(\frac{1-4d_{k}(\bm{\Delta})^{2}}{4q_{k,j}}\right)\right)^{-1} \tag{139}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\frac{R_{k}^{2}-\Delta_{k}^{2}}{4R_{k}^{2}}\frac{\frac{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}{R_{k}^{2}}}{1-\frac{\Delta_{k}^{2}}{R_{k}^{2}}}=\frac{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}{4R_{k}^{2}}, \tag{140}
```

and thus,

```math
\mathrm{Var}[X_{k}(\bm{\Delta})]=4R_{k}^{2}\mathrm{Var}[Y_{k}(\Delta)]=\sigma_{k,j}^{2}. \tag{141}
```

### E.3 Proof of $`(ii)`$: KL of True(Null) and Perturbed Models

Because the KL divergence is invariant under affine transformations, it
suffices to compute the KL divergence between the standard beta
distributions $`Y_{k}(\bm{\Delta})`$ and $`Y_{k}(\mathbf{0})`$:

```math
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}+\bm{\Delta},k,j},{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star},k,j}\right)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_{k}(\bm{\Delta}),\beta_{k}(\bm{\Delta}))\ \|\ \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_{k}(\mathbf{0}),\beta_{k}(\mathbf{0}))\right). \tag{142}
```

To simplify notation, let
$`q\coloneq q_{k,j}=\frac{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}{4R_{k}^{2}}`$ and
$`d\coloneq d_{k}(\bm{\Delta})=\frac{\Delta_{k}}{2R_{k}}`$. Recall our
structural assumptions: $`|\Delta_{k}|\leq R_{k}/4`$, which implies
$`|d|\leq 1/8`$, and $`\max_{j}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}\leq R_{k}^{2}/2`$, which
implies $`q\leq 1/8`$. The parameters for the alternate and true models
respectively are $`\bm{\theta}(d)\coloneq(\alpha(d),\beta(d))`$ and
$`\bm{\theta}(0)\coloneq(\alpha(0),\beta(0))`$, defined as:

```math
\alpha(d)=\frac{1-4d^{2}-4q}{8q}(1+2d),\quad\beta(d)=\frac{1-4d^{2}-4q}{8q}(1-2d). \tag{143}
```

#### Step 1: Uniform Lower Bound on the Parameter Space.

We first establish that the parameters are strictly bounded away from
zero. Using $`|d|\leq 1/8`$ and $`q\leq 1/8`$, we can lower bound the
common term:

```math
1-4d^{2}-4q\geq 1-4\left(\frac{1}{64}\right)-4\left(\frac{1}{8}\right)=1-\frac{1}{16}-\frac{1}{2}=\frac{7}{16}. \tag{144}
```

Similarly, $`(1\pm 2d)\geq 1-2(1/8)=\frac{3}{4}`$. Thus, both
$`\alpha(d)`$ and $`\beta(d)`$ are uniformly lower bounded by:

```math
\alpha(d),\beta(d)\geq\frac{7/16}{8q}\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)=\frac{21}{512q}\triangleq\frac{C_{\min}}{q}, \tag{145}
```

where $`C_{\min}=\frac{21}{512}`$. This lower bound identically holds
for the null parameters $`\bm{\theta}(0)`$.

#### Step 2: Bounding the Parameter Perturbation.

Next, we bound the squared Euclidean distance between the parameters
$`\bm{\theta}(d)`$ and $`\bm{\theta}(0)`$ as a function of the shift
$`d`$:

```math
\displaystyle\alpha(d)-\alpha(0) \displaystyle=\frac{(1-4d^{2}-4q)(1+2d)-(1-4q)}{8q} \tag{146}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\frac{1+2d-4d^{2}-8d^{3}-4q-8dq-1+4q}{8q} \tag{147}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\frac{2d-4d^{2}-8d^{3}-8dq}{8q} \tag{148}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\frac{d}{4q}(1-2d-4d^{2}-4q). \tag{149}
```

Applying the absolute bounds $`|d|\leq 1/8`$ and $`q\leq 1/8`$ to the
polynomial term yields:

```math
|1-2d-4d^{2}-4q|\leq 1+2|d|+4d^{2}+4q\leq 1+\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{16}+\frac{1}{2}=\frac{29}{16}. \tag{150}
```

Therefore, $`|\alpha(d)-\alpha(0)|\leq\frac{29}{64}\frac{|d|}{q}`$. By
identical algebraic steps,
$`|\beta(d)-\beta(0)|\leq\frac{29}{64}\frac{|d|}{q}`$, and thus, the
squared Euclidean distance is thus strictly bounded by:

```math
\left\lVert\bm{\theta}(d)-\bm{\theta}(0)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq 2\left(\frac{29}{64}\right)^{2}\frac{d^{2}}{q^{2}}=\frac{841}{2048}\frac{d^{2}}{q^{2}}. \tag{151}
```

#### Step 3: Exact KL via Bregman Divergence.

The Beta distribution forms an exponential family, meaning the KL
divergence between two Beta distributions can be written exactly as the
Bregman divergence on their log-partition function
$`m(\alpha,\beta)\triangleq\log B(\alpha,\beta)`$ (Wainwright and
Jordan, 2008). By Taylor’s theorem, there exists some intermediate
parameter $`\tilde{\bm{\theta}}=(\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\beta})`$ lying
on the line segment between $`\bm{\theta}(d)`$ and $`\bm{\theta}(0)`$
such that:

```math
D_{\mathrm{KL}}({\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}+\bm{\Delta},k,j},{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star},k,j})=\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\theta}(d)-\bm{\theta}(0))^{\top}\bm{I}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}})(\bm{\theta}(d)-\bm{\theta}(0)), \tag{152}
```

where $`\bm{I}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}})=\nabla^{2}A(\tilde{\bm{\theta}})`$
is the Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) evaluated at
$`\tilde{\bm{\theta}}`$ (Amari, 2016). The elements of the FIM for the
Beta distribution are given by the trigamma function
$`\psi_{1}(z)\coloneq\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}\log\Gamma(z)`$:

```math
\bm{I}(\alpha,\beta)=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}(\alpha)-\psi_{1}(\alpha+\beta)&-\psi_{1}(\alpha+\beta)\\
-\psi_{1}(\alpha+\beta)&\psi_{1}(\beta)-\psi_{1}(\alpha+\beta)\end{pmatrix}. \tag{153}
```

For any vector $`{\bm{v}}=(v_{1},v_{2})^{\top}`$, the quadratic form is
$`v_{1}^{2}\psi_{1}(\alpha)+v_{2}^{2}\psi_{1}(\beta)-(v_{1}+v_{2})^{2}\psi_{1}(\alpha+\beta)`$.
Since $`\psi_{1}(z)>0`$ for all $`z>0`$, the subtracted term is strictly
negative, allowing us to upper-bound the quadratic form strictly by its
diagonal elements:

```math
{\bm{v}}^{\top}\bm{I}(\alpha,\beta){\bm{v}}\leq v_{1}^{2}\psi_{1}(\alpha)+v_{2}^{2}\psi_{1}(\beta)\leq\left\lVert{\bm{v}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\max(\psi_{1}(\alpha),\psi_{1}(\beta)). \tag{154}
```

#### Step 4: Trigamma Asymptotics and Final Assembly.

We use the standard uniform upper bound for the trigamma function:
$`\psi_{1}(z)\leq\frac{1}{z}+\frac{1}{z^{2}}`$ for $`z>0`$. Because
$`\tilde{\bm{\theta}}`$ lies on the convex line segment between
$`\bm{\theta}(d)`$ and $`\bm{\theta}(0)`$, and both endpoints are
uniformly lower-bounded by $`C_{\min}/q`$, it must be that
$`\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\beta}\geq C_{\min}/q`$. Thus,

```math
\max(\psi_{1}(\tilde{\alpha}),\psi_{1}(\tilde{\beta}))\leq\frac{q}{C_{\min}}+\frac{q^{2}}{C_{\min}^{2}}=q\left(\frac{1}{C_{\min}}+\frac{q}{C_{\min}^{2}}\right). \tag{155}
```

Since $`q\leq 1/8`$ and $`C_{\min}=\frac{21}{512}`$, we can numerically
upper bound the term in the parenthesis:

```math
\frac{1}{C_{\min}}+\frac{q}{C_{\min}^{2}}\leq\frac{512}{21}+\frac{1/8}{(21/512)^{2}}=\frac{512}{21}+\frac{32768}{441}=\frac{43520}{441}\triangleq C_{\psi}. \tag{156}
```

Substituting this maximum eigenvalue bound back into the Bregman
divergence yields:

```math
\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}({\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}+\bm{\Delta},k,j},{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star},k,j}) \displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\bm{\theta}(d)-\bm{\theta}(0)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\max(\psi_{1}(\tilde{\alpha}),\psi_{1}(\tilde{\beta})) \tag{157}
```

```math
\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{841}{2048}\frac{d^{2}}{q^{2}}\right)(C_{\psi}q)=\frac{841\cdot 43520}{4096\cdot 441}\frac{d^{2}}{q}=\frac{71485}{3528}\frac{d^{2}}{q}. \tag{158}
```

Finally, mapping back to the original parameterization
$`d^{2}=\frac{\Delta_{k}^{2}}{4R_{k}^{2}}`$ and
$`q=\frac{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}{4R_{k}^{2}}`$, the $`4R_{k}^{2}`$
denominators cancel out to yield the desired bound:

```math
D_{\mathrm{KL}}({\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}+\bm{\Delta},k,j},{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star},k,j})\leq\underbrace{\frac{71485}{3528}}_{\triangleq C_{1}}\frac{\Delta_{k}^{2}}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}. \tag{159}
```

∎

### E.4 Proof of $`(iii)`$: KL of Two “Adjacent” Perturbed Models

Similarly, it suffices to bound

```math
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}+\bm{\Delta},k,j},{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}-\bm{\Delta},k,j}\right)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_{k}(d),\beta_{k}(d))\ \|\ \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_{k}(-d),\beta_{k}(-d))\right). \tag{160}
```

First, we evaluate the squared Euclidean perturbation between the
endpoints $`\bm{\theta}(d)`$ and $`\bm{\theta}(-d)`$:

```math
\alpha(d)-\alpha(-d)=\frac{1-4d^{2}-4q}{8q}\left[(1+2d)-(1-2d)\right]=\frac{1-4d^{2}-4q}{2}\frac{d}{q}. \tag{161}
```

Since $`d^{2}\geq 0`$ and $`q\geq 0`$, we unconditionally have
$`1-4d^{2}-4q\leq 1`$. Therefore,
$`|\alpha(d)-\alpha(-d)|\leq\frac{1}{2}\frac{|d|}{q}`$. By symmetry, the
exact same bound applies to $`|\beta(d)-\beta(-d)|`$, and thus, we have
that

```math
\left\lVert\bm{\theta}(d)-\bm{\theta}(-d)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq 2\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\frac{d^{2}}{q^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d^{2}}{q^{2}}. \tag{162}
```

Following the exact same Bregman divergence formulation from Step 3
above, there exists an intermediate point $`\hat{\bm{\theta}}`$ on the
line segment connecting $`\bm{\theta}(-d)`$ and $`\bm{\theta}(d)`$. By
the convexity of the parameter space and the symmetry of our
construction, the endpoints $`\bm{\theta}(-d)`$ and $`\bm{\theta}(d)`$
both identically satisfy the lower bound $`C_{\min}/q`$ derived in Step

1. Thus, the entire line segment, and consequently
   $`\hat{\bm{\theta}}`$, also satisfies this uniform lower bound.

As a result, the maximum eigenvalue bound of the Fisher Information
Matrix remains
$`\max(\psi_{1}(\hat{\alpha}),\psi_{1}(\hat{\beta}))\leq C_{\psi}q`$,
utilizing the identical constant $`C_{\psi}=\frac{43520}{441}`$.
Substituting this into the Bregman divergence quadratic form yields:

```math
\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}({\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}+\bm{\Delta},k,j},{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}-\bm{\Delta},k,j}) \displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\bm{\theta}(d)-\bm{\theta}(-d)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\max(\psi_{1}(\hat{\alpha}),\psi_{1}(\hat{\beta})) \tag{163}
```

```math
\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{d^{2}}{q^{2}}\right)(C_{\psi}q)=\frac{C_{\psi}}{4}\frac{d^{2}}{q}=\frac{10880}{441}\frac{d^{2}}{q}. \tag{164}
```

Mapping back to the original parameterization
$`d^{2}/q=\Delta_{k}^{2}/\sigma_{k,j}^{2}`$, we obtain the
non-asymptotic adjacent pair bound:

```math
D_{\mathrm{KL}}({\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}+\bm{\Delta},k,j},{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bm{s}}^{\star}-\bm{\Delta},k,j})\leq\underbrace{\frac{10880}{441}}_{\triangleq C_{2}}\frac{\Delta_{k}^{2}}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}. \tag{165}
```

∎

## Appendix F Gaussian Scores

In this Appendix, we elaborate on how our results generalize to the case
when the scores are Gaussian:

###### Assumption 1 (Gaussian Scores).

If the learner queries $`k\in[K]`$ to a judge $`j\in[J]`$, she observes
a noisy score $`\tilde{s}\sim s_{k}+\varepsilon_{k,j}`$, where
$`\varepsilon_{k,j}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma_{k,j}^{2})`$.

This then allows us to utilize the following standard Gaussian tail
bound:

###### Lemma F.1.

For $`Z\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma^{2})`$,
$`{\mathbb{P}}\left(|Z|\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-t^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})\right).`$

For clarity, we recall the inverse-variance weighted estimator:

![](arxiv-2605-23362v2--7a924eed1710.figures/inline-1.svg)

### F.1 Upper Bounds

#### Known Variances.

First, when the variances are known, we have the following error bound:

###### Proposition F.2.

Let $`p\in[1,\infty]`$, $`B\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}`$ be a total budget, and
$`\delta\in(0,1)`$ be a target confidence level. Also, let
$`(N_{k,j})_{k,j}`$ be a fixed integer allocation satisfying
$`N_{k,j}\geq 0`$, $`\sum_{k,j}c_{j}N_{k,j}\leq B`$, and
$`\sum_{j}N_{k,j}\geq 1`$ for every $`k`$. Then, IVWE is
$`(B,\delta,\ell_{p})`$-budget efficient at any
$`({\bm{s}},\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}^{K\times J}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}^{J}`$
with the following error rate $`\varepsilon_{p}`$:

```math
\varepsilon_{p}=\sqrt{\frac{2{\mathcal{A}}_{p}(\bm{\omega};\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\log\frac{2K}{\delta}}{B}}, \tag{167}
```

where
$`\bm{\omega}\coloneq\left(\omega_{k,j}\triangleq\frac{c_{j}N_{k,j}}{B}\right)_{k,j}`$,
the allocation objective
$`{\mathcal{A}}_{p}(\bm{\omega};\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})`$ is defined the
same as in Theorem 3.2. Then, with the optimal allocation
$`\bm{\omega}^{*}`$ that maximizes
$`{\mathcal{A}}_{p}(\bm{\omega};\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})`$, we have
$`\varepsilon_{p}=\sqrt{\frac{2{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\log\frac{2K}{\delta}}{B}}.`$

###### Proof Sketch.

This follows from applying Lemma F.1 and taking a union bound over
$`k\in[K]`$. ∎

#### A sharper confidence bound for $`p=2`$.

For a fixed allocation with at least one observation per query, the
Gaussian structure gives a simple refinement without a union bound over
queries. Write $`v_{k}\coloneq(\sum_{j}N_{k,j}/\sigma_{k,j}^{2})^{-1}`$
for the variance of $`\hat{s}_{k}`$. Since the coordinate errors are
independent Gaussians, the weighted $`\chi^{2}`$ bound of Laurent and
Massart (2000, Lemma 1) gives, with probability at least $`1-\delta`$,

```math
\displaystyle\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2} \displaystyle\leq\sum_{k}v_{k}+2\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{\delta}\sum_{k}v_{k}^{2}}+2\log\frac{1}{\delta}\max_{k}v_{k}, \tag{168}
```

```math
\displaystyle\leq\left(\sqrt{\sum_{k}v_{k}}+\sqrt{2\max_{k}v_{k}\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)^{2}. \tag{169}
```

The second inequality follows from
$`\sum_{k}v_{k}^{2}\leq(\max_{k}v_{k})\sum_{k}v_{k}`$. This separates
the overall estimation error from the additional confidence requirement.
With one unit-cost judge, identical variances $`\sigma^{2}`$, and
$`N_{k}=B/K`$, it gives the example in Section 5. More generally, the
allocation minimizing $`\sum_{k}v_{k}`$ need not minimize
$`\max_{k}v_{k}`$; this refinement alone therefore does not settle the
optimal joint dependence on allocation and confidence.

Readers familiar with Gaussian process concentration may recognize this
two-term structure from the classical Tsirelson–Ibragimov–Sudakov (TIS)
inequality (Cirel’son et al., 1976; Katz-Samuels et al., 2020): an
expected-supremum (Gaussian width) term and a dimension-free deviation
term governed by the largest variance. Indeed, applying TIS to the
process $`\langle u,\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\rangle`$ indexed by
$`\|u\|_{2}\leq 1`$ also yields the second bound in Eqn. (169), since
its expected supremum is
$`\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{2}]\leq\sqrt{\sum_{k}v_{k}}`$
and its largest variance is $`\max_{k}v_{k}`$.

#### Unknown Variances.

Here, as we have briefly elaborated in Section 4, we can utilize the
following multiplicative concentration for the sample variance of a
Gaussian:

###### Lemma F.3 (Eqn. (4.3) & (4.4) of Laurent and Massart (2000)).

With $`\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}^{2}`$ as in line 3 of Algorithm 2 and
$`\epsilon_{0}\triangleq\log\frac{2KJ}{\delta}`$, suppose that $`N_{0}`$
is an integer satisfying $`N_{0}-1>4\epsilon_{0}`$, so that the
denominators below are positive. Then

```math
{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}^{2}}{1+2\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{N_{0}-1}}+\frac{2\epsilon_{0}}{N_{0}-1}}\leq\sigma_{k,j}^{2}\leq\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}^{2}}{1-2\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{N_{0}-1}}},\quad\forall(k,j)\in[K]\times[J]\right)\geq 1-\delta. \tag{170}
```

Input: Total budget $`B>0`$, Cost vector
$`{\bm{c}}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}_{+}`$, Number of forced exploration per pair
$`N_{0}\geq 2`$

// Phase I: Forced Exploration

1 for _each $`(k,j)\in[K]\times[J]`$_ do

    2 Pull arm $`(k,j)`$ exactly $`N_{0}`$ times and observe noisy
evaluation scores $`\left\{s_{k,j}^{(n)}\right\}_{n\in[N_{0}]}`$ ;

    3 Compute the empirical mean and variance estimator

```math
\displaystyle\hat{s}_{k,j}\coloneq\frac{1}{N_{0}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{0}}s_{k,j}^{(n)},\quad\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}^{2}\coloneq\frac{1}{N_{0}-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{0}}\left({s}_{k,j}^{(n)}-\hat{s}_{k,j}\right)^{2} \tag{171}
```

// Phase II: IVWE

4 Compute the optimal allocation
$`\left(\widehat{\omega}_{k,j}^{*}\right)_{k,j}`$ as in Theorem 3.2
using remaining budget
$`B^{\prime}\triangleq B-N_{0}K\sum_{j\in[J]}c_{j}-K(\max_{j\in[J]}c_{j})>0`$,
based on $`\left\{\left(\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}\right)^{2}\right\}_{k,j}`$
Set
$`\widehat{N}_{k,j}^{*}=\left\lceil B^{\prime}\widehat{\omega}_{k,j}^{*}/c_{j}\right\rceil`$,
and query each pair $`(k,j)`$ exactly $`\widehat{N}_{k,j}^{*}`$ times;

5 return IVWE with $`\left(\widehat{N}_{k,j}^{*}\right)_{k,j}`$ as
allocation and $`\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}^{2}`$ as variance proxies;

Algorithm 2 Estimate-then-IVWE (Gaussian)

Then we have the following error rate, whose proof is provided in
Section G.1:

###### Theorem F.4 (Error Rates of Est-IVWE-Gaussian).

Let $`p\in[1,\infty]`$, $`B\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}`$, and
$`\delta\in(0,1)`$. Let us set
$`N_{0}=1+\left\lceil 16\log\frac{4KJ}{\delta}\right\rceil`$ and suppose
that $`B>N_{0}K\sum_{j\in[J]}c_{j}+Kc_{\max}`$. Then, Est-IVWE-Gaussian
is $`(B,\delta,\ell_{p})`$-budget efficient at any
$`({\bm{s}},\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})`$ with the following error rate
$`\varepsilon_{p}`$:

```math
\varepsilon_{p}=\sqrt{\frac{13{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{2\left(B-N_{0}K\sum_{j}c_{j}-Kc_{\max}\right)}}. \tag{172}
```

Additionally, if $`B\geq 2\left(N_{0}K\sum_{j}c_{j}+Kc_{\max}\right)`$,
this rate reduces to

```math
\varepsilon_{p}\leq\sqrt{\frac{13{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{2B}}+{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{K\sum_{j}c_{j}\,\sqrt{{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})}\left(\log\frac{4KJ}{\delta}\right)^{3/2}}{B^{3/2}}\right). \tag{173}
```

### F.2 Minimax Lower Bounds

We first have the following very similar-looking high-probability lower
bound:

###### Theorem F.5 (Local & High Probability Lower Bound (Gaussian)).

Let $`p\in[1,\infty]`$, $`B>0`$, $`\delta\in(0,e^{-2}]`$, and
$`({\bm{s}},\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})`$ be a given problem instance. Then,
any algorithm that is $`(B,\delta,\ell_{p})`$-budget efficient with an
error rate of $`\varepsilon_{p}>0`$ at any
$`({\bm{s}}^{\prime},\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})`$ with
$`\left\lVert{\bm{s}}^{\prime}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{p}\leq 2\varepsilon_{p}`$
must satisfy the following: for some absolute constants
$`C_{1},C_{2}>0`$,

```math
\varepsilon_{p}\geq\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{kl}(1-\delta,\delta)}{C_{1}B}{{\mathcal{A}}_{\frac{2p}{2-p}}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})}}\ \ \ \text{for $1\leq p<2$},\quad\varepsilon_{p}\geq\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{C_{2}B}{{\mathcal{A}}^{*}_{\infty}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})}}\ \ \ \text{for $p\geq 2$}. \tag{174}
```

###### Proof.

The proof is almost the same as that of Theorem 5.1, except we now
construct the instances via Gaussians instead of the Beta distributions,
resulting in constant-wise difference only. ∎

As in the bounded setting, this Fano-type change-of-measure argument
captures the $`\log\frac{1}{\delta}`$ dependence. We complement it with
global in-expectation lower bounds that recover
$`{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}`$, using different proof techniques that may be
of independent interest.¹⁰¹⁰ 10 One may wonder whether it is possible to
utilize Assouad’s lemma (Assouad, 1983; Yu, 1997) as done in our
Theorem 5.2. This is definitely possible that leads to a similar local
minimax lower bound. Here, we intend to show a completely different
argument for global minimax lower bounds. Again, the
$`\inf_{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}`$ is over all adaptive algorithms satisfying the
budget constraint. In particular, the lower bound holds even when the
learner is given the true variance profile $`\bm{\sigma}`$.

###### Theorem F.6 (Global, In-Expectation Minimax Lower Bounds).

For each $`1\leq p\leq\infty`$,

```math
\inf_{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}\sup_{{\bm{s}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}}\mathbb{E}^{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}_{{\bm{s}}\sim\mu}\left[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{p}\right]\geq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})}{B}}. \tag{175}
```

For $`1\leq p<\infty`$, we also have:

```math
\inf_{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}\sup_{{\bm{s}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}}\mathbb{E}^{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}_{{\bm{s}}\sim\mu}\left[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}\geq m_{p}\sqrt{\frac{{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})}{B}}, \tag{176}
```

where¹¹¹¹ 11 $`\Gamma(\cdot)`$ is the usual gamma function.
$`m_{p}\coloneq\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{\Gamma((p+1)/2)}{\sqrt{\pi}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}=\mathbb{E}[|Z|^{p}]^{1/p}`$
for $`Z\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)`$.

###### Proof Sketch.

The full proof is deferred to Section G.2. Instead of following the
standard lower bound framework (Yu, 1997; Tsybakov, 2009), we heavily
exploit the fact that everything is Gaussian. Specifically, this implies
that the posterior risk is minimized at the posterior mean, and
everything (e.g., posterior distribution, Fisher information) is
computable in closed forms. ∎

When the _variances are known_ and the continuous oracle allocation has
integer sample counts, the lower bound for the $`p`$-th moment is
constant-wise tight for every finite $`p`$, as shown by the following
matching upper bound:

###### Theorem F.7.

Let $`1\leq p<\infty`$. When the variances are _known_ and the
continuous oracle allocation at budget $`B`$ has integer sample counts,
IVWE with optimal allocation achieves the following:

```math
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq m_{p}\sqrt{\frac{{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})}{B}}. \tag{177}
```

###### Proof.

Recall that
$`\hat{s}_{k}-s_{k}\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,\left(\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{N_{k,j}}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}\right)^{-1}\right)`$.
Thus, we have that

```math
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k\in[K]}|\hat{s}_{k}-s_{k}|^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq m_{p}\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{N_{k,j}}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{p}{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}, \tag{178}
```

where $`m_{p}=\mathbb{E}[|Z|^{p}]^{1/p}`$ for
$`Z\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)`$ as in Theorem F.6. Then, we conclude by
Theorem 3.2. ∎

We lastly show another global lower bound for
$`\mathbb{E}^{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}_{{\bm{s}}\sim\mu}\left[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}`$
based on a completely different (and from our perspective, slightly less
well-known) technique that may be of an independent interest:

###### Theorem F.8.

For $`1\leq p<\infty`$, we have:

```math
\inf_{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}\sup_{{\bm{s}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}}\mathbb{E}^{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}_{{\bm{s}}\sim\mu}\left[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}\geq\frac{\sqrt{2\pi e}}{C_{\mathrm{ME}}(p)}\sqrt{\frac{{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})}{B}}, \tag{179}
```

where
$`C_{\mathrm{ME}}(p)\triangleq 2e^{\frac{1}{p}}\Gamma(p^{-1})p^{\frac{1}{p}-1}`$
is the partition function of the $`L_{p}`$ max-entropy distribution.

###### Proof Sketch.

The full proof is deferred to Section G.3. The key technical tool is the
van Trees inequality (van Trees, 1968; Gill and Levit, 1995), recently
extended to $`L_{p}`$-norm by Chen and Özgür (2024), with cosine-squared
prior (Tsybakov, 2009) and taking a limit to uninformative prior. ∎

Note that when $`p=2`$, this bound is also constant-wise optimal:
$`m_{2}=1=\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\pi e}}{C_{\mathrm{ME}}(2)}\right)^{2}`$.

## Appendix G Proofs of Theorems in Appendix F (Gaussian Scores)

### G.1 Proof of Theorem F.4: Error Rate of Est-IVWE-Gaussian

Denote the lower and upper bounds on variances $`\sigma_{k,j}^{2}`$
after the exploration phase by

```math
\underline{\sigma}_{k,j}^{2}=\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}^{2}}{1+2\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{N_{0}-1}}+\frac{2\epsilon_{0}}{N_{0}-1}}\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\sigma}_{k,j}^{2}=\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}^{2}}{1-2\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{N_{0}-1}}}, \tag{180}
```

where $`\epsilon_{0}=\log\frac{4KJ}{\delta}`$. By Lemma F.3, we know
that

```math
{\mathbb{P}}\left(\underline{\sigma}_{k,j}^{2}\leq\sigma_{k,j}^{2}\leq\overline{\sigma}_{k,j}^{2},\quad\forall(k,j)\in[K]\times[J]\right)\geq 1-\frac{\delta}{2}, \tag{181}
```

which we will assume to hold throughout the proof. We denote the ratio
between the upper and lower bounds as
$`\kappa=\overline{\sigma}_{k,j}^{2}/\underline{\sigma}_{k,j}^{2}`$,
which is the same across all pairs $`(k,j)`$. Since
$`N_{0}=1+\left\lceil 16\log\frac{4KJ}{\delta}\right\rceil`$,
$`\epsilon_{0}/(N_{0}-1)\leq\frac{1}{16}`$, we have that
$`\kappa\leq\frac{13}{4}`$.

Recall that Est-IVWE-Gaussian (Algorithm 2) designates an (predicted)
optimal judge
$`\hat{j}^{*}(k)\triangleq\argmin_{j\in[J]}c_{j}\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}^{2}`$
for each query $`k\in[K]`$, then computes the optimal allocation with
variance estimator $`\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}^{2}`$ and available continuous
budget $`B^{\prime}=B-CN_{0}-Kc_{\max}>0`$ with
$`C\coloneq K\sum_{j\in[J]}c_{j}`$ as follows:

```math
\widehat{\omega}_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{*}=\frac{\left(c_{\hat{j}^{*}(k)}\hat{\sigma}_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+2}}}{\sum_{k^{\prime}\in[K]}\left(c_{\hat{j}^{*}(k^{\prime})}\hat{\sigma}_{k^{\prime},\hat{j}^{*}(k^{\prime})}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+2}}},\quad\widehat{N}_{k,j}^{*}=\left\lceil\frac{B^{\prime}\widehat{\omega}_{k,j}^{*}}{c_{j}}\right\rceil. \tag{182}
```

There is one positive allocation per query, so rounding costs at most
$`\sum_{k}c_{\hat{j}^{*}(k)}\leq Kc_{\max}`$; together with exploration,
the total cost is at most $`B`$. Conditional on Phase I, the allocation
and selected judges are fixed, and the fresh Phase-II sample mean
$`\hat{s}_{k}`$ is Gaussian with variance
$`\sigma_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{2}/\widehat{N}_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{*}`$.
Thus, by Lemma F.1 and a union bound, the following event has
conditional probability at least $`1-\delta/2`$:

```math
{\mathbb{P}}\left(|\hat{s}_{k}-s_{k}|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{2}\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{\widehat{N}_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{*}}},\quad\forall k\in[K]\right)\geq 1-\frac{\delta}{2}. \tag{183}
```

Thus, by summing up $`p`$-th power of these terms over $`k\in[K]`$ and
substituting the definition of $`\widehat{\omega}_{k,j}^{*}`$,the
following holds with probability at least $`1-\delta`$:

```math
\displaystyle\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{p} \displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(\frac{\widehat{N}_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{*}}{\sigma_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{p}{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}} \tag{184}
```

```math
\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{B^{\prime}}}\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(\frac{\widehat{\omega}_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}}{c_{\hat{j}^{*}(k)}\sigma_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{p}{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}} \tag{185}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{B^{\prime}}}\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(\frac{1}{c_{\hat{j}^{*}(k)}\sigma_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\left(c_{\hat{j}^{*}(k)}\hat{\sigma}_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+2}}}{\sum_{k^{\prime}\in[K]}\left(c_{\hat{j}^{*}(k^{\prime})}\hat{\sigma}_{k^{\prime},\hat{j}^{*}(k^{\prime})}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+2}}}\right)^{-\frac{p}{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}} \tag{186}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{B^{\prime}}}\sqrt{\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(c_{\hat{j}^{*}(k)}\hat{\sigma}_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+2}}}\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(\frac{\sigma_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}}{\hat{\sigma}_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}}\right)^{p}\left(c_{\hat{j}^{*}(k)}\hat{\sigma}_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}} \tag{187}
```

```math
\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{1-2\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{N_{0}-1}}}}\sqrt{\frac{2\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{B^{\prime}}}\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(c_{\hat{j}^{*}(k)}\hat{\sigma}_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+2}}\right)^{\frac{p+2}{2p}}, \tag{188}
```

where the last inequality holds since
$`\sigma_{k,j}^{2}/\hat{\sigma}_{k,j}^{2}\leq\left(1-2\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{N_{0}-1}}\right)^{-1}`$
for any $`(k,j)\in[K]\times[J]`$ by Lemma F.3. When $`p=\infty`$, we set
$`p/(p+2)=1`$ and $`(p+2)/p=1`$ by convention, and the above inequality
holds in that case as well:

```math
\displaystyle\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{\infty} \displaystyle\leq\max_{k\in[K]}\sqrt{2\log\frac{4K}{\delta}\frac{\sigma_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{2}}{\widehat{N}_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{*}}} \tag{189}
```

```math
\displaystyle\leq\max_{k\in[K]}\sqrt{\frac{2\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{B^{\prime}}\frac{c_{\hat{j}^{*}(k)}\sigma_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{2}}{\widehat{\omega}_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{*}}} \tag{190}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{B^{\prime}}}\left(\max_{k\in[K]}\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{2}}{\hat{\sigma}_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{2}}\sum_{k^{\prime}\in[K]}c_{\hat{j}^{*}(k^{\prime})}\hat{\sigma}_{k^{\prime},\hat{j}^{*}(k^{\prime})}^{2}}\right) \tag{191}
```

```math
\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{1-2\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{N_{0}-1}}}}\sqrt{\frac{2\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{B^{\prime}}}\sqrt{\sum_{k\in[K]}c_{\hat{j}^{*}(k)}\hat{\sigma}_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{2}}. \tag{192}
```

As
$`c_{\hat{j}^{*}(k)}\hat{\sigma}_{k,\hat{j}^{*}(k)}^{2}\leq c_{j^{*}(k)}\hat{\sigma}_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}`$
by the definition of $`\hat{j}^{*}(k)`$, we have

```math
\displaystyle\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(c_{\hat{j}(k)}\hat{\sigma}_{k,\hat{j}(k)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+2}}\right)^{\frac{p+2}{2p}} \tag{193}
```

```math
\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(c_{j^{*}(k)}\hat{\sigma}_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+2}}\right)^{\frac{p+2}{2p}} \tag{194}
```

```math
\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{1+2\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{N_{0}-1}}+\frac{2\epsilon_{0}}{N_{0}-1}}\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(c_{j^{*}(k)}\sigma_{k,j^{*}(k)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{p+2}}\right)^{\frac{p+2}{2p}}.
```

Combining the two events, with probability at least $`1-\delta`$ we have

```math
\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{p}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\kappa{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{B^{\prime}}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{13{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{2(B-CN_{0}-Kc_{\max})}}, \tag{195}
```

where $`\kappa\leq 13/4`$. This proves the exact bound for every
$`B>CN_{0}+Kc_{\max}`$. If $`B\geq 2(CN_{0}+Kc_{\max})`$, we may
additionally apply Lemma B.3 to obtain

```math
\displaystyle\varepsilon_{p} \displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{13{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{2B}}+\frac{(CN_{0}+Kc_{\max})\sqrt{\frac{13}{2}{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}}{B^{3/2}} \tag{196}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{13{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})\log\frac{4K}{\delta}}{2B}}+{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(\frac{C\sqrt{{\mathcal{A}}_{p}^{*}(\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})}\left(\log\frac{4KJ}{\delta}\right)^{3/2}}{B^{3/2}}\right). \tag{197}
```

Here $`Kc_{\max}\leq C`$ and $`N_{0}={\mathcal{O}}(\log(4KJ/\delta))`$,
with universal constants. In particular, the expansion requires the
stronger budget condition, while the unexpanded bound requires only a
positive Phase-II budget.

### G.2 Proof of Theorem F.6: Global In-Expectation Lower Bounds for Gaussian Scores

Again, we start with the Yao’s minimax principle (Yao, 1977), but
without the locality: for any prior $`\mu`$ over $`{\mathbb{R}}^{K}`$
(note that now, we consider the prior over the entire
$`{\mathbb{R}}^{K}`$ instead of near some given $`{\bm{s}}^{\star}`$),

```math
\inf_{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}\sup_{{\bm{s}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}}\mathbb{E}^{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}_{{\bm{s}}}\left[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{p}\right]\geq\inf_{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}\mathbb{E}^{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}_{{\bm{s}}\sim\mu}\left[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{p}\right], \tag{198}
```

and analogously for
$`\mathbb{E}^{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}_{{\bm{s}}\sim\mu}\left[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}`$.
We will thus lower bound the RHS (Bayes error) with an appropriate
choice of $`\mu`$.

In particular, we consider an isotropic Gaussian prior: for a
$`\lambda>0`$,

```math
\mu\coloneq{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{0}},\lambda^{2}{\bm{I}}_{K}). \tag{199}
```

Let us fix an arbitrary, adaptive algorithm $`{\mathcal{A}}`$ that
interacts with the true environment $`{\bm{s}}^{\star}`$ sequentially as
follows: at each timestep $`t`$, when the learner selects query
$`k_{t}`$ and judge $`j_{t}`$, they observe a noisy score
$`\tilde{s}_{t}\coloneq s_{k_{t}}+\varepsilon_{k_{t},j_{t}}`$. Let us
denote $`N_{k,j}\coloneq\sum_{t}\mathds{1}[k_{t}=k,j_{t}=j]`$ as the
number of times $`{\mathcal{A}}`$ queries the pair $`(k,j)`$ throughout
the interaction. Suppose that $`{\mathcal{A}}`$ interacts for $`T`$
rounds, while satisfying the budget constraint almost surely. For each
time $`t`$, let
$`{\mathcal{H}}_{t}\coloneq(k_{1},j_{1},\tilde{s}_{1},\cdots,k_{t},j_{t},\tilde{s}_{t})`$
be the interaction history til time $`t`$. Then, at the end of the
interaction, $`{\mathcal{A}}`$ outputs an estimator
$`\hat{{\bm{s}}}=\hat{{\bm{s}}}({\mathcal{H}}_{T})`$. For clarity, with
a slight abuse of notation, we will distinguish between the two:
$`{\mathcal{A}}`$ as an adaptive data collection subprocedure, and
$`\hat{{\bm{s}}}`$ as an aggregation subprocedure that takes
$`{\mathcal{H}}_{T}`$ as an input.

Then, using the fact that everything is Gaussian, we can compute the
posterior distribution of $`{\bm{s}}\mid{\mathcal{H}}_{T}`$ in
closed-form, as shown in the following lemma whose proof is deferred to
the end:

###### Lemma G.1.

$`{\bm{s}}\mid{\mathcal{H}}_{T}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\mathbb{E}[{\bm{s}}\mid{\mathcal{H}}_{T}],{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{A}})^{-1})`$,
where
$`{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{A}})=\mathrm{diag}\left(({\mathcal{I}}_{k}({\mathcal{A}}))_{k\in[K]}\right)`$
is the (diagonal) posterior Fisher information of the algorithm
$`{\mathcal{A}}`$ defined as

```math
{\mathcal{I}}_{k}({\mathcal{A}})=\lambda^{-2}+\sum_{j\in[J]}\frac{N_{k,j}}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}. \tag{200}
```

Denoting $`{\mathcal{H}}_{T}({\mathcal{A}})`$ to be the random full
interaction history for $`{\mathcal{A}}`$,

```math
\displaystyle\inf_{\mathcal{A}}\inf_{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{s}}\sim\mu}^{{\mathcal{A}},\hat{{\bm{s}}}}\left[f\left(\hat{{\bm{s}}}({\mathcal{H}}_{T}({\mathcal{A}}))-{\bm{s}}\right)\right]=\inf_{\mathcal{A}}\inf_{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathcal{A}}\left[\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{s}}\mid{\mathcal{H}}_{T}({\mathcal{A}})}^{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}\left[f\left(\hat{{\bm{s}}}({\mathcal{H}}_{T}({\mathcal{A}}))-{\bm{s}}\right)\mid{\mathcal{H}}_{T}({\mathcal{A}})\right]}_{\textbf{{conditional posterior risk}}}\right], \tag{201}
```

where either $`f({\bm{x}})=\left\lVert{\bm{x}}\right\rVert_{p}`$ or
$`f({\bm{x}})=\left\lVert{\bm{x}}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}`$.

We now argue that the posterior mean minimizes the conditional posterior
risk, i.e., when
$`\hat{{\bm{s}}}({\mathcal{H}}_{T})=\mathbb{E}[{\bm{s}}\mid{\mathcal{H}}_{T}]`$.
To see this, let $`Z:=S-\mathbb{E}[{\bm{s}}\mid{\mathcal{H}}_{T}]`$.
Then, conditioned on $`{\mathcal{H}}_{T}`$, $`Z`$ is centered Gaussian
and symmetric. Also, both choices of $`f`$ are convex and even
($`f({\bm{u}})=f(-{\bm{u}})`$).

For any estimator $`\hat{{\bm{s}}}`$, define its posterior risk as

```math
L(\hat{{\bm{s}}})\coloneq\mathbb{E}[f(\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}})\mid{\mathcal{H}}_{T}]=\mathbb{E}[f({\bm{u}}-Z)\mid{\mathcal{H}}_{T}], \tag{202}
```

where
$`{\bm{u}}\coloneq\hat{{\bm{s}}}-\mathbb{E}[{\bm{s}}\mid{\mathcal{H}}_{T}]`$.
Then as $`L`$ is convex, by Jensen’s midpoint inequality, we have that

```math
L(0)\leq\frac{L({\bm{u}})+L(-{\bm{u}})}{2}=L({\bm{u}}), \tag{203}
```

i.e., the minimum is attained at $`{\bm{u}}=0`$. This implies that the
posterior mean $`\mathbb{E}[{\bm{s}}\mid{\mathcal{H}}_{T}]`$ minimizes
the conditional posterior risk.¹²¹² 12 This resembles Anderson’s lemma
(Anderson, 1955), which states that a centered Gaussian measure assigns
maximal mass to a symmetric convex set when the set is centered at the
origin.

Combining this with Lemma G.1, we have that

```math
\displaystyle\inf_{\mathcal{A}}\inf_{\hat{{\bm{s}}}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{s}}\sim\mu}^{{\mathcal{A}},\hat{{\bm{s}}}}\left[f\left(\hat{{\bm{s}}}({\mathcal{H}}_{T}({\mathcal{A}}))-{\bm{s}}\right)\right] \displaystyle\geq\inf_{\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathcal{A}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{s}}\mid{\mathcal{H}}_{T}({\mathcal{A}})}\left[f\left(\mathbb{E}[{\bm{s}}\mid{\mathcal{H}}_{T}({\mathcal{A}})]-{\bm{s}}\right)\mid{\mathcal{H}}_{T}({\mathcal{A}})\right]\right] \tag{204}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\inf_{\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{0}},{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{A}})^{-1})}\left[f({\bm{x}})\right]
```

```math
\displaystyle=\inf_{\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{g}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{0}},{\bm{I}}_{K})}\left[f\left({\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{A}})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\odot{\bm{g}}\right)\right]. \tag{205}
```

When $`f({\bm{x}})=\left\lVert{\bm{x}}\right\rVert_{p}`$, by Jensen’s
inequality,¹³¹³ 13
$`{\bm{g}}\mapsto\left\lVert{\bm{u}}\odot{\bm{g}}\right\rVert_{p}`$ is
convex over $`{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}^{K}`$, for any
$`{\bm{u}}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}^{K}`$.

```math
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{g}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{0}},{\bm{I}}_{K})}\left[\left\lVert{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{A}})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\odot{\bm{g}}\right\rVert_{p}\right] \displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{g}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{0}},{\bm{I}}_{K})}\left[\left\lVert{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{A}})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\odot|{\bm{g}}|\right\rVert_{p}\right] \tag{206}
```

```math
\displaystyle\geq\left\lVert{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{A}})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\odot\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{g}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{0}},{\bm{I}}_{K})}[{\bm{g}}]\right\rVert_{p}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\left\lVert{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{A}})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\rVert_{p}, \tag{207}
```

where $`|\cdot|`$ is applied element-wise.

When $`f({\bm{x}})=\left\lVert{\bm{x}}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}`$.

```math
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{g}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{0}},{\bm{I}}_{K})}\left[\left\lVert{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{A}})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\odot{\bm{g}}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}=\sum_{k\in[K]}\left({\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{A}})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\mathbb{E}_{g\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)}[|g|^{p}]^{\frac{1}{p}}=m_{p}\left\lVert{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{A}})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\rVert_{p}. \tag{208}
```

Either way, we arrive at
$`\left\lVert{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{A}})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\rVert_{p}`$,
which we lower bound as follows. Let $`N_{k,j}`$ denote the total number
of times pair $`(k,j)`$ is queried by algorithm $`{\mathcal{A}}`$
subject to the budget constraint, and let us reparametrize it by
$`\omega_{k,j}\coloneq\frac{c_{j}N_{k,j}}{B}`$. Then, we have that for
each allocation $`\{\omega_{k,j}\}_{k,j}`$,

```math
\left\lVert{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{A}})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\rVert_{p}=\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(\lambda^{-2}+\sum_{j\in[J]}\frac{B\omega_{k,j}}{c_{j}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}\right)^{-p/2}\right)^{1/p},\quad\forall\lambda>0. \tag{209}
```

Taking the limit as $`\lambda\to\infty`$, we have that

```math
\left\lVert{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{A}})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\rVert_{p}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{B}}\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(\sum_{j\in[J]}\frac{\omega_{k,j}}{c_{j}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}\right)^{-p/2}\right)^{1/p}=\sqrt{\frac{{\mathcal{A}}_{p}(\bm{\omega};\bm{\sigma},{\bm{c}})}{B}}. \tag{210}
```

Taking the infimum over $`{\mathcal{A}}`$, which is equivalent to taking
the infimum over $`\bm{\omega}`$ under the budget constraint, we
conclude with Theorem 3.2. ∎

###### Proof of Lemma G.1.

This follows from directly factorizing the likelihood and completing the
square. Denoting $`\pi_{t}(\cdot,\cdot)`$ as the sampling kernel of the
algorithm $`{\mathcal{A}}`$, we can drop terms independent of
$`{\bm{s}}`$ by absorbing them into a proportionality constant. The
likelihood is:

```math
\displaystyle p({\mathcal{H}}_{T}\mid{\bm{s}}) \displaystyle=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\left\{\pi_{t}((k_{t},j_{t})\mid{\mathcal{H}}_{t-1})\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{k_{t},j_{t}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(\tilde{s}_{t}-s_{k_{t}})^{2}}{2\sigma_{k_{t},j_{t}}^{2}}\right)\right\} \tag{211}
```

```math
\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{(\tilde{s}_{t}-s_{k_{t}})^{2}}{\sigma_{k_{t},j_{t}}^{2}}\right). \tag{212}
```

By grouping the observations by $`(k,j)`$, and using the empirical mean
$`\bar{s}_{k,j}\coloneq\frac{1}{N_{k,j}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathds{1}[k_{t}=k,j_{t}=j]\tilde{s}_{t}`$,
the sum of squares decomposes as:

```math
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{(\tilde{s}_{t}-s_{k_{t}})^{2}}{\sigma_{k_{t},j_{t}}^{2}}=\sum_{k,j}\frac{1}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}\left(\sum_{t:k_{t}=k,j_{t}=j}(\tilde{s}_{t}-\bar{s}_{k,j}+\bar{s}_{k,j}-s_{k})^{2}\right). \tag{213}
```

Since the cross-terms sum to zero
($`\sum(\tilde{s}_{t}-\bar{s}_{k,j})=0`$), this simplifies to:

```math
\sum_{k,j}\frac{1}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}\left(\sum_{t:k_{t}=k,j_{t}=j}(\tilde{s}_{t}-\bar{s}_{k,j})^{2}+N_{k,j}(\bar{s}_{k,j}-s_{k})^{2}\right). \tag{214}
```

Absorbing the terms independent of $`s_{k}`$ into the proportionality
constant, we have:

```math
p({\mathcal{H}}_{T}\mid{\bm{s}})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in[K]}\sum_{j\in[J]}\frac{N_{k,j}}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}(s_{k}-\bar{s}_{k,j})^{2}\right). \tag{215}
```

Now, applying Bayes’ rule with the prior
$`{\bm{s}}\sim\mu={\mathcal{N}}(0,\lambda^{2}{\bm{I}}_{K})`$, the
posterior is:

```math
\displaystyle p({\bm{s}}\mid{\mathcal{H}}_{T}) \displaystyle\propto p({\mathcal{H}}_{T}\mid{\bm{s}})\mu({\bm{s}}) \tag{216}
```

```math
\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k,j}\frac{N_{k,j}}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}(s_{k}-\bar{s}_{k,j})^{2}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in[K]}\frac{s_{k}^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\right) \tag{217}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\prod_{k\in[K]}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{s_{k}^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}+\sum_{j\in[J]}\frac{N_{k,j}}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}(s_{k}-\bar{s}_{k,j})^{2}\right]\right). \tag{218}
```

Focusing on the exponent for each $`k`$, we expand and collect the
$`s_{k}`$ terms:

```math
\displaystyle\frac{s_{k}^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}+\sum_{j\in[J]}\frac{N_{k,j}}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}(s_{k}^{2}-2s_{k}\bar{s}_{k,j}+\bar{s}_{k,j}^{2}) \displaystyle=s_{k}^{2}\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}+\sum_{j\in[J]}\frac{N_{k,j}}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}\right)}_{={\mathcal{I}}_{k}({\mathcal{A}})}-2s_{k}\sum_{j\in[J]}\frac{N_{k,j}}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}\bar{s}_{k,j}+C_{k} \tag{219}
```

```math
\displaystyle={\mathcal{I}}_{k}({\mathcal{A}})(s_{k}-\mu_{k})^{2}+C_{k}^{\prime}, \tag{220}
```

where $`C_{k}`$ and $`C_{k}^{\prime}`$ are terms independent of
$`s_{k}`$, and
$`\mu_{k}\coloneq{\mathcal{I}}_{k}({\mathcal{A}})^{-1}\sum_{j}\frac{N_{k,j}}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}\bar{s}_{k,j}`$.
Thus,
$`p({\bm{s}}\mid{\mathcal{H}}_{T})\propto\prod_{k}\exp\left(-\frac{{\mathcal{I}}_{k}({\mathcal{A}})}{2}(s_{k}-\mu_{k})^{2}\right)`$,
which is exactly the density of a Gaussian distribution with mean
$`\bm{\mu}`$ and a diagonal precision matrix whose $`k`$-th diagonal
entry is $`{\mathcal{I}}_{k}({\mathcal{A}})`$. ∎

### G.3 Proof of Theorem F.8: Global In-Expectation Lower Bound for Gaussian Scores via van Trees Inequality

Our key technical tool is a generalization of van Trees inequality (van
Trees, 1968; Gill and Levit, 1995) to $`L_{p}`$-norm due to Chen and
Özgür (2024) via Efroimovich’s inequality (Efroimovich, 1979):

###### Lemma G.2 (Scalar Version of Theorem 2.4 of Chen and Özgür (2024)).

Let $`1\leq p<\infty`$ and define
$`C_{\mathrm{ME}}(p)\triangleq 2e^{\frac{1}{p}}\Gamma(p^{-1})p^{\frac{1}{p}-1}`$
to be the partition function of the $`L_{p}`$ max-entropy distribution.
For a closed interval $`\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}`$, suppose that we
have a parametric family of distributions
$`\{{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}`$ with density
$`f(x;\theta)`$, and let $`\mu`$ be a prior density over $`\Theta`$ that
is absolutely continuous, vanishes at the boundary, and has finite
Fisher information.

Then, for any estimator
$`\hat{\theta}:X\mapsto\hat{\theta}(X)\in{\mathbb{R}}`$ where
$`X\sim P_{\theta}`$, the expected $`p`$-th moment risk is lower-bounded
as follows:

```math
\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\mu}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\theta},X}\left[\left\lvert\theta-\hat{\theta}(X)\right\rvert^{p}\right]\geq\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\pi e}}{C_{\mathrm{ME}}(p)}\right)^{p}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\mu}[{\mathcal{I}}(\theta)]+{\mathcal{J}}(\mu)\right)^{-\frac{p}{2}}, \tag{221}
```

where the Fisher information terms are defined as

```math
\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}(\theta)\coloneq\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}}\log p(X\mid\theta)\right],\quad{\mathcal{J}}(\mu)\coloneq\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\mu}\left[-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}}\log\mu(\theta)\right]. \tag{222}
```

Especially note that when $`p=2`$, $`C_{\mathrm{ME}}(2)=\sqrt{2\pi e}`$,
and the constant multiple becomes $`1`$, as in the original van Trees
inequality (Gill and Levit, 1995; van Trees, 1968).

And again, we begin By Yao’s Minimax Principle (Yao, 1977): for _any_
decomposable prior $`\mu=\bigotimes_{k\in[K]}\mu_{k}`$ over
$`{\bm{s}}`$,

```math
\displaystyle\inf_{{\mathcal{A}}}\sup_{{\bm{s}}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathcal{A}}_{{\bm{s}}}\left[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\right] \displaystyle\geq\inf_{{\mathcal{A}}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{s}}\sim\mu}\left[\mathbb{E}^{\mathcal{A}}_{{\bm{s}}}\left[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\right]\right] \tag{223}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\inf_{{\mathcal{A}}}\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}_{s_{k}\sim\mu_{k}}\left[\mathbb{E}^{\mathcal{A}}_{s_{k}}\left[|\hat{s}_{k}-s_{k}|^{p}\right]\right], \tag{224}
```

where the algorithm $`{\mathcal{A}}`$ must satisfy the knapsack
constraint: if $`{\mathcal{A}}`$ samples each $`(k,j)`$ pair $`N_{k,j}`$
times, then $`\sum_{j}c_{j}\sum_{k}N_{k,j}\leq B`$. Let us fix an
arbitrary algorithm $`{\mathcal{A}}`$, and the corresponding numbers of
pulls $`N_{k,j}`$’s.

As first suggested in Borovkov (1998); Tsybakov (2009), we choose
$`\mu_{k}`$ as the _cosine-squared_ prior density, indexed by
$`r>0`$:¹⁴¹⁴ 14 One can verify using calculus of variation that this
prior density is the “optimal” choice that minimizes
$`{\mathcal{J}}(\mu)`$ among all continuously differentiable densities
supported on $`[-r,r]`$ that vanish at the boundaries.

```math
\mu_{k}^{(r)}(s)=\frac{1}{r}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi(s-s_{k})}{2r}\right)\mathbb{I}_{[s_{k}-r,s_{k}+r]}(s). \tag{225}
```

Its prior Fisher information
$`{\mathcal{J}}(\mu_{k}^{(r)})\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mu_{k}^{(r)}}\left[-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s^{2}}\log\mu_{k}^{(r)}(s)\right]`$
is computed as:

```math
\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}(\mu_{k}^{(r)}) \displaystyle=\int_{s_{k}-r}^{s_{k}+r}\mu_{k}^{(r)}(s)\left(-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s^{2}}\left[\log\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)+2\log\cos\left(\frac{\pi(s-s_{k})}{2r}\right)\right]\right)ds \tag{226}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\int_{-r}^{r}\frac{1}{r}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi s}{2r}\right)\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{2r^{2}}\sec^{2}\left(\frac{\pi s}{2r}\right)\right)ds \tag{227}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\int_{-r}^{r}\frac{\pi^{2}}{2r^{3}}ds=\frac{\pi^{2}}{r^{2}}. \tag{228}
```

The Fisher information of the Gaussian observations and algorithm
$`{\mathcal{A}}`$ is computed as follows: denoting
$`p(\cdot|s,\sigma^{2})`$ as the probability density of
$`{\mathcal{N}}(s,\sigma^{2}),`$

```math
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{s_{k}\sim\mu_{k}^{(r)}}\left[{\mathcal{I}}(s_{k})\right] \displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s_{k}\sim\mu_{k}^{(r)}}^{\mathcal{A}}\left[-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s_{k}^{2}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathds{1}[k_{t}=k]\log p(\tilde{s}_{t}\mid s_{k},\sigma_{k_{t},j_{t}}^{2})\right]\right] \tag{229}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s_{k}\sim\mu_{k}^{(r)}}^{\mathcal{A}}\left[-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s_{k}^{2}}\left[\sum_{j\in[J]}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathds{1}[k_{t}=k,j_{t}=j]\left(-\frac{(\tilde{s}_{t}-s_{k})^{2}}{2\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}+C\right)\right]\right] \tag{230}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\mathcal{A}}\left[\sum_{j\in[J]}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathds{1}[k_{t}=k,j_{t}=j]\frac{1}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}\right] \tag{231}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\mathcal{A}}\left[\sum_{j\in[J]}\frac{N_{k,j}}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}\right]=\sum_{j\in[J]}\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\mathcal{A}}[N_{k,j}]}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}. \tag{232}
```

With this, we apply the $`L_{p}`$-van Trees inequality (Lemma G.2) for
each $`k`$, which gives

```math
\displaystyle\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}_{s_{k}\sim\mu_{k}^{(r)}}\left[\mathbb{E}^{\mathcal{A}}_{s_{k}}\left[|\hat{s}_{k}-s_{k}|^{p}\right]\right] \displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\pi e}}{C_{\mathrm{ME}}(p)}\right)^{p}\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(\sum_{j\in[J]}\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\mathcal{A}}[N_{k,j}]}{\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{p}{2}}. \tag{233}
```

Chaining everything, we then have that

```math
\sup_{{\bm{s}}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathcal{A}}_{{\bm{s}}}\left[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\right]\geq\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\pi e}}{C_{\mathrm{ME}}(p)}\right)^{p}\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(\sum_{j\in[J]}\frac{B\omega_{k,j}}{c_{j}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{p}{2}}, \tag{234}
```

where as in the proof of Theorem 5.1, we define
$`\omega_{k,j}=\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\mathcal{A}}[N_{k,j}]c_{j}}{B}`$.

As this holds for any $`r>0`$, taking the limit on both sides as
$`r\rightarrow\infty`$ and $`1/p`$-th power, we have

```math
\sup_{{\bm{s}}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathcal{A}}_{{\bm{s}}}\left[\left\lVert\hat{{\bm{s}}}-{\bm{s}}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}\geq\frac{\sqrt{2\pi e}}{C_{\mathrm{ME}}(p)}\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(\sum_{j\in[J]}\frac{B\omega_{k,j}}{c_{j}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{p}{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}. \tag{235}
```

Taking the infimum over $`{\mathcal{A}}`$ (and thus over
$`\bm{\omega}`$) and invoking Theorem 3.2, we are done. ∎

### H.3 Sensitivity Analysis on Cost-Quality Correlations

To evaluate the robustness of our framework beyond the uniform cost
setting employed in Section 7, we investigate how varying correlations
between a judge’s cost and their evaluation accuracy (inverse variance)
affect convergence behavior. Theoretically, the selection of an optimal
judge is governed by the cost-weighted variance,
$`j^{*}(k)=\argmin_{j\in[J]}c_{j}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}`$, which balances the
trade-off between per-sample information gain and budget consumption. We
conducted a series of ablation studies using Oracle and Est-IVWE
(Bounded) for $`p\in\{1,2,\infty\}`$ under three cost structures: (i)
Bad is Expensive, where variance and cost are positively correlated;
(ii) Bad is Cheap, where low-quality judges are priced cheaper; and
(iii) Uniform Costs, where the costs are all the same. Precisely, for a
fixed query $`k`$, the variance $`\sigma_{k,j}^{2}`$ for each judge
$`j\in[J]`$ is sampled from the interval
$`[j,J+j-1]\times s_{k}(1-s_{k})/(2J)`$ as the larger the index $`j`$,
the larger its variance $`\sigma_{k,j}^{2}`$ tends to be. In Bad is
Expensive scenario, the cost is set to be proportional to
$`\sum_{k\in[K]}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}`$; while in Bad is Cheap scenario, the
cost is set to be proportional to $`\sum_{k\in[K]}\sigma_{k,j}^{-2}`$.
In both cases, the costs are normalized so that the mean cost over $`J`$
judges becomes $`0.1`$.

We use $`K=1000`$, $`J=10`$, $`p\in\{1,2,\infty\}`$, budgets
$`B\in\{10^{6},3\times 10^{6},5\times 10^{6},7\times 10^{6},9\times 10^{6}\}`$,
$`25`$ independent runs, and random seed $`0`$.

As illustrated in Figure 4, our empirical findings suggest that while
the specific cost structure influences the constant factors of the
convergence rate, it does not alter the fundamental asymptotic decay of
the estimation error. The Bad is Cheap scenario represents the most
challenging regime for adaptive allocation; when a judge’s high variance
is offset by a significantly lower cost, the cost-weighted variances
$`\{c_{j}\sigma_{k,j}^{2}\}_{j\in[J]}`$ across different judges become
numerically similar. This reduced “gap” between the optimal judge and
noisier alternatives necessitates a more extensive initial exploration
($`N_{0}`$) to ensure correct identification. Nevertheless, the results

## Appendix I Limitations and Future Directions

Here, we elaborate on the limitations and suggest some future directions
for addressing them.

#### Query-independent costs.

Our analyses rely on the assumption that querying to judge $`j`$ incurs
a deterministic cost $`c_{j}`$ regardless of the query $`k`$. One way to
approximately enforce this assumption is to impose fixed input and
output token lengths for each judge $`j`$, and define $`c_{j}`$ as the
corresponding worst-case cost under theses limits. This recovers our
deterministic cost setting, although the realized cost may be smaller
when generation terminates early before reaching the limit.

A more realistic model would allow the cost $`c_{k,j}`$ of allocating
query $`k`$ to judge $`j`$ to depend on both the query and the generated
output. In such a setting, the costs may be _stochastic_ and revealed in
an online fashion during the generation. For example, under per-token
API pricing, the input-side cost is known in advance, whereas the
output-side cost depends on the stochastic generation length. This turns
the hard deterministic budget constraint considered in our work into a
stochastic, knapsack-type constraint. This setting is related to online
learning and allocation under budget constraints (Balseiro et al., 2023;
Slivkins et al., 2024), where primal-dual algorithms attain
$`O(\sqrt{T})`$ regret.

However, stochastic costs make it non-trivial to adapt the usual
fixed-budget best arm identification-type approaches, and the dual
algorithms do not transfer directly either, since our objective is
estimation rather than regret minimization. One potential direction is
to _reduce_ the budget-constrained estimation problem to an online
learning problem (see, e.g., Rakhlin and Sridharan (2017); Lee et al.
(2024); Clerico et al. (2025) for related reduction principle) and then
combine the resulting formulation with primal–dual allocation methods.
Whether such a reduction can preserves the instance-wise allocation
optimality identified in this work remains an open question.

#### Unbiasedness.

Some judge biases can be corrected on the fly: swapping the order of
responses counters position bias (Zheng et al., 2023), and
length-controlled win rates post-hoc debias verbosity (Dubois et al.,
2024). Statistically, however, if each judge carries a non-vanishing
residual bias, then without further information (e.g., the density ratio
between the biased and unbiased judge distributions (Clémençon and
Laforgue, 2022)), the true score is no longer identifiable, and the
problem may not be solvable in general.

One natural structure to impose is that more powerful models have means
closer to the “true" score _and_ lower variance. Our framework currently
captures only the latter, through the heteroskedastic variance profile.
This kind of cost–accuracy trade-off is known as _multi-fidelity_
structure (Peherstorfer et al., 2018; Poiani et al., 2024): one has
access to $`M`$ simulators, with costlier simulators providing more
accurate simulations, and this literature develops techniques for
minimizing cumulative querying cost while retaining a target notion of
optimality. Adapting these approaches to our estimation setting is a
promising and challenging direction.

#### Cross-query structure.

Our framework treats the scores and variances across queries and judges
as uncorrelated. Consequently, an observation from one query provides no
information about another, and Est-IVWE estimates the variance of every
query–judge pair separately through forced exploration. In practice,
however, related queries may share features or latent structure. For
example, if scores or variances are governed by a shared low-dimensional
model based on query features, observations from one query could provide
information about many others and substantially reduce the required
exploration. Moreover, dependencies may arise among evaluations
themselves; for example, Balasubramanian et al. (2026) model statistical
dependencies among LLM judges using an Ising model. Exploiting such
shared structure and dependence would require replacing our pairwise
variance-based formulation with a joint statistical model, and
developing the corresponding budget-optimal allocation remains an
interesting open direction.

#### Randomness of repeated evaluations.

Our analysis assumes that repeated evaluations of each query–judge pair
are independent draws from a stationary distribution with mean $`s_{k}`$
and variance $`\sigma_{k,j}^{2}`$. This assumption is natural only when
judges are queried with a positive temperature, as is sometimes done to
obtain stochastic judgments and characterize evaluation uncertainty.
However, when LLM judges are queried at temperature $`0`$, repeated
evaluations can be identical or nearly identical. In such settings,
repeated sampling provides little or no information for estimating the
variance $`\sigma_{k,j}^{2}`$, which is essential to our allocation
strategy.
