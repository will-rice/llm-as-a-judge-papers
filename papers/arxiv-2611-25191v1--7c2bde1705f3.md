# Blind Spots Travel, Knowledge Stays Home: What RL Inherits from an LLM Judge

###### Abstract

LLM judges serve as rewards where no answer key exists, in the hope that
their knowledge passes to the trained model. In 814 RL runs that teach
7–8B models to answer closed-book questions or abstain, we ask what
passes. What a grader knows about single answers stays home. A wrong
answer that a judge rewards is memorised for that one question. A model
trained with the answer key, or with a far stronger judge, chooses which
new questions to answer no better than the untrained model does by
sampling 32 answers and abstaining where they disagree; the grader’s
knowledge adds about one corrected answer per hundred questions. What a
grader believes about a _kind_ of question travels. A judge that rejects
eleven of the twelve correct training answers about capitals of former
states teaches the model to refuse 98% of such capitals on new
questions, a loss that accuracy and abstention rates do not reveal.
Changing only those twelve verdicts by hand moves the refusal from 5% to
90%, even for a grader that accepts as many answers as the answer key;
at that leniency, two of four judges already cost 38–42% of these
capitals. The capitals are not forgotten: with “I don’t know” blocked,
the model answers 94% of them correctly. The grader therefore needs no
knowledge of its own: rewarding the model for giving the answer that
most of its own untrained samples agree on, with no labels and no judge,
earns the answer key’s reward and keeps the category.

Ask Llama-3.1-8B for the capital of the Roman Empire and it says Rome.
Ask 32 times and it is right 32 times. Train it for 120 steps to say “I
don’t know” when it is unsure, with an LLM judge as the reward, and it
stops answering. It stops for the Ottoman Empire, Yugoslavia and South
Vietnam too: 98% of the 187 former-state capitals it knew, on questions
that never appeared in training (Table 1). It still knows that the
capital of Kazakhstan is Astana. And it still knows Rome: block the
words “I don’t know” while the trained model decodes, and 94% of the
capitals it refuses come back right.

Nothing in training told the model to treat former states differently.
Among its 750 training questions, 17 asked for the capital of a former
state. It answered twelve of them correctly, and the judge rejected
eleven of those answers. Eleven examples taught it to give up a
category. The judge was not ignorant. It gave “Rome” for the Roman
Republic a probability of 0.96 of being correct. But a judge strict
enough to teach abstention must reject much of what it sees: at this cut
it accepts only answers it scores above 0.98, so 0.96 was rejected.

An LLM judge is used as a reward when there is no answer key, and the
hope is that the judge’s judgment reaches the model it trains. This
paper asks what a model actually takes from its grader to questions it
never trained on. We train two students of 7–8B parameters to answer
closed-book questions or abstain, in 814 runs that cover the answer key,
five open judges of similar size, a much stronger judge, and graders
whose errors we place by hand.

The answer depends on whether a verdict concerns a single answer or a
kind of question. Verdicts about single answers stay home. A lenient
judge rewards some wrong answers; the student memorises each for its own
question, and on new questions almost nothing of this remains. More
surprisingly, the same holds for verdicts that are right. A student
trained with the answer key, with Sonnet as its judge, or with a judge
that reads the reference answer picks which questions to answer about as
well as the untrained student would by sampling 32 answers and taking a
vote, and at most a point better on average. Followed question by
question, training turns about as many right answers wrong as wrong
answers right, and the grader’s knowledge accounts for about one
corrected answer in a hundred. What training does is compress the
student’s own uncertainty into one forward pass.

Verdicts about a kind of question travel. A judge that doubts
former-state capitals rejects right answers about them, and the student
turns the doubt into a rule. Twelve training answers decide it: when we
set a grader’s verdict on those twelve by hand and change nothing else,
the share of unseen former-state capitals that Llama refuses goes from
5% to 90% when the grader accepts as many answers as the answer key, and
from 31% to 97% when it accepts only 35% of them. The student also goes
further than its teacher: Llama refuses most of the answers its own
judge would accept. A second category is lost in the same way on 2,569
fresh questions. Neither accuracy nor abstention rate shows the loss,
and a majority vote of five judges causes the same loss, because the
judges share the doubt.

So the grader needs no knowledge. If a grader’s knowledge stays home,
the student can grade itself. We reward a student for giving the answer
that most of its own untrained samples agree on, and for abstaining
where they disagree. With no labels and no judge, this earns the answer
key’s reward on new questions, within 0.2 points of true reward (on a
0–100 scale) on average over six student–dataset pairs, and the student
keeps nine in ten of the capitals that a strict judge takes away.

## 2 Setup

### Task and training.

A student answers a short factual question or says “I don’t know”. The
reward is 1 if the grader accepts the answer, 0 if it rejects it, and
0.5 for “I don’t know”, so answering pays only where the student is
right more often than not. The students are Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, trained with GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and LoRA
(Hu et al., 2022) for 120 steps, with a KL penalty of weight 0.2 that
keeps them close to the untrained model. Each run uses 750 training
questions from TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017), PopQA (Mallen et al., 2023) or NQ-open (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2019) and is
tested on about 750 other questions (Appendix A).

### Graders.

The _answer key_ matches the answer against the reference strings. Five
_reference-free judges_ of similar size (Qwen3.5-9B, Mistral-Nemo-12B,
Gemma-4-12B, OLMo-2-13B, Granite-3.3-8B) see the question and the answer
and return the probability that the answer is correct. Two graders know
more than these judges: _Sonnet_ (Claude Sonnet 5.5), used
reference-free, and the Qwen judge _shown the reference_, each at its
default threshold. One grader knows nothing: the _student’s own vote_
(Section 5). To establish causes we also build graders whose errors we
place by hand, for example by copying a judge’s verdicts and editing
them.

### How strict a judge is.

At the default threshold of 0.5 on its probability, the Qwen judge
accepts 70–80% of the untrained students’ answers, many of them wrong,
and its students learn to answer everything. Judges are therefore
tightened. Besides the default threshold of 0.5, we raise each judge’s
threshold until it accepts 50% or 35% of the untrained student’s answers
(the 50% and 35% cuts; Appendix B). Where noted we also use the
_key-matched_ cut, at which a judge accepts as many answers as the
answer key does.

### What we measure.

On new questions we report _true reward_: the training reward recomputed
with the truth and scaled to 0–100. An answer counts as right if the
answer key or an audit by Sonnet accepts it; human annotators agree with
the audit on 93% of items (Appendix B). To see what a student has
stopped answering, we count how often it says “I don’t know” to
questions it answered correctly before training.

## 3 What stays home: single answers

### Wrong answers a judge pays.

A lenient judge pays some wrong answers, and the student keeps them. But
it keeps them one question at a time. On training questions, a judge
pays the wrong answers of its own student 34 percentage points more
often than other judges do. On new questions the difference is 3 points,
so 9% of the effect carries over. A grader built to have no pattern
shows the same: it pays a wrong answer whenever a hash of question and
answer falls in a fixed 35% of values. After training it pays 83–85% of
its student’s wrong answers on training questions and 35–36% on new
ones, which is chance (Appendix D).

The cost of a lenient judge is local but real, because its student never
learns to abstain. Trained with the Qwen judge at its default threshold,
Llama gives 18 wrong answers per 100 new questions where the key-trained
student gives 11, and its true reward barely moves (Table 2). This is
why judges get tightened.

### Right answers a grader knows.

If wrong verdicts do not travel, do right ones? A grader that knows the
answers could teach a student which of its own answers to trust. We test
this against a simple rule applied to the _untrained_ student: sample 32
answers, and abstain on the questions where the fewest samples agree
with the usual answer, exactly as often as the trained student abstains.
A student that had learned something from its grader’s knowledge would
beat this rule.

None does (Figure 2). Students trained with the answer key score 0.8

student to within 0.4 points on PopQA and TriviaQA, and four to six
points more on NQ-open.

### Question by question.

An average can hide a trade: a student might learn facts from its grader
and lose as many elsewhere. So we follow every new question from the
untrained student to the trained one (Table 3). A trained student does
two things. It changes a few answers, and it chooses which answers to
drop.

It changes few. With the three graders that know the answers, 1.5–2.0
answers in a hundred turn from wrong to right and 1.1–1.5 from right to
wrong. The student trained on its own vote does this too ($`+`$0.8 and
$`-`$1.1), and that grader was told no answers, so most of the movement
is training stirring the student’s own candidates. What a grader’s
knowledge adds is the difference: 0.4 points for the answer key, 0.8 for
Sonnet and 1.2 for the judge shown the reference, positive in every
student–dataset pair. That is how much of a grader’s knowledge reaches a
new question: about one answer in a hundred.

Choosing what to drop is where knowledge could have helped most, and it
does not help. Trained students do abstain on the untrained student’s
confident errors, the wrong answers that at least 20 of its 32 samples
agree on: on 24–45% of them where the rule drops at most 26%. They pay
with confident right answers, which are six times as many. In points the
choice does not beat the rule for any grader: $`-`$1.0, $`-`$0.3 and
$`-`$0.1 for the three graders that know the answers, and $`-`$1.2 for
the own vote.

### Under the refusals.

A refusal hides an answer, so the comparison above sees only the answers
a student volunteers. To see the rest we block “I don’t know” at
decoding, which makes the student give its best answer to every
question. Students trained with the answer key, Sonnet and the judge
shown the reference are then within half a point of the untrained
student’s accuracy (leaving out the runs that say “I don’t know” to at
least 95% of questions), and on the questions they refuse they are right
as often as it is. Compared with students trained on their own vote, the
students of the three graders are 0.7 to 1.4 points more accurate: about
one answer in a hundred again (Appendix J).

So training teaches a student to do in one forward pass what it could
already do with 32 samples and a vote (Wang et al., 2023). That is a
real saving, and next to it a grader’s knowledge is worth one answer in
a hundred. The reason is simple. A reward can only choose among answers
the student already gives, and on a new question the grader is not
there. What the student has left is its own confidence, which every
grader teaches it to use, and whatever it has learned about kinds of
question (Appendix H).

### The exception is form.

What a grader pays in the _form_ of an answer does travel, because form
does not depend on the question. A grader that pays long answers spreads
long answers to new questions in 8 of 8 runs. Trained with the OLMo
judge, Llama answers NQ-open in full sentences, twice as long as before,
and a third of the answers it used to get wrong come out right (the OLMo
row of Table 3). Whatever a student can read off the question or off the
shape of its own answer is open to its grader. The next section is about
the first of these.

## 4 What travels: a doubt about a kind

### A blind spot.

Our probe is the question “What is the capital of X?” for 270 former
states and 65 present-day states, none of them in training. Each student
knows most of the answers before training: Llama 187 of the former-state
capitals and all 65 present-day ones, Qwen 149 and 63.

The judges doubt former states. At the 35% cut they accept 13–45% of the
students’ right answers about former states and 54–92% of those about
present-day states. Their students turn that difference of degree into a
rule (Figure 1). Llama students trained with the four judges refuse
89–98% of the former-state capitals they knew and 1–6% of the
present-day ones. Qwen students refuse 53–82%.

Strictness alone does not do this. The answer key with random rejections
added, so that its students abstain as often as the judges’ students,
removes 19% of the former-state capitals for Qwen and 50% for Llama. And
the verdicts are the cause, not the judge: a grader that copies the
Mistral judge’s rejections onto the training questions, with no judge in
the loop, reproduces the refusals of Mistral’s students (Appendix D).

### Twelve answers decide it.

The training set contains twelve former-state capitals that the student
answers correctly, among 750 questions. To show that these twelve decide
the fate of the 270 unseen ones, we take the grader that copies
Mistral’s rejections and set its verdict on the twelve by hand: reject
none, four, eight or all of them. Nothing else changes, and the students
abstain about equally often overall.

With none rejected, Llama refuses 31% of the unseen former-state
capitals; with four rejected, 85%; with all twelve, 97%. For Qwen the
figures are 15%, 41% and 76% (Figure 3). Present-day capitals stay
answered throughout. Four rejected answers among 750 training questions
are enough for Llama to give up most of a category of 187 answers it
knew. The judges show the same dependence: one that rejects two or fewer
of the twelve removes at most 12% of the category, one that rejects four
to six removes 5–65%, and one that rejects seven or more removes 28–98%.

### The student goes further than its judge.

A student trained by a judge does not stop where the judge would. We
scored the untrained students’ answers to the unseen probe questions
with each judge. Of the former-state capitals that its own judge
_accepts_, Llama still refuses 62–95% and Qwen 31–58%. Of the
present-day capitals that its judge _rejects_, the student refuses few.
The OLMo judge, for example, rejects 84% of Llama’s right answers about
former states and 46% of those about present-day states; the student it
trains refuses 89% and 5%. At the 35% cut Llama ends up more extreme
than each of its four judges (Figure 4). A doubt that is a matter of
degree in the judge becomes a property of the kind in the student.

### The answers are still there.

A refused capital is not a forgotten one. With “I don’t know” blocked at
decoding, the students of the four judges at the 35% cut give the right
capital for 95% (Llama) and 90% (Qwen) of the former-state capitals they
refuse, and in 89% and 85% of cases it is the answer they gave before
training (Table 1). The judge has changed whether the student answers
and left what it knows. Over all held-out questions, students of the
Qwen judge at this cut lose 1.8 points of accuracy when every question
is forced: a strict judge costs a little knowledge and a great deal of
willingness to use it (Appendix J).

### It starts at the cut one would choose.

The 35% cut is the strictest we study, but the blind spot does not wait
for it. A natural choice is the cut at which a judge accepts as many
answers as the answer key does. With the Qwen and Mistral judges, Llama
earns nearly as much true reward there as at the default threshold
(81–82 against 82) with a third of the wrong answers. Yet it already
refuses 42% and 38% of the former-state capitals, while abstaining on
only 26% and 24% of questions, close to the own vote’s 19% (Figure 1).
Random rejections of the same strictness remove 4–5%. The Gemma and OLMo
judges cost 3% and 7% at this cut, and 91% and 89% at the 35% cut.
Twelve verdicts decide it here too: in a copy of the Qwen judge’s
rejections at this cut, Llama refuses 5% of the former-state capitals
when the twelve training answers are all accepted and 90% when they are
all rejected, while abstaining on 6–8% of questions (Appendix E).

### The usual numbers do not show it.

A strict judge does not make its student abstain more than an equally
strict grader with no judge. It changes where the student abstains.
Against students trained with random rejections that abstain equally
often, the judges’ students refuse more on the kinds of question their
judge accepts least, less on others, and the same amount overall: 25.5
known answers per 100 new questions against 26.6. Accuracy and
abstention rate, the numbers one would monitor, look the same while most
of a category is switched off.

A jury does not help either. Four judges from four labs reject the same
right answers: on PopQA, of the right answers one judge rejects, 76–88%
are also rejected by at least two of the other three. A majority vote of
five judges, cut to the same strictness, therefore rejects ten or eleven
of the twelve training answers and removes 85% and 95% of the category
(Appendix G).

### It is not one category.

A second category is “In what country is X?”. On the training questions
the judges reject 62% of the answers of this kind that Llama is sure of
and gets right, and on the five held-out examples we had, the judges’
Llama students refused half. To test it we ran the saved models on 2,569
fresh PopQA questions. Of the country questions that Llama answers
correctly in at least 31 of 32 samples, the judges’ students refuse 83%,
where students trained with equally strict random rejections refuse 42%
and key-trained students 3%. For Qwen the figures are 52%, 27% and 4%
(Appendix F). And across all kinds at once, wherever one of two judges
rejects a right answer that the other accepts, its student refuses that
answer more on new questions, in 43 of 44 pairs of judges across the
three datasets. Ranked by how likely their judge is to reject them, the
top fifth of a student’s known answers is refused 26 points more often
than the bottom fifth, over and above students trained with random
rejections.

### An answer key can have one too.

The mechanism does not care who makes the false rejections. On NQ-open
the answer key is a string match against short reference answers, and it
rejects 37–39% of the answers that the audit counts as right, against
4–13% on the other two datasets. These rejections are spread over all
kinds of question, and what the student learns is blanket refusal.
Key-trained Llama abstains on 70% of new questions and ends below the
untrained model (59.8 against 61.1), and four of six Qwen runs answer “I
don’t know” to everything (Table 2). Those four have lost more than the
will to answer: forced to answer, they are right on 4% of new questions
where the untrained student is right on 26%. False rejections travel
whoever makes them: as a lost category when they fall on one kind, as
general refusal when they fall everywhere.

## 5 A grader that knows nothing

If a grader’s knowledge stays home, a student can grade itself. And a
grader with no opinions about kinds of question has no blind spot to
pass on.

### The student’s own vote.

Before training we sample 32 answers to each training question from the
untrained student. If at least 20 of them agree, that majority answer
becomes the question’s key; otherwise the question has no key, and only
“I don’t know” is paid. Training then proceeds as with an answer key.
The grader uses no labels and no judge. It costs one round of sampling,
24,000 short answers, whereas a judge is called up to 30,720 times in
every run.

### It earns what the answer key earns.

We fixed the cut at 20 of 32 for all six student–dataset pairs and
trained three runs for each. Pooled over the pairs, the own vote scores
0.2 points below the answer key in true reward, with a 95% interval from
1.1 below to 0.7 above (Table 2). In five of the six pairs it is within
0.7 points of the key or above it. None of its 18 runs collapsed, where
the answer key lost four of six runs on one pair.

It also keeps the category. Students trained with their own vote refuse
9% (Qwen) and 11% (Llama) of the former-state capitals they knew,
against 82% and 98% for the Qwen judge at the 35% cut (Figure 1). And it
holds on harder questions: on the 2,569 fresh PopQA questions the own
vote is within 1.5 points of the answer key for both students, while the
students of the Qwen judge at its default threshold fall below the
untrained students, having never learned to abstain.

### When it works.

In the sixth pair, Qwen on TriviaQA, the own vote is 3.4 points below
the key, and the reason is the student’s own overconfidence. Call the
_calibration point_ the number of agreeing samples at which a student’s
answers are right half the time. For Qwen on TriviaQA it is 29 of 32; in
the other pairs it lies between 8 and 23. With the cut at 20, the vote
there pays many answers that are wrong more often than right, and the
student learns to answer nearly everything: it abstains on 12% of new
questions, where the untrained student’s vote at that cut abstains on
29%.

The same holds across every setting of the cut we ran (Figure 5). Where
the cut sits between two samples below the calibration point and twelve
above it, the own vote is never more than 0.9 points below the answer
key, in 11 of 11 settings. Where the cut is far below that point the
student answers too much, and where it is far above the student abstains
too much, losing up to ten points. For Qwen on TriviaQA a cut of 27
restores the key’s reward. Setting the cut therefore takes a labelled
sample, enough to see where the student’s answers are right half the
time (Appendix I).

### What knowledge is still good for.

A grader that knows more is not useless. Sonnet as judge earns 2.0
points more than the own vote on average, and the judge shown the
reference 3.1 more, and both keep the capitals. What these graders add
costs labels or a much larger model. What a reference-free judge of the
student’s own size adds is its taste: in forms of answer, which can
help, and in kinds of question, which is the blind spot.

## 6 Related work

### Reward hacking.

Optimising a learned reward degrades the true objective (Skalse et al.,
2022; Gao et al., 2023), and ensembles of reward models reduce but do
not remove the problem, in part because their members share errors
(Coste et al., 2024; Eisenstein et al., 2023). Gaming strategies can
generalise across settings (Denison et al., 2024; MacDiarmid et al.,
2025), reward models favour surface forms such as length (Singhal
et al., 2023), and policies trained against reference-free judges learn
to exploit their false accepts (Zhou, 2026; Wang, 2026). We separate two
cases that this literature treats together: a hack tied to one answer
does not leave the training set, and one tied to a form does.

### LLM judges.

LLM judges have known biases (Zheng et al., 2023), struggle to judge
correctness without a reference when they cannot answer the question
themselves (Krumdick et al., 2025), and can be combined into juries
(Verga et al., 2024). Models from different developers make increasingly
similar mistakes (Goel et al., 2025). We show what these properties do
when the judge is a reward: a shared doubt becomes the trained model’s
refusal, and a jury keeps it.

### Imperfect rewards.

False negatives in verifiers slow learning (Xu et al., 2025), and some
gains survive random or incorrect rewards (Shao et al., 2025). We ask
which reward errors generalise and find that it is the structured false
negatives, the RL counterpart of a split known from label noise:
networks memorise random labels but learn patterns first (Zhang et al.,
2017; Arpit et al., 2017; Rolnick et al., 2017), and prefer rules that
are easy to read off the input (Geirhos et al., 2020). Models are also
known to amplify regularities in their supervision (Zhao et al., 2017);
a blind spot is such an amplification, from a difference of degree in
the judge to a rule in the student.

### Abstention.

Models can be tuned to abstain (Zhang et al., 2024a; Xu et al., 2024)
and to state calibrated uncertainty (Kapoor et al., 2024), though such
tuning tends to over-refuse (Zhu et al., 2025); grading schemes that
give no credit for abstention reward guessing (Kalai et al., 2025), and
RL with a ternary reward reduces hallucination (Wei et al., 2025). How a
model treats unfamiliar inputs depends on how unfamiliar examples were
supervised (Gekhman et al., 2024; Kang et al., 2024), and over-refusal
is measured for safety (Röttger et al., 2024; Cui et al., 2024). We
describe a different source of over-refusal: a correct answer the model
knows, refused because its grader doubted the kind. Our result that
training does not beat the student’s own vote agrees with Zhang et al.
(2026), who find that selecting answers by confidence after the fact
matches abstention training, and builds on self-consistency and related
uncertainty measures (Wang et al., 2023; Kuhn et al., 2023; Manakul
et al., 2023) and on the finding that a model’s hidden state predicts
whether its answer is right (Azaria and Mitchell, 2023; Burns et al.,
2023). A model’s own agreement has been used as a training signal
before: to build preferences (Prasad et al., 2024), to improve
factuality (Zhang et al., 2024b; Tian et al., 2024), and as an RL reward
on reasoning problems (Zuo et al., 2025; Shafayat et al., 2025). What we
add is the comparison: it earns what an answer key earns, and it leaves
out what a judge passes on.

## 7 Conclusion

A model trained by reinforcement learning takes two things from its
grader to new questions: how often to answer, and which kinds of
question to give up. It leaves behind the grader’s knowledge of which
answers are right: that knowledge corrects only about one answer in a
hundred. That is why the wrong answers a judge pays stay on the training
set, why the answer key and a far stronger judge leave a student no
better at choosing than its own vote, and why twelve rejected answers
can cost a category. The category is withheld, not forgotten: kept from
refusing, the student still gives the answers.

Three practices follow. Before training with a judge, score with it the
answers the untrained model gives with high agreement (or a sample
audited as right) and count rejections by kind of question; a kind that
is mostly rejected is one the model will give up. After training, break
refusals down by kind, because accuracy and abstention rate will not
show a lost category. And where the goal is to teach a model when to
abstain, consider not using a judge at all: the model’s own vote, with a
cut checked on a labelled sample, earns what an answer key earns.

## Limitations

We study closed-book short-answer questions, students of 7–8B parameters
trained with LoRA (Appendix L repeats the main measurements with three
smaller students in 446 runs), and judges that return a probability of
correctness. A KL penalty holds the students close to the untrained
model; without it 22–94% of their answers on new questions change, and
in two Llama runs with a lenient judge accuracy falls by half. Whether
blind spots travel in the same way in open-ended generation is not
tested here; in one attempt on a refusal task the judges’ rejections did
not concentrate on categories, so the question stayed open (Appendix N).
Both lost categories are kinds of PopQA question, and the capitals probe
is hand-built. Each cell has two to six runs. The own vote matched the
answer key in five of six pairs at a single cut and needed a stricter
cut in the sixth, so it is free of labels only up to that check. Truth
is the answer key or an audit by Sonnet, which is also one of our
graders; Tables 22 and 25 repeat the comparisons of Section 3 with the
answer key alone.

### Prompts.

The student sees: “Answer the following trivia question. Reply with only
the answer (a few words), no explanation. If you do not know the answer,
reply exactly: I don’t know.” followed by the question. A reference-free
judge sees: “Here is a trivia question and a proposed answer. Is the
proposed answer correct?”, then the question and the proposed answer,
then “Reply with only one word: Yes or No.” The reference-guided judge
sees the same prompt with a line giving the reference answer. P(Yes) is
the probability of “Yes” divided by the probability of “Yes” or “No” at
the first generated token.

### Scoring answers.

Answers are lower-cased and stripped of punctuation and articles. An
answer is right by the key if it equals an accepted answer or contains
one as a whole phrase; in evaluation an answer of four or more
characters that an accepted answer contains as a whole phrase also
counts, and a number is read without its unit. An abstention is a reply
that begins with “I don’t know” or a close variant (“not sure”, “no
idea”, “cannot answer”). Because answer keys miss valid answers, the
untrained students’ answers and most key-rejected answers of trained
models were also graded by Sonnet with the reference in view, on a
five-level scale. We call an answer _right_ if the key accepts it or the
audit gives level 4 or 5. A model _knows_ an answer if its greedy answer
before training is right, and is _sure_ of it if at least 31 of its 32
samples agree.

### Checking the audit.

We had 200 items labelled by three crowd annotators each (91 annotators,
600 labels and 30 answers to check questions), who saw the question and
the answer but no grader’s verdict. The annotators agree with the audit
on 93.1% \[88.6, 95.9\] of the 189 items they settle (Fleiss’ $`\kappa`$
= 0.53 among annotators). Of the answers a trained model refused that
the audit calls right, they confirm 85.3% \[75.6, 91.6\]. Of 52 answers
the key rejects and the audit accepts, they call 44 right.

### Coverage of the audit.

Every answer of a trained model to a held-out question that the key
rejects has an audit verdict; the last 3,859 verdicts were added after
the final runs. Every number in this paper that uses the audited truth
of trained answers is computed with all of them.

### Data.

From each of TriviaQA, PopQA and NQ-open we draw 1,500 questions and
train on 750. The PopQA questions are the ones with the most popular
subject entities in the dataset (PopQA popularity of at least 24,568,
median 63,633; 20% ask for a capital), so the PopQA results concern
well-known entities. Held-out questions exclude any question whose text
or identifier matches a training question, which leaves 718 on TriviaQA
and 750 on the other two. On PopQA, 324 of the 750 held-out questions
are near-duplicates of a training question (same gold answer, or TF-IDF
similarity over word unigrams and bigrams of at least 0.5); results that
could depend on this are reported with and without them. Of the 750
PopQA training questions, 142 use the probe’s template (“What is the
capital of X?”), 17 of them about a former state.

### Strictness comes from the threshold.

Table 6 shows each judge at each cut. At the default threshold the
judges accept 62–87% of answers and pay 18–55% of the wrong ones.
Reaching 35% acceptance takes thresholds of 0.97 to 0.998 for the Qwen,
Mistral and Gemma judges: at that cut a rejection is usually an answer
the judge itself rates as probably correct. The prompt does not offer
the same control. Told to answer Yes only if the answer “is fully
correct and you are certain of it”, the Qwen judge at its default
threshold rejects 10.4% and 12.6% of key-right PopQA answers (Qwen and
Llama students), against 8.9% and 11.2% with the standard prompt, and
still accepts 68% and 76% of all answers.

### The judge knows most of what it rejects.

We asked the Qwen judge the 1,500 TriviaQA questions closed-book. Of the
right answers it rejects at the 35% cut, it answers the question
correctly itself in 81% of cases for the Qwen student’s answers and 67%
for the Llama student’s. Of the right answers it accepts, it answers 96%
and 93%. Rejections are more frequent where the judge does not know the
answer, and still two thirds to four fifths of them fall on questions it
answers correctly.

### Forced share.

If a grader accepts a share $`A`$ of the answers and a share $`\rho`$ of
the answers is right, it must reject at least $`\max(0,\,1-A/\rho)`$ of
the right answers, whatever it knows. This is the cost of strictness
itself. A judge’s _blind spot_ is what it does beyond this: which right
answers it chooses to reject.

### Graders with placed errors.

_Random errors_: the answer key, with each right answer rejected and
each wrong answer paid independently at fixed rates. _Hash-placed paid
strings_: a hash of the question and the normalised answer pays a fixed
35% of wrong question–answer pairs, the same pairs at every visit.
_Relation types_: on PopQA, right answers are rejected with probability
0.8 on eight relation types and 0.2 elsewhere (visible), or on a random
half of the questions of every type (hidden). _Long answers_: the key
pays right answers, and a wrong answer of at least five words is paid
with probability 0.8. _Copy of a judge_: right answers are rejected on
exactly the training questions where a judge at its 35% cut rejects the
untrained answer; wrong answers are paid at random at that judge’s rate.
The _scrambled_ copy rejects the same number of questions chosen by a
hash. The _type-wide_ copy rejects whole relation types, starting from
those the judge rejects most, until the set is as large.

### Strict graders with no judge.

_Random rejections_: the answer key with right answers rejected at
random at rates from 0.2 to 0.7; for each judge we compare with the
three such runs nearest in held-out abstention. The rejections are drawn
anew for every sampled answer, so no question carries a fixed verdict,
and 17 of these 91 runs end in refusing nearly everything. The
judge-free grader with fixed verdicts is the scrambled copy of a judge’s
rejections (Appendix E). _Own vote_: the key for a question is the
student’s most frequent untrained answer if at least $`k`$ of its 32
samples give it, and “I don’t know” is the only paid reply otherwise;
$`k=32`$ is the unanimous vote.

### Jury, Sonnet, reference.

The jury accepts an answer if at least three of the five judges accept
it, with the judges’ cuts set so that the jury accepts 35% of the
untrained answers. Its verdicts are computed once on the untrained
answers and used as a fixed key. As a classifier of right and wrong
answers the jury is better than its members (AUROC 0.96 and 0.90 for the
two students, against 0.90–0.94 and 0.82–0.87). Cut to accept 35% it
rejects eleven and ten of the twelve former-state training answers, like
the single judges (7–12). Cut only until it pays as many wrong answers
as single judges do at their 50% cut, it rejects 16% and 30% of right
answers where they reject 26–29% and 41–43%, and two and four of the
twelve where they reject four to eight; we did not train with that
looser jury. A single judge frozen in the same way reproduces live
training with that judge (82% of known former-state capitals refused in
both cases, Qwen student). Sonnet (claude-sonnet-5-5) grades without a
reference on a five-level scale and pays levels 4 and 5. The
reference-guided judge is the Qwen judge with the reference answer in
its prompt.

## Appendix C Paid wrong answers: supporting results

### Paid answers come from the model’s own samples.

With the hash grader we know exactly which wrong strings are paid. On
training questions, a wrong string that the untrained model gave in 2 to
7 of its 32 samples becomes the final answer in 26.0% (Qwen) and 20.7%
(Llama) of cases when the run’s grader pays it, and in 0.5% when it does
not. Scored against another run’s table, the same strings show no such
gap (10.0% against 9.6%, and 8.3% against 7.1%). On held-out questions
paid and unpaid strings are equally likely (9.8% against 9.9%, and 9.4%
against 8.9%).

### Visits, not steps.

We vary the number of training questions so that each is seen 1, 2, 5 or
10 times in the same 120 steps (TriviaQA, lenient Qwen judge, 15 runs
per student). The first revisit raises the judge-paid share of wrong
training samples by 0.12 in both students (\[0.11, 0.13\]); by the fifth
visit the rise is 0.24–0.28. At a fixed number of visits, 100 more steps
add 0.03 (Qwen) and 0.00 (Llama). Visits explain 15–18% of the variance
left by step position; steps explain 0.5% of the variance left by
visits.

Table 7 splits the 173 judge-trained runs by cut. The effect on training

### What does carry over.

No run gives the same string on more than 2.1% of held-out questions.
Table 8 separates what one judge pays from what all judges pay: measured
against the other judges, a judge’s paid answers transfer at 0.09;
measured against graders with random errors, which removes nothing the
judges share, the figure is 0.26 \[0.19, 0.36\]. Most of that shared
part is explained by the form of the answer and the kind of question
(half to two thirds). The same holds for where a model answers: on
questions where only its own judge pays the wrong answer, a model
answers more, at 0.23 \[0.09, 0.40\] of the training effect at the 35%
cut and 0.35 \[0.17, 0.55\] at the default threshold. The extreme case
is a form: in one of three runs, a Granite student trained without the
KL penalty answers 590 of 718 held-out questions with one invented
sentence that its judge pays.

### The model’s knowledge is untouched.

Under the KL penalty, 94.5% of a trained model’s right answers on
held-out questions are the answers it gave before training. Of the
accuracy gained among answered questions, 95% comes from choosing which
questions to answer and 5% from changed answers.

On held-out questions that the Mistral judge would reject, models

### One pass is enough.

On TriviaQA we train the strict Qwen judge’s models on 3,840 questions
seen once (three runs per student) and on 750 questions seen about five
times (six runs). The extra refusal of known answers that the judge
rejects, beyond key-trained models, is 0.21 against 0.19 for Qwen and
0.25 against 0.29 for Llama.

### The mirror image for wrong answers.

On NQ-open, where judges at the 35% cut still pay enough wrong answers
to test it, a model answers more on held-out questions where only its
own judge would pay the wrong answer: the contrast is $`-`$0.15
\[$`-`$0.24, $`-`$0.06\], negative in 18 of 20 judge pairs. What travels
is again a pattern over questions, here which questions are worth
answering, and not the paid strings.

### The loss can be forecast before training.

A linear probe on the untrained student’s hidden states, fitted to a
judge’s verdicts on the training questions, predicts which held-out
questions the trained model will refuse with AUROC 0.93 over 122
judge-trained runs. A probe that never sees the judge reaches 0.90, and
the judge’s own probe adds 0.02–0.04 in all 35 judge–student–dataset
cells.

### The test.

The fresh questions are the 2,569 questions of a second PopQA sample
that are not among our 1,500. They are spread over all six popularity
bins of PopQA (median popularity 683, against 63,633 for our 1,500), so
the test changes the popularity of the entities as well as the
questions. No model was trained for this test: the kept adapters of 167
runs answered the questions, and the untrained students answered them
with 32 samples. Right here means right by the answer key, since these
answers were not audited. The count by kind was taken on the training
questions before any output on the fresh questions existed. By the count
alone the country kind is third of ten for Llama (62% of sure, right
training answers rejected; 54% for Qwen). We singled it out because on
its five held-out examples the judges’ Llama models refused 53% and
judge-free graders none; the fresh questions are the test of that
choice. The count itself needs no labels. Taken over the answers the
student gives in at least 31 of its 32 samples, right or not (92% of
them are right for Llama, 89% for Qwen), it ranks the relation types as
the labelled count does (rank correlation 0.92 and 0.98), and the judges
reject 75% of such answers about former states, against 46% and 39% of
all such answers.

Table 15 gives every kind. On the country questions the judges’ models
refuse 83% (Llama) and 52% (Qwen) of the answers the student was sure
of, 0.41 \[0.27, 0.57\] and 0.26 \[0.16, 0.42\] beyond random rejections
(intervals over questions and runs; over questions alone \[0.34, 0.49\]
and \[0.19, 0.33\]), for each judge separately ($`+`$0.31 to $`+`$0.58
with Llama, $`+`$0.15 to $`+`$0.40 with Qwen). Of the 84 country
questions Llama answers correctly in all 32 samples, 56 are refused by
all six models of the strict Qwen judge and by none of the five
key-trained models. At the 50% cut the excess is 0.13 \[0.05, 0.20\] and
0.12 \[$`-`$0.05, 0.30\], and at Llama’s key-matched cut 0.01
\[$`-`$0.07, 0.06\]. The fresh capital questions, which are mostly about

Of the 173 combinations of grader and pair with at least two runs, 15

### A probe gives the same reward.

We fit a logistic regression to the untrained student’s hidden state at
the last prompt token (layers 16 and 24, 256 principal components each)
on the 750 training questions, with the audited correctness of the
student’s usual answer as the label, and use it as a rule: answer where
the predicted probability of being right is at least one half. On new
questions this rule earns 71.6 and 69.6 points on PopQA and TriviaQA for
Qwen and 83.4 and 81.3 for Llama. RL with the answer key earns 71.4,
69.3, 83.7 and 81.0.

### How much room is left.

A grader that paid exactly the right answers, followed perfectly, would
earn 79.2, 80.6 and 69.8 points for Qwen on PopQA, TriviaQA and NQ-open,
and 89.9, 89.1 and 79.1 for Llama. The best cut of the student’s own
vote, applied as a rule, earns 73.5, 71.3 and 59.5, and 84.9, 83.4 and
65.5. The rest would take knowledge the student does not have.

the other as whole words) and take the largest group. If it has at least

is empty and only “I don’t know” is paid. Training then follows
Appendix A. At $`k=20`$ the share of training questions with an empty
key is 40%, 27% and 56% for Qwen on PopQA, TriviaQA and NQ-open, and
23%, 21% and 52% for Llama.

### Where the cut sits.

With a reward of 1, 0 and 0.5, an answer is worth giving when it is
right more than half the time. We call a student’s _calibration point_
the smallest number of agreeing samples at which its usual answer is
right at least half the time, read from an isotonic fit on the training
questions with audited labels (last column of Table 23). A cut below
this point pays answers that are wrong more often than right. For Qwen
on TriviaQA the answers just above the cut of 20, those with 20 to 25
agreeing samples, are right 47% of the time; in the four pairs where the
cut is above the calibration point they are right 65–83% of the time.
The student trained on this vote learns to answer nearly everything: it
abstains on 12% of new questions, where the rule at the same cut
abstains on 29% and earns 71.0 points, more than the key-trained
students’ 69.3.

Across all 20 settings of the cut that we trained (49 runs; Figure 5),
the 11 settings whose cut lies between two samples below the calibration
point and twelve above it are between 0.9 points below the answer key
and 3.0 above. The other nine are 3.0 points below on average, and the
loss grows by about 0.8 points for every further sample of strictness.
This range is read off the same runs and is a description of them. For
Qwen on TriviaQA the two earlier runs with a cut of 27 are 0.4 points
above the key.

### Finding the calibration point takes labels.

Neither the judges nor the other student single out the overconfident
pair: on the answers just above the cut, the Qwen judge accepts 77% for
Qwen on TriviaQA, where 47% are right, and 59% for Qwen on PopQA, where
65% are right. With 100 labelled training questions the calibration
point is estimated at 25 for Qwen on TriviaQA (5–95% range over random
draws 15–31) and with 5–95% ranges that are 9 to 18 samples wide in the
other pairs.

## Appendix J What the students answer when they cannot refuse

### Method.

Nothing is trained. A kept adapter answers each question greedily with
the usual prompt. If the answer is a refusal (“I don’t know” or a
variant such as “Unknown”), the question is decoded again with the first
token of that refusal blocked by a logit bias of $`-100`$, then with the
first tokens of every refusal so far blocked, for up to five rounds. The
first answer that is not a refusal is the _forced answer_. An output is
a refusal if it matches the pattern used throughout the paper or a wider
list of refusal phrases, unless the answer key accepts it. We decoded
152 PopQA adapters on the capitals probe, and on the 1,500 questions of
each dataset 118 adapters of five graders and the 8 kept adapters
trained without the KL penalty, together with the untrained students.
Outside collapsed runs, at most 0.1% of the forced questions end without
an answer.

### The capitals.

Pooled over the 15 runs of the four judges at the 35% cut, the forced
answer is right for 95.3% of Llama’s 2,638 refused former-state capitals
(91–99% across runs) and 90.0% of Qwen’s 1,502 (85–95%); Table 24 gives
every grader. Some forced answers that count as wrong are spellings
outside the answer list (Samarqand, Kaegyŏng). The share is as high
where few capitals are refused, at the key-matched cut and with the
student’s own vote, and with the hand-set graders.

### Held-out questions.

Table 25 scores all held-out questions. Students of the three graders
that know the answers are within half a point of the untrained student.
Students of the own vote are 0.9 points below it, and students of the
Qwen judge at the 35% cut 1.8 points below, in all six pairs. On the
questions a student refuses, its forced answer is right about as often
as the untrained student’s. With key-or-audit truth the three
differences are $`-`$1.8, $`+`$0.5 and $`+`$0.1 points; these understate
the trained students, because 10–14% of their final answers have no
audit verdict and count as wrong, against 0.4–12% for the untrained
students.

### Collapsed runs.

Five of the 118 runs answer “I don’t know” to at least 95% of held-out
questions: four answer-key runs and one run of the judge shown the
reference, all with Qwen on NQ-open. Forced to answer, the four
answer-key runs are right on 4.4% of questions, against 26.0% for the
untrained student, and 45% of their forced decodings end without an
answer; the reference-judge run is right on 8.7%. A collapsed student
has lost answers as well as the will to give them. With these five runs
counted, the answer key is 2.2 points below the untrained student and
the judge shown the reference 1.8 below.

### Without the KL penalty.

The eight kept adapters trained without the KL penalty (TriviaQA)
abstain on under 2% of questions. The two Llama runs with the lenient
Qwen judge answer 38.6% of held-out questions correctly, against 74.9%
for the untrained student; the two Qwen runs are 1.4 points above
theirs. With an answer key that also pays 35% of wrong answers, Llama is
2.8 points below and Qwen 2.0 above.

advantages the size of the credit cancels within a group, so these runs

0.85 on PopQA.

control that does it.

## Appendix L Smaller students

We repeat the main measurements with Phi-4-mini, SmolLM3 and a Granite
student (446 runs; the numbers below are for TriviaQA). Paid wrong
answers stay home: the extra pay a judge gives its own model’s wrong
answers is 0.25 on training questions and 0.00 on held-out questions for
Phi-4-mini, 0.25 and 0.03 for SmolLM3, and 0.54 and 0.02 for the hash
grader. Blind spots travel, more weakly than at 7–8B: the contrast
between the Qwen and Gemma judges is $`+`$0.06 and $`+`$0.05. Knowledge
is preserved less exactly than in the larger students: 86% and 82% of
right held-out answers are the untrained answers. The remedy carries
over: wrong strings installed per training question are 0.28 with
reference-free judges, 0.05 with a reference-guided judge, and 0.01 with
the answer key. The Granite student, trained with a judge that accepts
free-form replies, is the one case where paid content reaches held-out
questions (0.29 on training questions, 0.171 \[0.035, 0.399\] held-out),
and what travels there is the form of the reply.

## Appendix M Statistics

Intervals are 95% percentile bootstraps with 1,000 to 2,000 resamples.
Where runs and questions both vary we resample both: questions within
student and dataset, and runs within grader. Contrasts between judges
are also tested by permuting judge labels within cells, and comparisons
of a few runs with a few runs by exact permutation of run labels.
Analyses were developed on the Qwen student and repeated unchanged on
Llama.

We designated ten primary tests. The jury, the self-consistency
comparison, the fresh-question test and the graders with hand-set
verdicts are not among them and rest on their own intervals. The ten
are: paid wrong answers stay on training questions; they form on
revisits; a lenient judge matches random errors on held-out questions;
abstention follows the grader’s leniency across students; the judge
contrast; refusals follow what the student can predict of a judge; the
copy beats the scrambled copy; visible placed rejections beat hidden
ones; false rejections cluster by question more than false accepts; and
the capitals gap. Pooled over the two students, all ten pass Holm
correction (largest adjusted $`p`$ = 0.016). Within one student, two
fall short of significance for each: for Llama, the equivalence test for
paid wrong answers ($`p`$ = 0.13) and the copy against the scrambled
copy (0.057); for Qwen, the lenient judge against random errors (0.12)
and visible against hidden rejections (0.10).

### Claims that did not hold.

The release contains a ledger of the 95 claims we tested during
analysis: 43 hold in both students, 33 hold pooled, in one student, or
with a caveat that changes the wording, 10 fail, one is underpowered,
and eight are magnitudes without a test. Seven further expectations were
not met. The own vote at a single cut was to stay within 2.5 points of
the answer key in every pair, and for Qwen on TriviaQA it is 3.4 points
below (Appendix I). Judges on an open-ended refusal task were expected
to reject harmless answers by category, which they did not (Table 27).
With none of the twelve training answers rejected, Llama was to refuse
at most 25% of the former-state capitals and refuses 31%; the loss was
to rise at every step, and for Qwen it is 81% at eight rejected answers
and 76% at twelve (Appendix E). And the count taken in training was to
rank the kinds at the cut where a student earns most (rank correlation
of at least 0.5); at Llama’s key-matched cut it is 0.14 (Appendix F).
Forced-choice accuracy was to stay within two points of the untrained
student for each grader with every run counted; with the five collapsed
runs the answer key is 2.2 points below, and 0.15 below without them
(Appendix J). With the twelve verdicts set by hand at the key-matched
cut, the reward by the answer key was to differ by at most 1.5 points
between the two graders; it is 2.6 points lower with the twelve rejected
(71.4 against 74.1, three runs each; Appendix E). The ledger entries
that fail are these. A direct test that false rejections transfer more
than false accepts because they are more predictable fails (difference
in transfer 0.04 \[$`-`$0.19, 0.27\]); the data support the split
between one judge’s strings and what a question predicts, in both signs,
and not a split by sign. Judges do not share false rejections more than
false accepts once base rates are equalised. At the default threshold a
judge’s rejections leave no trace on held-out questions ($`-`$0.01
\[$`-`$0.04, 0.02\]; the estimate of Table 9 for the same cut is
$`+`$0.03 \[$`-`$0.00, 0.05\]). A hidden half of each relation type
carries little, but equivalence to zero fails (0.03 \[$`-`$0.03,
0.12\]). An expected-reward rule with no fitted constants does not
explain differences between judges ($`R^{2}`$ $`-`$0.68 at the 35% cut).
No grader beats 32-sample self-consistency in a way that replicates from
Qwen to Llama. Without the KL penalty the answers do not stay the
model’s own (Table 27). An earlier capitals probe had too few known
former-state answers and showed that capitals of little-known
present-day places are lost as well (Appendix E). The remaining failures
follow. A curve that predicts abstention from the grader’s leniency
holds across the two students and for two of the three smaller students,
and does not hold for runs that change the credit, the KL weight or the
advantage estimator ($`R^{2}`$ $`-`$0.22), nor for the Granite student.
A model of the abstention rate with no fitted constants predicted that
forcing one “I don’t know” per group would lower abstention slightly; it
rose by 7–9 points with Qwen. How predictable a judge’s rejections are
from the question did not rank how far they transfer within TriviaQA
(rank correlation 0.31 \[$`-`$0.18, 0.72\]), and the Granite judge, the
least predictable, shows the largest effect. On TriviaQA and PopQA
strict judges pay too few wrong answers (1–6%) to test whether one
judge’s false accepts leave a trace; NQ-open is where that test was
possible (Appendix D).
