# Swapping the Judge Changes the Ranking: A Preregistered Study of Cross-Provider Replication in LLM-Judged System Comparisons

Sangdo Han Affiliation: CEO Business School
Email: [sangdohan@ceobizschool.kr](mailto:)    Moonsoo Kim
Affiliation: CEO Business School Email: [mskim@ceobizschool.kr](mailto:)

###### Abstract

Practitioners who compare systems with an LLM judge and have no human
labels often check a conclusion by repeating it with a judge from
another provider. We test how far that check can be trusted. In a
preregistered study (with a disclosed post-registration reduction of the
judge set), five judge models from three providers re-judged 4,000
Chatbot Arena battles (nine systems, 33 system pairs) that carry human
votes, each in both response orders. Agreement with the human vote was
low at the verdict level (median Cohen’s $`\kappa=0.22`$), and system
rankings did not survive a change of judge: Kendall’s $`\tau`$ with the
human ranking ranged from 0.17 to 0.89 (median 0.67), so the
preregistered hypothesis that rankings remain stable was not supported.
Conclusions replicated by two low-cost judges from different providers
matched the human conclusion more often than single-judge conclusions
($`+0.10`$ precision), but the gain came from filtering out one poorly
aligned judge, and replication involving that judge left a conclusion
for only 35% of comparisons. Judges that agreed with humans reached a
stable conclusion with no more verdicts than human votes required; the
others failed to converge on 26–70% of system pairs at the sample sizes
tested (up to 100 verdicts per pair). Disagreements with the human vote
co-occurred across judges far more often than independence predicts,
although a single human vote per battle cannot separate shared judge
bias from voter noise.

## 1 Introduction

LLM judges are now a routine way to compare language systems (Zheng
et al., 2023). A typical use is a pairwise comparison: a judge model
reads one request and two responses and says which response is better,
and the verdicts are aggregated into a win rate or a ranking. When human
labels are unavailable, the only check on such a conclusion is another
judge. A common recommendation is to combine judges from different model
families, on the expectation that their individual biases differ (Verga
et al., 2024).

This paper asks two practical questions about that check. First, when a
system-level conclusion obtained with one judge is reproduced by a judge
from a different provider, is it more likely to match the conclusion
that human votes would give? Second, how many verdicts does a judge need
before its system-level conclusion is stable, compared with the number
of human votes needed for the same comparison?

A widely repeated observation motivates optimism: judges can disagree
with humans on many individual items while still ranking systems
similarly (Gera et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2025). We preregistered this
observation as a replication hypothesis (H1) and three further
hypotheses on replication (H2), sample requirements (H3), and
co-occurring disagreement (H4). The registered design had six judges;
one was dropped after registration, as described in Section 3.6. We then
re-judged 4,000 battles from a public Chatbot Arena release (Chiang
et al., 2024; LMArena, 2025) with five judge models from three
providers. Every battle has a human vote, so verdict-level and
system-level quantities are computed on the same items.

The results are mixed, and we report them against the preregistered
criteria.

- •
  H1 was not supported. Verdict-level agreement with humans was low for
  every judge, and rank correlation with the human ranking depended
  strongly on the judge ($`\tau`$ from 0.17 to 0.89; median 0.67 against
  a preregistered threshold of 0.8).
- •
  H2 was supported, with a narrow mechanism. Replicated conclusions were
  more precise than single-judge conclusions, but only because
  replication removed the wrong conclusions of one poorly aligned judge.
  Two well-aligned judges gained nothing from replicating each other,
  and replication with the poorly aligned judge reduced coverage from
  100% to 35% of comparisons.
- •
  H3 held for four of five judges. Where a judge converged, it needed no
  more verdicts than human votes. Three judges failed to converge on a
  quarter or more of the system pairs within the sizes tested.
- •
  H4 was supported statistically, but its interpretation is limited
  because the reference label is a single vote from one user.

The study is small (nine systems, 33 system pairs, three low-cost judges
in the cross-provider analyses) and its contribution is correspondingly
modest: a preregistered measurement, on human-voted battles, of what
cross-provider replication buys and what it costs at the level of
system-comparison conclusions. We release battle identifiers, all
verdicts, the preregistration and deviation records, and the analysis
code as ancillary files with this preprint.

## 2 Related Work

#### Verdict-level versus system-level judge quality.

Gera et al. (2025) evaluate 48 judge realizations by how well the system
ranking they induce matches a human-based leaderboard, and show that
verdict-level and system-level quality can diverge. Gao et al. (2025)
compare combinations of input sets, judges, comparison formats, and
aggregation methods and report a similar divergence. Brocai and Becker
(2026) show for text ranking that disagreement concentrates on pairs
with small quality gaps, which limits its effect on rankings. Our H1
restates the common reading of this line of work as a threshold test and
finds that it fails for the judges and data studied here; unlike the
leaderboard-based evaluations, our human reference consists of votes on
the same battles the judges see.

#### Judge bias and sample efficiency.

Dorner et al. (2025) prove that when a judge is no more accurate than
the systems it evaluates, bias correction cannot save more than half of
the ground-truth labels, and show that judge bias can perturb rankings
even when judge accuracy is high. Our H3 analysis is an empirical
counterpart on arena data: it compares, pair by pair, the number of
judge verdicts and human votes needed to reach the same directional
conclusion.

#### Panels and correlated errors.

Panels of diverse judges were proposed to reduce the bias of any single
judge (Verga et al., 2024). Kohli (2026) report that errors of panel
members are strongly correlated on NLI items and that the best single
judge matches or beats the panel. We observe the same two patterns for
pairwise system comparison: disagreements with the human vote co-occur
across judges, and a five-judge majority does not beat the best single
judge.

#### Self- and family-preference.

Judges can favor their own generations (Panickssery et al., 2024), and
recent work measures preference at the level of model families (Awuni
et al., 2026). A recent analysis by the Arena team had twelve models
judge 1,460 English Text Arena battles from 2026 and compared the
verdicts with the human votes (Arena Team, 2026). It reports that most
judges favor their own responses and those of sibling models, that
judges agree with each other far more often than with the human voter,
and that even on the battles where all twelve judges chose the same
response the voter agreed only 60.6% of the time. That analysis is at
the verdict level and includes battles in which the judge wrote one of
the responses; it does not examine system rankings, replicated
system-level conclusions, or sample requirements. Our
provider-preference analysis is a prespecified secondary analysis that
is consistent with these reports, and we do not present it as a new
finding.

## 3 Study Design

### 3.1 Data and sample

We use a public Chatbot Arena release, arena-human-preference-140k
(LMArena, 2025), pinned to a fixed revision, which contains 135,634
battles. A battle consists of one user request, the responses of two
anonymous systems, and the user’s vote. We keep single-turn battles, the
first vote of each conversation, votes that choose one of the two
responses (ties and “both bad” are excluded), and battles whose request
and two responses total at most 6,000 tokens; 65,636 battles remain
(Appendix A). We do not filter by language. Restricting to English left
only three system pairs with at least 100 battles, so the language
restriction was dropped before any judge output was produced, and
results are reported separately for English and non-English battles.

Systems were added in decreasing order of battle count until system
pairs with at least 100 battles could supply 5,000 battles under a
common per-pair cap. This rule selected nine systems and 33 system pairs
with 111–183 battles each (Appendix B); the comparison graph is
connected. The sample was split by pair into a development set (1,000
battles) used for pipeline checks and a confirmation set (4,000 battles)
used for all results in this paper. Of the confirmation battles, 2,156
are English, 1,471 are in another identified language, and 373 have no
identified language.

### 3.2 Judges

Five judge models from three providers were used: Claude Opus 5.5,
Claude Sonnet 5.5, and Claude Haiku 4.5 (Anthropic); GPT-6 Luna
(OpenAI); and Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite (Google). The three low-cost models
from different providers (Haiku, Luna, Flash-Lite) form the _economy
set_ used for the cross-provider analyses, so that provider differences
are not confounded with tier differences. The two larger Anthropic
models show the effect of tier within one provider. Exact model
identifiers and request settings are given in Appendix C.

### 3.3 Judging protocol

All judges received the same prompt (Appendix C), which shows the
request and the two responses without system names and asks for a brief
justification followed by a final line with one of three verdicts: A, B,
or tie. Each battle was judged twice with the response order swapped. If
the two verdicts selected the same system, that system wins; if they
selected different systems or either was a tie, the resolved verdict is
a tie. We call a battle order-consistent when the two orders selected
the same system or were both ties. A battle with an unparseable or
refused response in either order is missing for that judge. For 500
confirmation battles, both orders were judged two additional times to
measure run-to-run consistency. Each judge therefore produced 10,000
confirmation responses.

### 3.4 Measures

For pair-level quantities, scores are oriented to the alphabetically
first system $`X`$ of each pair: 1 if $`X`$ wins, 0 if it loses, and 0.5
for a tie. For a judge or for the human votes, the win rate $`w`$ of a
pair is the mean score, and its _direction_ is the sign of $`w-0.5`$. A
direction is _significant_ if $`|w-0.5|>1.96\,\mathrm{se}`$, where
$`\mathrm{se}`$ is the standard error of the mean. We write
$`\mathrm{dir}_{H}`$ and $`g_{H}=|w_{H}-0.5|`$ for the human direction
and gap.

#### Verdict level.

Cohen’s $`\kappa`$ (Cohen, 1960) between a judge’s resolved verdicts and
the human votes over the categories {first system, second system, tie},
in the original left/right orientation of each battle.

#### System level.

Bradley–Terry strengths (Bradley and Terry, 1952) fitted with an MM
algorithm (Hunter, 2004), with ties counted as half a win for each side
and 0.5 pseudo-wins added in each direction for every observed pair, and
Kendall’s $`\tau_{b}`$ (Kendall, 1938) and Spearman’s $`\rho`$ between
the judge-based and human-based rankings.

#### Conclusion level.

Let $`E`$ be the set of pairs whose human direction is significant (23
of 33 pairs in the confirmation set). The single-judge precision
$`P_{1}(j)`$ is the fraction of pairs in $`E`$ with a significant
direction for judge $`j`$ whose direction equals $`\mathrm{dir}_{H}`$.
The replicated precision $`P_{2}(j,k)`$ is the same fraction over pairs
for which judges $`j`$ and $`k`$ are both significant in the same
direction. Coverage is the fraction of pairs in $`E`$ that yield a
conclusion.

#### Sample requirement.

For a source (human votes or a judge), a pair, and
$`n\in\{25,50,100\}`$, we draw 1,000 samples of $`n`$ battles with
replacement and record the fraction whose direction equals the full-data
$`\mathrm{dir}_{H}`$. The requirement $`n^{*}`$ is the smallest $`n`$
for which this fraction is at least 0.95; if no $`n`$ reaches it,
$`n^{*}`$ is censored. A size $`n`$ is skipped for a source when it
exceeds that source’s number of valid battles for the pair. The
preregistered grid also contained $`n=200`$, which exceeds every pair’s
confirmation size, and $`n=100`$ was skipped for the five pairs with
fewer than 100 confirmation battles.

#### Co-occurring disagreement.

Over battles on which all three economy judges give a non-tie verdict,
the observed rate $`O`$ at which all three disagree with the human vote
is compared with the product $`E_{\perp}`$ of their individual
disagreement rates.

### 3.5 Hypotheses

H1  
The median $`\kappa`$ over judges is below 0.6 and the median $`\tau`$
is above 0.8.

H2  
$`\Delta=\overline{P_{2}}-\overline{P_{1}}>0`$, where the means are over
the cross-provider pairs and the members of the economy set.

H3  
For pairs with $`g_{H}\geq 0.1`$ and a finite human $`n^{*}`$, a judge’s
median ratio $`n^{*}_{\text{judge}}/n^{*}_{\text{human}}`$ is at most 2,
with censored values treated as infinite.

H4  
$`D=O-E_{\perp}>0`$.

H2 and H4 are tested with one-sided bootstrap tests and Holm correction
(Holm, 1979); H1 and H3 are decided by the stated thresholds. Each of
the 1,000 bootstrap replicates resamples battles with replacement within
pairs, jointly for all judges and the human votes; the set $`E`$ is held
fixed; $`p`$-values use the plus-one estimator; and precision terms with
a zero denominator are excluded from the means and counted.

### 3.6 Preregistration and deviations

The prompt, parser, verdict-resolution rule, sample, hypotheses, and
analysis code were hashed and frozen before any confirmation response
was requested. The set $`E`$ was fixed from human votes alone. Two
changes were made after the freeze. First, the registered design had six
judges, including an open-weight model served by a fourth organization
as a fourth economy judge. That judge was dropped for a logistical
reason (no additional hosting account was opened), and the decision was
taken after the confirmation results of the three Anthropic judges had
been seen. H1 is therefore evaluated over five judges and H2 and H4 over
three economy judges. Second, after the freeze, the majority-vote rule
was changed from the original requirement that all judges have valid
verdicts to an abstention rule: ties and missing verdicts abstain, A and
B votes are counted, and equal counts (including no votes) give a tie.
Both changes were recorded on October 6, 2026, after the Anthropic
confirmation results had been seen and before the full five-judge
analysis. The majority vote does not enter any hypothesis criterion. No
other definition changed. Analyses marked _post hoc_ below were not
preregistered.

## 4 Results

### 4.1 Response quality

All 50,000 confirmation responses were retrieved. Parse failures were at
most four per judge. Opus and Sonnet refused 76 and 39 requests (0.76%
and 0.39%); the other judges refused none. Resolved verdicts were
available for 3,971–4,000 of the 4,000 battles per judge.

Three further observations qualify these numbers. First, the two
low-cost judges Haiku and Flash-Lite agree with human votes more closely
than the two larger Anthropic judges in $`\kappa`$ and in both rank
correlations. This says that their preferences are closer to those of
Arena voters; it does not show that their verdicts are more correct.
Second, the majority vote of the five judges ($`\tau=0.778`$) does not
beat the best single judges. Third, for every judge the observed
$`\tau`$ lies inside the 95% range of a random-error model that keeps
the judge’s tie rate and its agreement rate on non-tie verdicts but
places disagreements at random. This does not show that the
disagreements are random; it shows that with nine systems the ranking
statistic alone cannot tell systematic disagreement from random
disagreement. The provider-level analysis in Section 4.6 can.

### 4.3 Replication across providers (H2)

Table 2 reports precision and coverage on the 23 pairs in $`E`$. The
mean replicated precision is 0.902 and the mean single-judge precision
of the economy set is 0.804, giving $`\Delta=0.098`$ (95% interval
0.052–0.194; Holm-adjusted $`p=0.002`$). H2 is supported. On all 33

$`\Delta=0.125`$ (0.028–0.172).

The table also shows where the gain comes from. Haiku and Flash-Lite
each reach the human conclusion on 22 of 23 pairs, and the conclusions
they share are right on the same 22 of 23: replication between these two
judges adds nothing, and the one wrong conclusion is common to both.
Luna reaches a significant conclusion on 14 pairs and is right on seven.
Requiring agreement with a second judge removes most of Luna’s wrong
conclusions (seven of eight replicated conclusions are right), but only
eight of 23 comparisons then have a conclusion at all. Replication thus
acted as a filter against a poorly aligned judge, at a large cost in
coverage, and not as a general improvement in validity. A user cannot
tell from replication alone which of the two situations applies:
agreement between Haiku and Flash-Lite and agreement between Haiku and
Luna look the same from the inside. The verdict-level analogue has been
reported by Arena Team (2026), where unanimous agreement among twelve
judges still matched the human vote on only 60.6% of battles.

### 4.5 Co-occurring disagreement (H4)

On the 2,351 confirmation battles where all three economy judges give a
non-tie verdict, all three disagree with the human vote on 15.8% of
battles, against 3.5% expected if their disagreements were independent
($`D=0.122`$, 95% interval 0.111–0.133; Holm-adjusted $`p=0.002`$). H4
is supported.

This result needs a careful reading. The reference is one vote from one
user. If that user preferred the response that most readers would
consider worse, every competent judge “disagrees” at once, and the
disagreements co-occur without any shared judge bias. Our data cannot
separate this from shared bias. Two observations are consistent with a
substantial contribution of voter noise: at the level of system pairs,
where single-vote noise averages out, the economy judges share only one
wrong conclusion among the 23 pairs in $`E`$ (post hoc), and the
co-occurrence rate is lower in pairs with larger human gaps (post hoc).
We therefore describe H4 as co-occurring disagreement with the human
vote and not as evidence of common judge error.

Because the nine selected systems are dominated by three providers,

and Sonnet judges rank it fourth. These measurements compare judges with
Arena voters and say nothing about which side is right; they are
consistent with recent reports of self- and family-preference
(Panickssery et al., 2024; Awuni et al., 2026; Arena Team, 2026).

## 5 Discussion

#### What replication with a second provider can and cannot show.

In this study, agreement between two judges from different providers was
informative only in one direction. When a poorly aligned judge was
involved, disagreement with a second judge flagged most of its wrong
conclusions. But agreement between two judges did not certify a
conclusion: the two best-aligned judges shared their one wrong
conclusion, and the pair-level precision of replicated conclusions was
no higher than that of the better judge alone. Replication is therefore
better understood as a screen for an unreliable judge than as a
substitute for human validation, and its cost in lost conclusions can be
large.

#### Judge choice matters more than sample size.

For well-aligned judges, 25 verdicts per system pair were enough on
almost all pairs with a human gap of at least 0.1. For the other judges,
a quarter or more of the pairs never converged. Within the sizes we
tested, more verdicts from the same judge did not resolve these pairs; a
small human-labeled set that reveals which judge is aligned for the task
at hand would address the cause directly.

#### Stronger models were not better aligned with voters.

The two larger Anthropic judges agreed less with Arena votes than the
low-cost judges did. One reading is that larger models apply criteria
that differ from those of casual voters, for example by weighting
correctness over presentation. Our data do not test this reading.

## 6 Limitations

The study has nine systems and 33 system pairs, and rank correlations
over nine systems are coarse. Conclusion-level results rest on 23 pairs,
and the H2 effect depends on a single poorly aligned judge. The
cross-provider analyses use one low-cost judge per provider; we did not
test replication between larger models from different providers. The
registered design included a sixth judge, an open-weight economy model,
that was dropped after partial results had been seen; although the
reason was logistical, this is a deviation from the preregistration. All
five judges are proprietary models that may be retired, which limits
exact reproduction of the verdicts; we release the verdicts themselves.

The human reference is a single crowd vote per battle from 2025. Such
votes are noisy and reflect voter preference, not correctness, and the
judged systems are older than most of the judge models. Some battle
texts may have been seen by the judges during training. The judging
prompt is in English while 37% of confirmation battles are in another
identified language. Ties and “both bad” votes were excluded, so the
results apply to battles where the voter expressed a preference. We
tested one prompt and one verdict-resolution rule; treating
order-inconsistent verdicts as ties is one of several reasonable
choices. Reasoning settings were not uniform across judges, because
providers expose different controls (Table 6): GPT-6 Luna ran with
reasoning turned off, while Opus and Sonnet ran with low-effort
reasoning. We cannot tell whether Luna’s low agreement with human votes
reflects the model or this setting, and the H2 effect depends on that
judge. The prediction-powered baseline listed in the preregistered plan
was not implemented.

## Ethics and Reproducibility

The data are a public release of user conversations collected between
April and July 2025 with pre-existing votes; no new human annotation was
collected. The dataset documentation does not describe removal of
personal information. We did not apply further filtering; the texts were
processed only through the providers’ batch interfaces, and we do not
redistribute request or response text. The release contains battle
identifiers, all judge verdicts with token usage, the frozen analysis
code, the preregistration, and the deviation record, from which every
number in this paper can be regenerated. The code is released under the
MIT License and the verdicts and derived tables under CC BY 4.0; the
underlying requests and responses remain under the terms of the original
release. Judging cost about USD 157 at the fixed exchange rate used in
the cost ledger.

#### Use of AI assistants.

The judging and analysis pipeline was implemented with an LLM-based
coding agent, and LLM assistants were used in planning the analysis and
in drafting this manuscript. The authors reviewed the code through its
test suite and an independent reproduction run, checked the reported
numbers against the released tables, and take responsibility for the
content.

## Appendix C Judging protocol

All requests were sent through the providers’ batch interfaces. The
target for visible output was 1,024 tokens; for Opus and Flash-Lite the
total output limit was set to 5,120 tokens to leave room for reasoning
tokens, which does not guarantee a fixed amount of visible output. An
initial limit of 300 tokens truncated some responses in a partial
development run; the limit was raised and the development run repeated
before the freeze. Table 6 reports the settings used. Analyses used
Python 3.11 with NumPy 2.4.6, SciPy 1.17.1, and pandas 3.0.6; the
Bradley–Terry fit, bootstrap, and resampling procedures were implemented
directly, and all package versions are pinned in the released lock file.

#### System prompt.

_You are an impartial evaluator comparing two AI assistant responses to
the same user request._

#### User prompt.

The request and the two responses are inserted under the headings
_\[User request\]_, _\[Response A\]_, and _\[Response B\]_, followed by:
_Which response better serves the user’s request? Give a brief
justification in at most three sentences. Then end with exactly one
final line in one of these forms: VERDICT: A / VERDICT: B / VERDICT:
TIE_.

After the analysis, all 60,000 development and confirmation responses
were downloaded again from the providers and matched to their requests
by the server-side request identifier; no mismatch was found (post hoc
integrity check).
