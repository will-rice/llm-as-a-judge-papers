# What Moves a LoCoMo Score? Memory Context, Answer Prompts and Judges

Nikhil Kumar Jha Affiliation: becosAI    Amitoj Cheema
Affiliation: nikhil@becos.ai, amitoj@becos.ai

November 2026

###### Abstract

LoCoMo is a widely reported benchmark for long-term conversational
memory, but published scores mix memory quality with choices of answer
prompt, judge and baseline layout that are rarely reported together. We
measure how much these choices move a score, and evaluate Becos, a team
memory system for coding agents, as a black box under all of them: ten
conversations (1,540 questions), two answer models (gpt-4o-mini,
gpt-4.1-mini), two answer prompts and three LLM judges, against a
whole-conversation baseline rerun under identical conditions. Changing
only the judge moves a score by up to 7.2 points and changing only the
answer prompt by up to 9.3. A blind human check of 150 answers finds the
judges reliable when they agree, and the primary judge’s errors on
disputed answers working against Becos rather than for it. Becos answers
from a context with 90% fewer characters than the whole conversation.
Its configuration was developed on LoCoMo itself, so there is no
held-out set. With gpt-4o-mini answers it scores clearly higher than the
whole conversation (79.7 vs 73.0 with the short prompt, paired 95%
interval for the difference +3.8 to +9.5; 88.8 vs 77.5 with a
step-by-step prompt). With gpt-4.1-mini answers the two score about the
same (83.2 vs 83.1; 92.5 vs 92.1). We release every answer and verdict
and the scoring script.

## 1 Introduction

Memory systems for LLM agents are compared on a small number of public
benchmarks, LoCoMo \[1\] chief among them. Reported scores have climbed
from the low 60s \[2\] to above 90 \[5\], but papers differ in answer
models, answer prompts, judges and even in how they lay out the
whole-conversation baseline, and rarely report all of these. That makes
it hard to tell a better memory from a different setup.

This paper is primarily a controlled study of that sensitivity, with a
black-box evaluation of one memory system alongside it. We rerun the
whole-conversation baseline in-house, vary one setting at a time, and
report every combination.

Our contributions:

- •
  A sensitivity analysis on LoCoMo: answer model, answer prompt, judge,
  a date-generous judge, and the layout of the whole-conversation
  baseline (Section 6).
- •
  A black-box evaluation of Becos on all ten conversations under 2
  answer models $`\times`$ 2 answer prompts $`\times`$ 3 judges, with
  paired uncertainty estimates (Figures 1 and 2; Tables 1–2).
- •
  A consistency check of published LoCoMo tables (Section 7).
- •
  The prompts, every answer and verdict, and the scoring script.

## 2 Related work

#### Memory for LLM agents.

MemGPT \[8\] manages a limited context window by moving information
between memory tiers, in the manner of an operating system. Later
systems store information extracted from conversations and retrieve it
for each query: Mem0 \[2\], Zep’s time-aware knowledge graph \[9\],
A-Mem’s linked, self-updating notes \[10\], Nemori’s memory distillation
\[3\], EverMemOS \[7\] and the curation and consolidation method of
\[5\]. Most of them report LoCoMo. We evaluate one such system as a
black box and focus on the evaluation choices around it.

#### Benchmarks.

Multi-Session Chat \[11\] introduced open-domain conversations that span
several sessions. LoCoMo \[1\] has far longer conversations and
evaluates question answering, event summarization and multimodal
dialogue; we use its question answering. LongMemEval \[12\] tests five
long-term memory abilities of chat assistants over long interaction
histories. An independent audit of LoCoMo \[6\] documents errors in its
answer key and the leniency of its standard judge.

#### Long contexts.

The whole-conversation baseline depends on how well the answer model
uses a long input. Models use information in the middle of a long input
less reliably than at its start or end \[13\], and many perform worse
well before their stated context length \[14\]. Either effect could
explain why gpt-4o-mini does better from a short context in our results;
we do not test which.

#### LLM judges.

LoCoMo scores are usually assigned by an LLM judge. Strong LLM judges
agree with human preferences about as well as humans agree with each
other, but show position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases \[15\].
Verbosity bias matters for our 7-step prompt, which produces longer
final answers (Section 6).

## 3 Setup

Figure 1: Experiment overview. Each question is answered from one of
three contexts, by one of two answer models with one of two answer
prompts, and every answer is graded by three judges.

### 3.1 Benchmark

LoCoMo \[1\] has ten long two-person conversations (369 to 689 messages
over 19 to 32 sessions) with question–answer pairs in five categories.
As in prior work \[2, 3, 5\] we drop the adversarial category (446
questions about events the conversation attributes to someone else, for
which the data gives only the trap answer and no gold answer, so the
standard judge cannot score them), leaving 1,540 questions: 841
single-hop, 282 multi-hop, 321 temporal and 96 open-domain (category ids
4, 1, 2 and 3 in the released data).

### 3.2 Systems compared

#### Becos.

Becos is a commercial memory system for teams using AI coding agents,
developed by the authors’ company
([https://becos.ai](https://becos.ai)); we evaluate it as a black box.
Only conversation histories are ingested into Becos, one turn at a time,
oldest session first, in timestamp order, the way an agent session
reaches it. Evaluation questions are supplied at retrieval time; gold
answers are used only for judging and scoring. At every turn Becos
breaks the message down into attributes (who, what, when) and stores
them. To answer a question it searches that store and returns a context
of at most 8,950 characters, which can include short excerpts of the
original messages. The configuration evaluated here is a research
configuration for conversation projects, frozen on 9 November 2026, and
every Becos run reported here was made with it on that day (the
baselines were run from 7 November); it is not yet part of Becos for
coding agents.

#### Whole conversation.

The entire conversation the question belongs to, in the answer prompt,
one line per message, with each session’s date as a header line (the
layout of the released data). This is the standard baseline \[2, 3, 5\];
we rerun it under the same answer models, prompts and judges.

#### No memory.

The question alone, as a floor.

### 3.3 Development and evaluation

This work was a research effort, and Becos’s configuration for
conversation projects was developed on LoCoMo itself. Changes to how
Becos stores a conversation were made while examining errors on conv-26,
conv-30 and conv-41; retrieval settings were chosen on conv-42, conv-43
and conv-44. The configuration was then frozen and run unchanged on all
ten conversations. conv-47 to conv-50 (655 questions) were never
examined question by question, but their overall results under an
earlier configuration were seen before the last change was adopted, so
there is no fully held-out set. We report results on all ten and,
separately, on conv-47 to conv-50 as the least-used conversations
(Table 2); all results should be read as an evaluation on a benchmark
that was also used during development.

### 3.4 Answering and judging

#### Answer prompts.

_Short_: Nemori’s answer prompt \[3, 4\], which asks for an answer under
5–6 words. _7-step_: the same prompt with that instruction replaced by
seven written reasoning steps (list relevant memories with timestamps,
extract key details, connect facts, convert relative times, resolve
conflicts, check details, plan the answer) and a FINAL ANSWER line with
no length limit; the step list follows the LoCoMo audit’s description
\[6\] of EverMemOS’s chain-of-thought prompt \[7\], in our own words.
Only the text after FINAL ANSWER is passed to the judge. Both are in
Appendix A. Answer models are gpt-4o-mini and gpt-4.1-mini at
temperature 0, one pass per question; the API responses report the
snapshots gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18 and gpt-4.1-mini-2025-04-14 for every
call.

#### Judges.

Nemori’s judge prompt \[4\] with three judge models: gpt-4o-mini (the
judge Nemori states, and our primary column), gpt-4.1-mini, and
DeepSeek-v4-flash (which the DeepSeek API reports as deepseek-flash).
Each verdict is CORRECT or WRONG; a score is the share of questions
judged correct, so the overall score weights each category by its number
of questions. Judges are never averaged. All runs took place on 7–9
November 2026.

## 5 Context size

Every question gets the whole conversation it belongs to, so the
whole-conversation context per question averages 89,048 characters over
the 1,540 questions (51,927 to 105,427 per conversation). Becos’s
context averages 8,774 characters (6,226 to 8,950), 9.9% as much (90.1%
fewer characters), and does not grow with the length of the
conversation. In tokens, as counted by the API for the short prompt: the
context is 2,337 tokens with Becos against 20,525 with the whole
conversation (88.6% fewer), and the whole answer prompt, including 480
tokens of instructions and question, is 2,817 against 21,005 (86.6%
fewer). These counts cover answering only; Becos’s own cost of storing
and searching the conversation is not included.

## 6 What else moves the score

#### The judge.

On identical answers the three judges differ by up to 7.2 points: the
whole conversation with gpt-4o-mini answers scores 75.5 under the
gpt-4.1-mini judge and 68.2 under DeepSeek (73.0 under gpt-4o-mini;
Table 1). The judges agree on 90–93% of individual verdicts for short
answers and 95–97% for 7-step answers. DeepSeek is the strictest, and
stricter on the whole conversation than on Becos.

#### A human check of the judges.

One author graded 150 answers (gpt-4o-mini answers, short prompt) blind
to system and judge, as correct, wrong or cannot tell, against a written
rubric: 60 answers from each system on which the three judges do not all
agree (of 176 for Becos and 200 for the whole conversation) and 15 from
each on which they all agree. The 17 marked cannot tell (13 of them
temporal) are excluded. Where all three judges agree, each matches the
human verdict on 29 of 30 answers. Where they disagree, gpt-4.1-mini
matches it on 65 of 103, DeepSeek on 57 and gpt-4o-mini, our primary
judge, on 47. The primary judge’s errors do not favour Becos: on Becos’s
answers it was too strict 22 times and too lenient 7, on the whole
conversation’s too strict 12 times and too lenient 15. Keeping the
unanimous verdicts and applying the human rate of correct answers to
each system’s disputed questions gives about 82.2 for Becos and 71.4 for
the whole conversation, a difference of 10.7 points before rounding (8.4
to 13.0 when resampling only the graded answers), against the judge’s
6.8. With one grader, who is also an author, we read this as a check
that judge error does not explain Becos’s lead, not as a corrected
score.

#### The answer prompt.

Switching from the short prompt to the 7-step prompt, with nothing else
changed, raises scores by 4.6 to 9.3 points under the gpt-4o-mini judge
and by 0.1 to 8.6 under the other two (Table 1). The 7-step prompt
changes three things at once: it adds written reasoning steps, removes
the short-answer limit (median final answers grow from 3–5 words to
17–29), and has only the final answer judged. Our comparison does not
separate these. One hypothesis is that part of the gain is
genuine—longer answers keep the specific detail a question asks for—and
part is grading: the judge prompt asks the judge to be generous, and an
independent audit found that this judge accepts 63% of deliberately
vague but on-topic wrong answers \[6\]. A length-controlled ablation or
a human audit would be needed to settle it. We therefore report both
prompts side by side and do not compare a 7-step number with a
short-answer number.

#### Date leniency.

A diagnostic judge that also accepts dates within seven days of the gold
date raises the whole conversation’s temporal score from 50.5 to 58.3
and Becos’s from 79.8 to 81.9 (gpt-4o-mini answers). We do not report it
as a score.

#### Layout of the whole-conversation baseline.

Putting the session date on every message instead of once per session
raises the whole conversation on conv-47 from 67.3 to 73.3 (temporal
26.5 to 44.1). We ran this on one conversation only, so its effect
across the benchmark is unknown. Our comparisons are therefore against
the baseline as laid out in the released data, not against every
possible layout; baselines in different papers may differ in this way
without saying so.

#### A consistency check.

An overall score over the 1,540 questions equals the question-weighted
mean of the four category scores; our tables and Nemori’s satisfy this
exactly (73.0 = 73.0; 72.3 = 72.3). The per-category whole-conversation
figures printed in \[5\] give 76.2 and 89.5 rather than the reported
79.0 and 91.3. Of the 24 ways to assign the four printed columns to the
four categories, exactly one brings both rows within 0.15 points of the
reported overall (78.89 vs 79.0; 91.29 vs 91.3), substantially reducing
the discrepancy, though the first row still falls just outside what
rounding alone explains. Under that assignment the column printed as
temporal corresponds to single-hop, single-hop to multi-hop, and
multi-hop to temporal, and the baseline’s temporal score would be 53.2
and 87.8, in line with ours and Nemori’s, instead of 90.3 and 94.8. This
is the pattern produced by mapping LoCoMo’s numeric category ids in a
different order, but we have not confirmed the cause. We contacted the
authors on 9 November 2026 and had not received a reply at the time of
writing.

## 8 Limitations

- •
  The configuration was developed on LoCoMo (six of the ten
  conversations informed specific changes; Section 3.3), and there is no
  fully held-out set. The four least-used conversations show the same
  pattern, but LoCoMo is not an unseen benchmark for this configuration.
- •
  One answer pass per question, and ten conversations; the paired
  intervals are approximate and conditional on the saved answers and
  verdicts.
- •
  The whole-conversation baseline uses the released data’s layout; a
  date on every message helped it on the one conversation we tried
  (Section 6).
- •
  LoCoMo is personal chat, not the coding work Becos is built for. These
  results do not show how Becos does on coding work.
- •
  LoCoMo’s answer key has errors \[6\], and its judge is lenient; both
  cap what a score can mean.
- •
  The 7-step prompt changes several things at once; its gains are not
  attributed to reasoning alone.
- •
  Each conversation was loaded into Becos once; loading again may store
  slightly different attributes. Retrieval was checked to return
  identical text across two runs.
- •
  Becos uses an LLM when storing each turn, and that model is not one of
  the answer models. Part of Becos’s gain with gpt-4o-mini answers may
  come from a different model having processed the conversation at
  storage time.
- •
  The human check has one grader, an author, on 150 answers from one
  setting; a second grader and a larger sample would be needed to report
  human-judged scores.
- •
  Context sizes and token counts cover answering only; Becos’s own cost
  and latency of storing and searching are not measured.

## Competing interests

Both authors work at becosAI, which develops Becos. All answers and
verdicts, and the scripts to rerun the baselines, are released so the

## 9 Reproducibility

We release at
[https://github.com/becos-ai/locomo-results](https://github.com/becos-ai/locomo-results)
every question’s answer and every judge verdict behind the tables, the
human-check sample with its key and grades, and locomo_eval.py, a
standalone script (Python standard library only) that answers LoCoMo
questions with the whole conversation, no context, or any system’s own
retrieved context, under both prompts of Appendix A, judges answers, and
scores them overall and by category. The release supports three levels
of checking: _score reproduction_—rescoring our released verdicts
reproduces every number in Tables 1–2, including the paired intervals (a
paired command with the same seed), and our answers can be re-judged
with any judge; _baseline rerunning_—the whole-conversation and
no-memory runs can be regenerated end to end; _memory-system
reproduction_—not possible from the release, since Becos’s retrieved
contexts and implementation are not included. Answers and verdicts are
released under CC BY-NC 4.0 (as LoCoMo is), the script under MIT.
LoCoMo’s conversations were generated by LLM agents, so no personal data
is involved. Researchers can request API access to Becos from the
authors. Total API cost of the runs reported here: about \$63 of OpenAI
calls (answers and the two OpenAI judges) and \$10.31 of DeepSeek calls
(the third judge and loading).

## Appendix A Prompts

We use four prompts. Three are Nemori’s evaluation prompts, reproduced
verbatim from the nemori-ai/nemori repository at commit d2a6dff,
directory evaluation/locomo (MIT licence) \[4\]: the short answer prompt
from search.py, and the judge’s system message and judge prompt from
metrics/llm_judge.py. The fourth, the 7-step answer prompt, is ours. It
is Nemori’s answer prompt with instruction 8 (“less than 5-6 words”)
replaced by seven reasoning steps and a FINAL ANSWER line; the step list
follows the description of EverMemOS’s chain-of-thought prompt \[7\] in
the LoCoMo audit \[6\], written in our own words. In every answer prompt
{episodic} receives Becos’s retrieved context or the whole conversation,
and {semantic} is left empty.

#### Short answer prompt (Nemori).

You are an intelligent memory assistant tasked with retrieving accurate
information from conversation memories. \# CONTEXT: You have access to
memories from two speakers in a conversation. These memories contain
timestamped information that may be relevant to answering the question.
\# INSTRUCTIONS: 1. Carefully analyze all provided memories from both
speakers 2. Pay special attention to the timestamps to determine the
answer 3. If the question asks about a specific event or fact, look for
direct evidence in the memories 4. If the memories contain contradictory
information, prioritize the most recent memory 5. If there is a question
about time references (like "last year", "two months ago", etc.),
calculate the actual date based on the memory timestamp. For example, if
a memory from 4 May 2022 mentions "went to India last year," then the
trip occurred in 2021. 6. Always convert relative time references to
specific dates, months, or years. For example, convert "last year" to
"2022" or "two months ago" to "March 2023" based on the memory
timestamp. Ignore the reference while answering the question. 7. Focus
only on the content of the memories from both speakers. Do not confuse
character names mentioned in memories with the actual users who created
those memories. 8. The answer should be less than 5-6 words. \# APPROACH
(Think step by step): 1. First, examine all memories that contain
information related to the question 2. Examine the timestamps and
content of these memories carefully 3. Look for explicit mentions of
dates, times, locations, or events that answer the question 4. If the
answer requires calculation (e.g., converting relative time references),
show your work 5. Formulate a precise, concise answer based solely on
the evidence in the memories 6. Double-check that your answer directly
addresses the question asked 7. Ensure your final answer is specific and
avoids vague time references Episodic Memories: {episodic} Semantic
Memories: {semantic} Question: {question} Answer:

#### Judge prompt (Nemori).

Your task is to label an answer to a question as ’CORRECT’ or ’WRONG’.
You will be given the following data: (1) a question (posed by one user
to another user), (2) a ’gold’ (ground truth) answer, (3) a generated
answer which you will score as CORRECT/WRONG. The point of the question
is to ask about something one user should know about the other user
based on their prior conversations. The gold answer will usually be a
concise and short answer that includes the referenced topic, for
example: Question: Do you remember what I got the last time I went to
Hawaii? Gold answer: A shell necklace The generated answer might be much
longer, but you should be generous with your grading - as long as it
touches on the same topic as the gold answer, it should be counted as
CORRECT. For time related questions, the gold answer will be a specific
date, month, year, etc. The generated answer might be much longer or use
relative time references (like "last Tuesday" or "next month"), but you
should be generous with your grading - as long as it refers to the same
date or time period as the gold answer, it should be counted as CORRECT.
Even if the format differs (e.g., "May 7th" vs "7 May"), consider it
CORRECT if it’s the same date. Now it’s time for the real question:
Question: {question} Gold answer: {gold_answer} Generated answer:
{generated_answer} First, provide a short (one sentence) explanation of
your reasoning, then finish with CORRECT or WRONG. Do NOT include both
CORRECT and WRONG in your response, or it will break the evaluation
script. Just return the label CORRECT or WRONG in a json format with the
key as "label".
